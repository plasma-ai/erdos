## Additive properties of random sequences of positive integers

by

P. ERDŐS and A. RÉNYI (Budapest)

**§ 0. Introduction.** It is well known (see e. g. [1]) that the number of those integers $n \leq x$ which can be represented in the form $n = k^2 + l^2$ ($k$ and $l$ integers) has the order of magnitude $x/\sqrt{\log x}$; as clearly the number of pairs $k, l$ of positive integers such that $k^2 + l^2 \leq x$ is $\sim \pi x/4$, the reason why the set of numbers which can be represented as the sum of two squares has zero density is not that the squares are too rare, but — loosely speaking — that they are “too regularly” distributed, so that among the sums $k^2 + l^2$ there are too many equal ones. This was first demonstrated by Atkin [2], who solved the following problem, proposed by J. E. Littlewood: If each square $k^2$ is replaced by a random integer $v_k$, chosen according to a certain probability law in the neighbourhood of $k^2$, then the sums $v_k+v_l$ almost surely have a positive density.

In § 1 of the present paper we introduce a class of sequences of random integers. This construction has been used already in [3]. In § 2, 3 and 4 we prove some theorems of a similar character to that of Atkin, mentioned above. We shall show that if the random sequences $v_k$ and $\mu_l$ have approximately the same order of magnitude as the sequence $ck^2$ with some $c > 0$, then the sequences $v_k+\mu_l$, $k^2+\mu_l$ and $v_k+v_l$ will have positive density with probability 1; moreover, in all three cases the sequences of numbers $n$ which have exactly $r$ representations in the form $n=v_k+\mu_l$, $n=k^2+\mu_l$ or $n=v_k+v_l$ ($k<l$), will almost surely have a positive density for each value of $r$ ($r=0,1,\dots$) and these densities form a Poisson distribution. In § 5 we shall show that the number $f(n)$ of representations of $n$ in the form $n=v_k+v_l$ has, in case it tends to $+\infty$, a normal distribution in the limit. In § 6 we generalize these results for sums of more than two terms of a random sequence of integers. In § 7 we consider the distribution of differences of a random sequence. § 8 deals with random sequences $v_k$ of the order $k^{2+\epsilon}$, where $\epsilon > 0$ is arbitrarily small, for which the number $f(n)$ of representations of $n$ in the form $n = \nu_k + \nu_l$ is almost surely bounded. This result is connected with a problem of S. Sidon ([4], [5]). § 9 deals with great values of $f(n)$ while § 10 contains the proof of the strong law of large numbers for $f(n)$. Finally in § 11 we give a stochastic analogon of Romanoff’s theorem [6], according to which the sequence of those numbers $n$ which can be represented in the form $n = p + a^k$, where $p$ is prime and $a > 1$ an integer, has positive density, while § 12 contains a similar but more general theorem.

Some of the results proved in detail in the present paper have been announced without proof in a previous paper [3] of the first-named author.

Throughout the paper we use the following notation: $P(\dots)$ denotes the probability of the event in the brackets. We denote random events by capital letters; $\bar{A}$ denotes the event contrary to $A$; if $A$ and $B$ are events, let $A+B$ denote the event consisting in the occurrence of at least one of the events $A$ and $B$, and let $AB$ denote the event consisting in the joint occurrence of the events $A$ and $B$. We denote random variables by Greek letters $\xi$, $\eta$, $\nu$, $\mu$ etc. $M(\xi)$ denotes the mean value and $D^2(\xi)$ the variance of the random variable $\xi$. $M(\xi|A)$ denotes the conditional mean value of $\xi$ under condition $A$. $C_1$, $C_2$, $\dots$ denote positive constants.

**§ 1. Random sequences of integers.** The notations introduced in this section will be used throughout the paper.

We define the random sequences of positive integers dealt with in the present paper as follows: Let $\xi_n$ ($n = 1, 2, \dots$) be a sequence of completely independent random variables such that $\xi_n$ takes on the values 1 and 0 with the corresponding probabilities $p_n$ and $(1-p_n)$, i. e. we suppose that

$$(1.1) \quad P(\xi_n = 1) = p_n,\quad P(\xi_n = 0) = 1-p_n,$$

where $p_n$ is an arbitrary sequence of numbers such that $0 \leq p_n \leq 1$ and

$$(1.2) \quad \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} p_n = +\infty.$$

We denote by $\nu_1$, $\nu_2$, $\dots$, $\nu_k$, $\dots$ the values of $n$ (in increasing order of magnitude) such that $\xi_n = 1$. Thus $\nu_1 < \nu_2 < \dots < \nu_k < \dots$, $\xi_{\nu_k} = 1$ ($k = 1, 2, \dots$) and $\xi_n = 0$ if $\nu_k < n < \nu_{k+1}$. We call the sequence $\{\nu_k\}$ a random sequence of positive integers generated by the sequence $\{p_n\}$ of probabilities. $p_n$ is clearly the probability that the number $n$ should be contained in the sequence $\{\nu_k\}$. This method of generating random sequences of integers has already been used in [3]. Evidently ordinary sequences of integers are special cases of random sequences of the above type, which we obtain if each $p_n$ is 1 or 0. Clearly we have

$$(1.3)\quad \xi_1+\xi_2+\ldots+\xi_{\nu_k}=k \quad (k=1,2,\ldots)$$

and $\nu_k$ is the least integer for which (1.3) holds for a given value of $k$.

It follows by the Lemma of Borel-Cantelli (see [9]) from (1.2) that the sequence $\{\nu_k\}$ is infinite with probability 1. It follows further from a variant of the strong law of large numbers (see [10], p. 438) that putting

$$(1.4)\quad P(n)=\sum_{k=1}^n p_k$$

we have with probability 1

$$(1.5)\quad \lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\xi_1+\xi_2+\ldots+\xi_n}{P(n)}=1.$$

Thus by (1.3) and (1.5) we have with probability 1

$$(1.6)\quad \lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{k}{P(\nu_k)}=1.$$

For instance, if $p_n=c/\sqrt{n}$ ($c>0$), we have $P(n)\sim 2c\sqrt{n}$ and thus with probability 1

$$(1.7)\quad \lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{\nu_k}{k^2}=\frac{1}{4c^2}$$

while if $p_n=c/n$ ($c>0$) we have $P(n)\sim c\log n$ and thus

$$(1.8)\quad \lim_{k\to+\infty}\sqrt[k]{\nu_k}=e^{1/c}.$$

Still more can be said about the sequence $\{\nu_k\}$. As a matter of fact, by the central limit theorem ([8], p. 130-131), putting $V(n)=\sum_{k=1}^n p_k^2$ we have

$$(1.9)\quad \lim_{n\to+\infty}P\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^n \xi_k-P(n)}{\sqrt{P(n)-V(n)}}<x\right)=\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^x e^{-u^2/2}\,du.$$

It follows that in the case $p_n=c/\sqrt{n}$ ($c>0$) for instance we have

$$(1.10)\quad \lim_{n\to+\infty}P\left(\frac{\nu_k-k^2/4c^2}{k^{3/2}/2c^2}<x\right)=\Phi(x),$$

i. e. the fluctuations of $\nu_k$ around $k^2/4c^2$ are of the order $k^{3/2}$ and are approximately normally distributed if $k$ is large.

It may be added that if we change only slightly the probabilities $p_n$, all assertions which were true with probability 1 remain true with the same probability. In fact, according to a theorem of Kakutani [12], this is true if we replace $p_n$ by $p_n'$ provided that

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(p_n-p_n')^2}{p_n(1-p_n)} < +\infty.
$$

Thus in the case of $p_n=c/\sqrt{n}$, we may replace $p_n$ by $p_n'=c/\sqrt{n}+d/n^a$ with $a>\frac{3}{4}$. We shall see, however, that for our problems still larger changes of the probabilities are admissible if those changes do not affect certain averages.

§ 2. Two independent random sequences. Let $\{v_k\}$ and $\{\mu_k\}$ be independent random sequences of positive integers, generated by the sequences $\{p_n\}$ and $\{q_n\}$ of probabilities $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_n=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_n=+\infty$, i. e. $v_k$ and $\mu_k$ respectively are the $k$-th values of $n$ for which $\xi_n=1$ and respectively $\eta_n=1$, where the random variables $\xi_1,\eta_1,\ldots,\xi_n,\eta_n,\ldots$ taking on only the values 0 and 1 are completely independent, $P(\xi_n=1)=p_n$ and $P(\eta_n=1)=q_n$. Let $f(n)$ denote the number of representations of $n$ in the form $n=v_k+\mu_l$. First we prove

THEOREM 1. If

$$
\tag{2.1}
\lim_{n\to+\infty}p_n=\lim_{n\to+\infty}q_n=0
$$

and (1)

$$
\tag{2.2}
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}p_kq_{n-k}=\lambda>0,
$$

then $f(n)$ has in the limit for $n\to+\infty$ a Poisson distribution with mean value $\lambda$, i. e.

$$
\tag{2.3}
\lim_{n\to+\infty}P(f(n)=r)=\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}\quad(r=0,1,\ldots).
$$

Proof. As the random variables $\xi_k\eta_{n-k}$ ($k=1,2,\ldots,n-1$) are by supposition independent and

$$
\tag{2.4}
f(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\xi_k\eta_{n-k}
$$

(1) Clearly (2.1) and (2.2) imply $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_k=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_k=+\infty$.

Theorem 1 follows from a well-known general theorem of probability theory (see [8], p. 132-133).

Let $S_r$ denote the sequence of those integers $m$ for which $f(m)=r$; Theorem 1 suggests that $S_r$ will have (with probability 1) a density equal to $\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}/r!$ ($r=0,1,\ldots$). This is in fact true under general conditions, contained in the following

**THEOREM 2.** *If the independent random sequences $v_k$ and $\mu_k$ are generated by the sequences of probabilities $p_n$ and $q_n$ where $p_n$ and $q_n$ are decreasing and tend to zero, if further (2.2) is satisfied and there exists a constant $\delta$ with $0<\delta<1$ such that*

$$
(2.5)\qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n}p_k=O(n^{1-\delta})\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_k=O(n^{1-\delta}),
$$

*then, denoting by $S_r$ the set of those positive integers $n$ which have exactly $r$ representations of the form $n=v_k+\mu_l$, we find that $S_r$ has with probability 1 the density $\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}/r!$ ($r=0,1,\ldots$).*

**COROLLARY.** *Under the conditions of Theorem 2 the sequence of all those integers which can be represented in the form $n=v_k+\mu_l$ has with probability 1 the density $1-e^{-\lambda}$.*

**Proof of Theorem 2.** The validity of Theorem 2 is a consequence of the fact that the random variables $f(n)$ ($n=1,2,\ldots$) are in a certain sense almost independent, as will be seen from the proof given below.

Let $\varepsilon_r(n)$ be equal to 1 if $f(n)=r$ and 0 otherwise. Then we have

$$
(2.6)\qquad M(\varepsilon_r(n))=\sum p_{k_1}q_{n-k_1}\cdots p_{k_r}q_{n-k_r}\prod_{j\ne k_h\,(h=1,2,\ldots,r)}(1-p_jq_{n-j}),
$$

where the summation is extended over all $r$-tuples of different integers $(k_1,\ldots,k_r)$ such that $1\leq k_1<k_2<\cdots<k_r\leq n-1$.

We shall need here and in what follows the following inequalities, valid for any sequence $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_N$ of positive numbers:

$$
(2.7)\qquad
\frac{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_k\right)^r}{r!}
\left(1-\frac{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_k^2\right)\binom{r}{2}}{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_k\right)^2}\right)
\leq
\sum_{1\leq k_1<k_2<\cdots<k_r\leq N}a_{k_1}a_{k_2}\cdots a_{k_r}
\leq
\frac{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_k\right)^r}{r!}.
$$

The upper inequality in (2.7) is trivial; the lower inequality, which has already been used in a previous paper ([7], p. 27) of the authors, also easily follows from the polynomial theorem.

It follows from (2.6) by using (2.7), with respect to (2.2) that

$$
(2.8)\qquad \lim_{n\to+\infty}M(\varepsilon_r(n))=\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}\quad(r=0,1,\ldots).
$$

Now we consider $M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m))$ for $n<m$. Let $A_k$ denote the event $\xi_k=\eta_{n-k}=\eta_{m-k}=1$ and $B_k$ the event $\eta_k=\xi_{n-k}=\xi_{m-k}=1$; further put, $A=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}A_k$ and $B=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}B_k$; then we evidently have

$$
\begin{aligned}
&M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m)\mid\bar A\bar B)P(\bar A\bar B)\\
&=\sum' p_{k_1}q_{n-k_1}\cdots p_{k_r}q_{n-k_r}p_{l_1}q_{m-l_1}\cdots p_{l_r}q_{m-l_r}\times\\
&\qquad\times\prod_{k\ne k_i,\ l\ne l_i\ (i=1,2,\ldots,r)}(1-p_kq_{n-k})(1-p_lq_{m-l}),
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\sum'$ denotes that the summation is extended over those pairs of $r$-tuples $1\leq k_1<k_2<\cdots<k_r<n$ and $1\leq l_1<l_2<\cdots<l_r<m$ which are disjoint $(k_i\ne l_j)$ and such that the $r$-tuples $(n-k_1,\ldots,n-k_r)$ and $(m-l_1,\ldots,m-l_r)$ are also disjoint. Thus

$$
(2.9)\quad M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m)\mid\bar A\bar B)P(\bar A\bar B)\leq M(\varepsilon_r(n))M(\varepsilon_r(m)).
$$

Now clearly $\bar A\cdot\bar B=\overline{A+B}$ and thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2.10)\quad &M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m))\\
&=M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m)\mid A+B)P(A+B)+M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m)\mid\bar A\cdot\bar B)P(\bar A\bar B)
\end{aligned}
$$

and as $\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m)\leq1$, we have

$$
(2.11)\quad M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m)\mid A+B)P(A+B)\leq P(A+B)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(P(A_k)+P(B_k)).
$$

Thus from (2.9), (2.10) and (2.11) it follows that

$$
(2.12)\quad M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m))-M(\varepsilon_r(n))M(\varepsilon_r(m))\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(p_kq_{n-k}q_{m-k}+q_kp_{n-k}p_{m-k})
$$

and thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2.13)\quad &M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m))-M(\varepsilon_r(n))M(\varepsilon_r(m))\\
&\leq(q_{m-n}+p_{m-n})\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}p_kq_{n-k}\leq C_1(q_{m-n}+p_{m-n}).
\end{aligned}
$$

Let us denote by $\zeta_r(N)$ the number of those values of $n\leq N$ for which $f(n)=r$, i. e. put

$$
(2.14)\quad \zeta_r(N)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\varepsilon_r(n).
$$

It follows from $(2.8)$ that

$$
(2.15)\quad \lim_{N\to+\infty} M(\zeta_r(N))=\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!};
$$

further, by $(2.13)$ and by our supposition $(2.5)$ we have

$$
(2.16)\quad D^2(\zeta_r(N))\leq C_2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(p_k+q_k)\frac{(N-k)}{N^2}=O\left(\frac{1}{N^\delta}\right).
$$

Thus by the inequality of Chebyshev (see [9]) we obtain for $\varepsilon>0$

$$
(2.17)\quad P\left(\left|\zeta_r(N)-M(\zeta_r(N))\right|>\varepsilon\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{N^\delta}\right).
$$

Thus the series

$$
(2.18)\quad \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}P\left(\left|\zeta_r(N_j)-M(\zeta_r(N_j))\right|>\varepsilon\right)
$$

is convergent if $N_j=[j^{2/\delta}]$ for any $\varepsilon>0$ and thus by the Lemma of Borel-Cantelli and by $(2.15)$ we have with probability 1

$$
(2.19)\quad \lim_{j\to+\infty}\zeta_r(N_j)=\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}\quad (r=0,1,\ldots).
$$

Since evidently for $N_j<N<N_{j+1}$

$$
(2.20)\quad \zeta_r(N_j)\frac{N_j}{N_{j+1}}\leq\zeta_r(N)\leq\zeta_r(N_{j+1})\frac{N_{j+1}}{N_j}
$$

and clearly $\lim_{j\to+\infty}\frac{N_{j+1}}{N_j}=1$, it follows from $(2.19)$ that with probability 1

$$
(2.21)\quad \lim_{N\to+\infty}\zeta_r(N)=\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}\quad (r=0,1,\ldots).
$$

Thus Theorem 2 is proved. The Corollary is an evident consequence of the assertion of Theorem 2 for $r=0$, since the sequence of those numbers $n$ which have at least one representation in the form $n=v_k+\mu_l$ is the complementary set of the sequence $S_0$.

Clearly if $p_n=a/\sqrt{n}$ and $q_n=b/\sqrt{n}$ with $a>0$, $b>0$ then

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}p_kq_{n-k}=ab\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(n-k)}}=ab\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=ab\pi+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
$$

and finally

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} p_k = O(n^{1/2}), \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} q_k = O(n^{1/2});$$

thus the conditions of Theorem 2 are fulfilled with $\lambda = ab\pi$ and $\delta = \frac{1}{2}$.

**§ 3. A square and a random integer.** In this section we consider the sums $k^2+\mu_l$ where $\{\mu_l\}$ is a random sequence generated by the sequence of probabilities $\{q_n\}$. For the sake of simplicity we restrict ourselves to the special case $q_n = c/\sqrt{n}$ ($c>0$), though our result could be proved also under more general suppositions.

**THEOREM 3.** *If $P(\eta_n = 1) = q_n = c/\sqrt{n}$ with $c>0$ and $f(n)$ denotes the number of representations of $n$ in the form $n=k^2+\mu_l$, then, if we denote by $S_r$ the sequence of integers $n$ for which $f(n)=r$, $S_r$ has with probability 1 the density $\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}/r!$ where $\lambda=c\pi/2$ ($r=0,1,\ldots$).*

**Proof.** The proof follows the same pattern as that of Theorem 2. Let $\varepsilon_r(n)$ be equal to 1 if $f(n)=r$ and 0 otherwise. Then we have

$$(3.1)\quad M(\varepsilon_r(n)) = \sum \frac{c^r}{\sqrt{(n-j_1^2)\dots(n-j_r^2)}} \prod_{j_i\ne j_h\ (h=1,2,\dots,r)} \left(1-\frac{c}{\sqrt{n-j_i^2}}\right),$$

where the summation is extended over all $r$-tuples of different integers $j_1,j_2,\dots,j_r$ with $j_h^2<n$. Now clearly if $n=N^2+h$ ($h=1,2,\dots,2N+1$), then

$$(3.2)\quad \sum_{1\leq j^2<n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{n-j^2}} = \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right);$$

therefore

$$(3.3)\quad M(\varepsilon_r(n)) = \frac{\left(\frac{\pi c}{2}+\frac{c}{\sqrt{h}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)^r}{r!}\exp\left\{-\left(\frac{\pi c}{2}+\frac{c}{\sqrt{h}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)\right\},$$

and thus

$$(3.4)\quad \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N M(\varepsilon_r(n)) = \frac{(\pi c/2)^r e^{-\pi c/2}}{r!}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{N}}\right)\right).$$

Now let us find for $n<m$ the mean value of $\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m)$. Let $H_N$ denote the set of integers $N-1,N-4,\dots,N-h^2,\dots,N-[\sqrt{N}]^2$. Then the intersection of the sets $H_n$ and $H_m$, which we denote by $H_nH_m$, does not contain more than $d(m-n)$ elements, where $d(N)$ denotes the number of divisors of $N$. As a matter of fact, each $l\in H_n H_m$ is of the form $l=n-j^2=m-h^2$, i. e. corresponds to a representation of $m-n$ in the form $m-n=h^2-j^2=(h-j)(h+j)$. Now every decomposition of $m-n$ into the product of two of its divisors of the same parity $m-n=d_1d_2$ corresponds to exactly one number $l\in H_n H_m$, namely to $l=n-j^2=m-h^2$ with $h=(d_1+d_2)/2$, $j=(d_1-d_2)/2$, and thus our assertion is proved.

Now let $A_{nm}$ denote the event that $\xi_l=0$ for all $l\in H_{nm}$. Then we have

$$
(3.5)\quad P(A_{nm})=\prod_{l\in H_{nm}}(1-p_l)=\prod_{h=(d_1+d_2)/2,\,d_1d_2=m-n}\left(1-\frac{c}{\sqrt{m-h^2}}\right).
$$

We distinguish two cases. Let $E$ denote the class of integers $N$ for which $N$ is contained in an interval $s^2-\sqrt[3]{s},s^2+\sqrt[3]{s}$ ($s=2,3,\ldots$). If $N$ is not in $E$, we have by (3.5)

$$
(3.6)\quad P(A_{nm})=1-O\left(\frac{d(m-n)}{\sqrt[12]{m}}\right)=1-O\left(\frac{1}{m^{1/13}}\right)
$$

if $m\geq m_0$, in view of $d(N)=O(N^\varepsilon)$ for every $\varepsilon>0$ (see [15], p. 260). Evidently under the condition $A_{nm}$ the random variables $\varepsilon_r(n)$ and $\varepsilon_r(m)$ are independent; thus if $m$ is not in $E$, we have

$$
M(\varepsilon_r(m)|A_{nm})=M(\varepsilon_r(m))+O\left(\frac{1}{m^{1/13}}\right),
$$

$$
M(\varepsilon_r(n)|A_{nm})=M(\varepsilon_r(n))+O\left(\frac{1}{m^{1/13}}\right)
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
M(\varepsilon_r(m)\varepsilon_r(n))&=M(\varepsilon_r(m)\varepsilon_r(n)|A_{nm})+O\left(\frac{1}{m^{1/13}}\right)\\
&=M(\varepsilon_r(m))M(\varepsilon_r(n))+O\left(\frac{1}{m^{1/13}}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

It follows that

$$
\begin{aligned}
(3.7)\quad &\sum_{n=1}^N\sum_{m=1}^N\left[M(\varepsilon_r(m)\varepsilon_r(n))-M(\varepsilon_r(m))M(\varepsilon_r(n))\right]\\
&=O(N^{2-1/13})+O\left(N\sum_{m\in E,m\leq N}1\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
\sum_{m\in E,\,m\leq N} 1 = O(N^{5/6}), \tag{3.8}
$$

it follows that

$$
\sum_{n=1}^N \sum_{m=1}^N \left[M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m))-M(\varepsilon_r(n))M(\varepsilon_r(m))\right] = O(N^{25/13}). \tag{3.9}
$$

Thus putting

$$
\zeta_r(N)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N\varepsilon_r(n), \tag{3.10}
$$

we have

$$
M(\zeta_r(N))=\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{N}}\right) \tag{3.11}
$$

and

$$
D^2(\zeta_r(N))=O\left(\frac{1}{N^{1/13}}\right). \tag{3.12}
$$

By the inequality of Chebyshev we obtain for any $\varepsilon>0$

$$
P\left(\left|\zeta_r(N)-\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}\right|>\varepsilon\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{N^{1/13}}\right). \tag{3.13}
$$

Thus the series

$$
\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}P\left(\left|\zeta_r(N^{14})-\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}\right|>\varepsilon\right) \tag{3.14}
$$

is convergent.

Since this holds for any $\varepsilon>0$, it follows by the Borel-Cantelli lemma that with probability $1$

$$
\lim_{N\to+\infty}\zeta_r(N^{14})=\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}\quad(r=0,1,\ldots). \tag{3.15}
$$

Since

$$
\lim_{N\to+\infty}\left(\frac{N+1}{N}\right)^{14}=1,
$$

it follows, as in the proof of Theorem 2, that with probability $1$

$$
\lim_{N\to+\infty}\zeta_r(N)=\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}\quad(r=0,1,\ldots), \tag{3.16}
$$

which proves Theorem 3.

**§ 4. Sum of two terms of the same random sequence.** In this section we prove

THEOREM 4. Let $v_k$ be a random sequence of positive integers generated by the sequence of probabilities $p_n$ such that $p_n$ is decreasing and tends to $0$; further, let

$$
\tag{4.1}
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\sum_{k<n/2}p_kp_{n-k}=\lambda
$$

and for some $\delta$ with $0<\delta<1$

$$
\tag{4.2}
\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_k=O(n^{1-\delta}).
$$

Let $f(n)$ denote the number of representations of $n$ in the form $n=v_k+v_l$ with $v_k<v_l$, and denote by $S_r$ the sequence of those integers $n$ for which $f(n)=r$. Then $S_r$ has with probability 1 the density $\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}/r!$ for $r=0,1,\dots$. Especially the set of those integers $n$ which have at least one representation in the form $n=v_k+v_l$ with $k<l$ has the density $1-e^{-\lambda}$.

The proof of Theorem 4 follows exactly the same pattern as that of Theorem 2 and thus may be left to the reader. Clearly if $p_n=a/\sqrt{n}$ with $a>0$ then the conditions of Theorem 4 are fulfilled with $\lambda=\pi a^2/2$ and $\delta=\frac12$, since

$$
\sum_{k<n/2}p_kp_{n-k}
=a^2\int_0^{1/2}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
=\frac{a^2\pi}{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right);
$$

further

$$
\sum_{k<n/2}p_k=O(\sqrt{n}).
$$

Let us mention that if we define $f^*(n)$ as the number of all representations of $n$ in the form $n=v_k+v_l$ without the restriction $v_k<v_l$, then clearly $f^*(n)=2f(n)$ if $n$ is odd and $f^*(n)=2f(n)+\xi_{n/2}$ if $n$ is even.

Clearly the sequence of those even numbers $2k$ for which $\xi_k=1$ has the density $0$, as a matter of fact, if $N\eta_N$ denotes the number of such integers $2k\leq 2N$, we have

$$
\eta_N=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\xi_k
$$

and thus

$$
M(\eta_N)=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right).
$$

This implies

$$
\sum M(\eta_{N^4}) < +\infty;
$$

therefore with probability 1

$$
\lim_{N\to+\infty}\eta_{N^4}=0,
$$

and thus also

$$
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\eta_n=0.
$$

Thus it follows from Theorem 4 that if $S_r^*$ denotes the sequence of those integers $n$ for which $f^*(n)=r$, then $S_{2r}^*$ has with probability 1 the density $\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}/r!$ while $S_{2r+1}^*$ has the density 0 $(r=0,1,\ldots)$.

**§ 5. On random sequences for which $f(n)\to+\infty$.** Let us choose a sequence $\{p_n\}$ of probabilities so that putting

$$
\tag{5.1}
A_1(n)=\sum_{k<n/2}p_kp_{n-k}
$$

and

$$
\tag{5.2}
A_2(n)=\sum_{k<n/2}p_k^2p_{n-k}^2
$$

we shall have

$$
\tag{5.3}
\lim_{n\to+\infty}(A_1(n)-A_2(n))=+\infty.
$$

Let $v_k$ denote the random sequence of integers generated by the sequence $p_n$ in the sense of § 1. In that case the number of representations of $n$ in the form $n=v_k+v_l$ with $k<l$ is in the limit for $n\to+\infty$ normally distributed. This is expressed by the following

**THEOREM 5.** Let $\{p_n\}$ be a sequence of probabilities such that if $A_1(n)$ and $A_2(n)$ are defined by (5.1) and (5.2) respectively, then we have (5.3). Then, denoting by $f(n)$ the number of representations of $n$ in the form $n=v_k+v_l$ with $k<l$, we have for $-\infty<x<+\infty$

$$
\tag{5.4}
\lim_{n\to+\infty}P\left(\frac{f(n)-A_1(n)}{\sqrt{A_1(n)-A_2(n)}}<x\right)=\Phi(x),
$$

where $\Phi(x)$ denotes the standard form of the normal distribution function, i. e.

$$
\tag{5.5}
\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-u^2/2}\,du.
$$

Proof. $f(n) = \sum_{k<n/2} \xi_k \xi_{n-k}$, where the random variables $\xi_k$ are independent and $P(\xi_k = 1) = p_k$, $P(\xi_k = 0) = 1-p_k$ $(k = 1, 2, \ldots)$. It follows that

$$M(f(n)) = A_1(n) \quad\text{and}\quad D^2(f(n)) = A_1(n) - A_2(n).$$

Since the conditions of the central limit theorem of probability theory (e. g. Lindeberg's conditions, see [8]) are fulfilled, (5.4) follows immediately.

The conditions of Theorem 5 are clearly fulfilled, e. g. if $p_n = \omega(n)/\sqrt{n}$ where $\omega(n) \to +\infty$ and $\omega(n)/\sqrt{n} \leq q < 1$.

A similar result holds for the number of representations of $n$ in the form $n = k^2 + \mu_l$ where $\{\mu_l\}$ is a random sequence of integers generated by a sequence of probabilities $q_n$ such that putting $A_1(n) = \sum_{k^2<n} q_{n-k^2}$ and $A_2(n) = \sum_{k^2<n} q_{n-k^2}^2$ we have $A_1(n) - A_2(n) \to +\infty$; thus for instance if $q_n = \omega(n)/\sqrt{n}$ with $\omega(n) \to +\infty$ and $\omega(n)/\sqrt{n} \leq q < 1$. In this case if we denote by $f(n)$ the number of representations of the number $n$ in the form $n = k^2 + v_l$, (5.4) is valid.

**§ 6. Sum of more than two terms of a random sequence.** The results obtained in the preceding sections can be generalized to sums of more than two terms. As an example we prove the following

**THEOREM 6.** Let $v_k$ be a random sequence of integers generated by the sequence of probabilities $p_n = c/n^{1-1/s}$ where $s \geq 3$ is a positive integer and $c > 0$. Let $f(n)$ denote the number of representations of the number $n$ in the form $n = v_{k_1} + v_{k_2} + \dots + v_{k_s}$ with $k_1 < k_2 < \dots < k_s$. Let $S_r$ denote the sequence of those integers $n$ for which $f(n) = r$. Then $S_r$ has with probability 1 the density $\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}/r!$ where $\lambda = \frac{c^s \Gamma(1/s)^s}{s!}$ $(r = 0, 1, \dots)$.

Proof. Let us put $\epsilon_r(n) = 1$ if $f(n) = r$ and $= 0$ otherwise. Now we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
(6.1)\quad &\lim_{n\to+\infty}
\sum_{\substack{k_1<k_2<\dots<k_s\\k_1+k_2+\dots+k_s=n}}
p_{k_1}p_{k_2}\dots p_{k_s}\\
&= \frac{c^s}{s!}
\int\!\!\int\cdots\!\int_{\substack{0\leq x_i<1\ (i=1,2,\dots,s-1)\\x_1+x_2+\dots+x_{s-1}<1}}
\frac{dx_1}{x_1^{1-1/s}}\cdot
\frac{dx_2}{x_2^{1-1/s}}\dots
\frac{dx_{s-2}}{x_{s-2}^{1-1/s}}\cdot
\frac{dx_{s-1}}{x_{s-1}^{1-1/s}(1-x_1-\dots-x_{s-1})^{1-1/s}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

and the integral on the right of (6.1) is equal to $\Gamma(1/s)^s$. Thus putting

$$(6.2)\quad \lambda = \frac{(c\Gamma(1/s))^s}{s!},$$

we have

$$(6.3)\qquad \lim_{n\to+\infty} M(\varepsilon_r(n))=\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}\qquad (r=0,1,\ldots).$$

The estimation of $M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m))$ can be obtained as before, and Theorem 6 is proved in the same way as Theorems 2-4.

**§ 7. Differences formed from a random sequence.** Let $\{v_k\}$ be a random sequence generated by the sequence of probabilities $\{p_n\}$. Let $g(n)$ denote the number of representations of $n$ in the form $n=v_k-v_l$. Clearly

$$(7.1)\qquad M(g(n))=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_kp_{n+k}.$$

Two cases are possible: either $M(g(n))$ is infinite, which is of minor interest, or it is finite for every $n$. The latter is the case if for instance $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_k^2<+\infty$. In that case, however, we have $\lim_{n\to+\infty}M(g(n))=0$. Hence it is clear that here we cannot hope to obtain similar results to those we obtained for the sums $v_k+v_l$. More reasonable results can be obtained if we consider only representations $n=v_k-v_l$ for which $v_k\leq(1+B)n$ where $B$ is a positive constant not depending on $n$. If $g_B(n)$ denotes the number of such representations of $n$, we have, putting $p_k=c/\sqrt{k}$ ($c>0$),

$$(7.2)\qquad \lim_{n\to+\infty}M(g_B(n))=c^2\int_0^B\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x(1+x)}}=c^2\log(B+1+\sqrt{B^2+2B}).$$

It can be shown by the same method as that used in proving Theorem 2 that the following theorem holds:

**THEOREM 7.** Let the random sequence $v_k$ be generated by the sequence of probabilities $p_n=c/\sqrt{n}$ ($c>0$); let $g_B(n)$ denote the number of representations of $n$ of the form $n=v_k-v_l$ where $v_k\leq(1+B)n$ ($B>0$); then the sequence of those integers $n$ for which $g_B(n)=r$ has the density $\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}/r!$, where $\lambda=c^2\log(B+1+\sqrt{B^2+2B})$. Especially for $B=(e-1)^2/2e$ we have $\lambda=c^2$.

**§ 8. On random sequences for which $f(n)$ is bounded.** S. Sidon ([7], [12]) considered such sequences $\{a_k\}$ of positive integers that the sequence of coefficients of the series $\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x^{a_k}\right)^l$ is bounded. He called such sequences $B_l$-sequences ($l=2,3,\ldots$). We shall call a sequence $\{a_k\}$ a $B_l(K)$ sequence if the coefficients of $\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x^{a_k}\right)^l$ are all $\leq K$. The interesting question is how dense a $B_l(K)$ sequence can be. It has been shown by Erdős [3] that there exists a $B_2(1)$ sequence $\{a_k\}$ such that $a_k < ck^3$, while Erdős and Turán [11] (for a detailed proof see [13]) have proved that for every $B_2(1)$ sequence one has $\limsup_{k\to+\infty} a_k/k^2 = +\infty$.

We shall prove in this section that if $\{v_k\}$ is a random sequence generated by the sequence of probabilities $p_n = c/n^{1/2+\epsilon}$ ($c>0,\epsilon>0$) then with probability 1 $v_k$ is a $B_2$ sequence.

Thus we shall prove the following

**THEOREM 8.** Let $v_k$ be a random sequence of positive integers generated by the sequence of probabilities $p_n = c/n^{1/2+\epsilon}$ where $c>0$ and $0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}$. Let $f(n)$ denote the number of representations of $n$ in the form $n=v_k+v_l$ with $k\leq l$; then with probability 1 $f(n)$ is bounded; moreover with probability 1 $f(n)\leq [1/2\epsilon]$ except perhaps for a finite number of values of $n$.

**Proof.** Let $\xi_n$ have the same meaning as in § 1. Then $f(n)=\sum_{k\leq n/2}\xi_k\xi_{n-k}$ and thus, taking into account that

$$
\lim_{n\to+\infty}n^{2\epsilon}\left(\sum_{k\leq n/2}p_kp_{n-k}\right)
=c^2\int_0^{1/2}\frac{dx}{[x(1-x)]^{1/2+\epsilon}}
=\frac{1}{2}c^2I(\epsilon),
$$

we have

$$(8.1)\quad M(e^{tf(n)})=\prod_{k\leq n/2}\left(1+\frac{(e^t-1)c^2}{(k(n-k))^{1/2+\epsilon}}\right)\leq\exp\frac{c^2(e^t-1)I(\epsilon)}{n^{2\epsilon}}$$

for $n\geq n_0$ where

$$I(\epsilon)=B\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)^2}{\Gamma(1-2\epsilon)}.$$

Here and in what follows $B(\alpha,\beta)$ denotes the beta-function of Euler

$$(8.2)\quad B(\alpha,\beta)=\int_0^1x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}\,dx=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}\quad\text{for }\alpha>0,\ \beta>0.$$

We infer, choosing $t=\log(1+n^{2\epsilon})$, that

$$(8.3)\quad P(f(n)\geq K+1)\leq\frac{e c^2I(\epsilon)}{n^{2\epsilon(K+1)}}.$$

Thus if $K+1>1/2\epsilon$ the series

$$(8.4)\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(f(n)\geq K+1)$$

is convergent. It follows from the Lemma of Borel-Cantelli that with probability $1$ $f(n) \leq K$ except for a finite number of values of $n$; we may choose $K=[1/2\epsilon]$. Thus Theorem 8 is proved.

Note that from (1.6) we have $v_k \sim (k/c)^{2/(1-2\epsilon)}$. Thus for any $\delta>0$ there exist sequences $\{a_k\}$ such that $a_k=O(k^{2+\delta})$ and the number $f(n)$ of representations of $n$ in the form $n=a_j+a_k$ is bounded. As a matter of fact, if we choose $p_k=1/(k^{1/2+\epsilon})$ with $\epsilon=\delta/(4+2\delta)$, almost all realizations of the corresponding random sequence $\{v_k\}$ may be taken for $\{a_k\}$. Of course (as is usual if a number-theoretical statement of existence is proved by probabilistic methods) our methods do not give an effective construction.

By the same method one can prove the existence of $B_l$-sequences $\{a_k\}$ such that $a_k=O(k^{l+\delta})$ for any $\delta>0$ ($l=3,4,\ldots$).

### § 9. A theorem of the „iterated logarithm” type.

**THEOREM 9.** *If the random sequence $\{v_k\}$ is generated by the sequence of probabilities $p_n=c/\sqrt{n}$ ($c>0$) and $f(n)$ denotes the number of solutions of $n=v_k+v_l$ ($k<l$), we have with probability 1*

$$(9.1)\quad \limsup_{n\to+\infty}\frac{f(n)\log\log n}{\log n}=1.$$

**Proof.** First we prove that with probability 1

$$(9.2)\quad \limsup_{n\to+\infty}\frac{f(n)\log\log n}{\log n}\leq 1.$$

Clearly for any $\epsilon>0$ if $n>n_0$ and $R=\left[(1+2\epsilon)\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\right]$

$$P(f(n)\geq R)\leq\sum p_{k_1}p_{n-k_1}\cdots p_{k_R}p_{n-k_R}\leq\frac{\left(\sum_{k<n/2}c^2/\sqrt{k(n-k)}\right)}{R!},$$

and thus

$$(9.3)\quad P\left(f(n)>(1+2\epsilon)\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{1+\epsilon}}\right).$$

It follows that the series $\sum P\left(f(n)>(1+2\epsilon)\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\right)$ is convergent for any $\epsilon>0$, and thus by the Borel-Cantelli Lemma it follows that (9.2) holds.

Now we prove that with probability 1

$$(9.4)\quad \limsup_{n\to+\infty}\frac{f(n)\log\log n}{\log n}\geq 1.$$

To prove (9.4) we need a lower estimate for $P(f(n) \geq R)$; we have by (2.7) for $R = \left[(1-2\epsilon)\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\right]+1$ and $n \geq n_0(\epsilon)$

$$
P(f(n) \geq R) \geq \frac{1}{n^{1-\epsilon}},\tag{9.5}
$$

which implies that the series $\sum P\left(f(n) \geq (1-2\epsilon)\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\right)$ is diver-  
gent.

Now let $A_n$ denote the event $f(n) \geq (1-2\epsilon)\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}$; the events $A_n$ (while not independent) are almost pairwise independent in the follow-  
ing sense:

$$
\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N}P(A_nA_m)}{\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}P(A_n)\right)^2}=1.\tag{9.6}
$$

As we have shown in a previous paper [16], this is sufficient for the val-  
idity of the Borel-Cantelli lemma and thus (9.6) and (9.5) imply (9.4). That (9.6) holds can be shown by the same method as that applied in the proof of Theorem 2 in estimating $M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m))-M(\varepsilon_r(n))M(\varepsilon_r(m))$.

**§ 10. The law of large numbers for $f(n)$.** In this section we prove

**THEOREM 10.** *If the random sequence $\{v_k\}$ is generated by the sequence of probabilities $p_n = c/\sqrt{n}$ ($c > 0$) and $f(n)$ denotes the number of solutions of $n = v_k+v_l$ with $k < l$ then with probability 1*

$$
\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(n)=\lambda,\tag{10.1}
$$

*where $\lambda=c^2\pi/2$.*

**Proof.** Putting

$$
\zeta_N=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(n)
$$

and in view of

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{N}1/\sqrt{k}=2\sqrt{n}+O(1),
$$

we evidently have

$$
M(\zeta_N)=c^2\int_0^1\sqrt{\frac{1-x}{x}}\,dx+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right).
$$

Since

$$
\int_0^1 \sqrt{\frac{1-x}{x}}\,dx = B\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2},
$$

where $B(\alpha,\beta)$ is Euler's beta function (see (8.3)), it follows that

$$(10.2) \quad M(\zeta_N) = \frac{\pi c^2}{2} + o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right).$$

On the other hand, we obtain by some easy calculations

$$(10.3) \quad D^2(\zeta_N) = \frac{10c^3}{3\sqrt{N}} + o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).$$

Thus by the inequality of Chebyshev

$$
P(|\zeta_N-\lambda|>\varepsilon) = O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right).
$$

It follows that the series

$$
\sum_{N=1}^{\infty} P(|\zeta_{N^3}-\lambda|>\varepsilon)
$$

is convergent for every $\varepsilon>0$; by the Lemma of Borel-Cantelli this implies that with probability 1

$$(10.4) \quad \lim_{N\to+\infty}\zeta_{N^3}=\lambda.$$

On the other hand, we have for $A < B < 3A/2$

$$
M\left(\sum_{n=A+1}^{B} f(n)\right)\sim(B-A)\lambda
$$

and

$$(10.5) \quad D^2\left(\sum_{n=A+1}^{B} f(n)\right)=o\left(\frac{(B-A)^2}{\sqrt{A}}\right).$$

Thus we have

$$
P\left(\underset{N^3<M\leq(N+1)^3}{\mathrm{Max}}\left|\sum_{N^3+1}^{M} f(n)\right|>\varepsilon N^3\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\right),
$$

and therefore for any $\varepsilon>0$

$$(10.6) \quad \sum_{N=1}^{\infty}P\left(\underset{N^3<M\leq(N+1)^3}{\mathrm{Max}}\left|\sum_{N^3+1}^{M} f(n)\right|>\varepsilon\right)<+\infty.$$

$(10.4)$ and $(10.6)$ imply that with probability 1

$$
(10.7)\qquad \lim_{N\to+\infty}\zeta_N=\lambda.
$$

Thus our Theorem is proved.

To obtain the limiting distribution of $\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(n)-\lambda N\right)/N^{3/4}$ the higher moments have to be calculated. We do not investigate this question here.

**§ 11. Stochastic analogons of Romanoff's theorem.** N. P. Romanoff proves [5] that if $p$ denotes primes and $a>1$ an integer, then the set of integers $n$ which can be represented in the form $n=a^k+p$ $(k=1,2,\ldots)$ has positive density. Erdős [14] has shown that if $Q(x)$ is a polynomial, then the sequence of numbers $n$ which can be written in the form $n=Q(a^k)+p$ also has positive density. In this section we shall consider stochastic analogons of these theorems. First we replace the sequence of primes by a random sequence $\{\mu_l\}$ generated by the sequence of probabilities $q_n=b/\log(n+1)$ $(b>0)$, in which case by (1.6) we have with probability 1 $\mu_l\sim b\log l$, i. e. for $b=1$ the sequence $\mu_l$ has the same order of magnitude as the $l$-th prime number.

We prove the following

**THEOREM 11.** *Let the random sequence $\{\mu_l\}$ be generated by the sequence of probabilities $q_n=b/\log(n+1)$. If $f(n)$ denotes the number of representations of $n$ in the form $n=a_k+\mu_l$, where $a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_k<\cdots$ is a given sequence of integers such that $\lim_{k\to+\infty}\sqrt[k]{a_k}=e^{1/a}$ exists, and $0<a<+\infty$, and $S_r$ is the sequence of those numbers $n$ for which $f(n)=r$ $(r=0,1,\ldots)$, then $S_r$ has with probability 1 the density $(ab)^r e^{-ab}/r!$.*

**Proof.** The proof of Theorem 11 is analogous to that of Theorem 2. Putting $\varepsilon_r(n)=1$ or $0$ according to whether $f(n)=r$ or $f(n)\ne r$, and taking into account that if $n'$ is defined by $a_{n'}\le n<a_{n'+1}$ then by supposition $n'/\log n\to a$, we obtain

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n'}\frac{b}{\log(n-a_k+1)}
=ab+\frac{b}{\log(n-a_{n'}+1)}+o(1).
$$

It follows that

$$
\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}M(\varepsilon_r(n))
=\frac{(ab)^r e^{-ab}}{r!}\qquad (r=0,1,\ldots).
$$

Taking into account that the number of quadruples $(n,m,a_k,a_l)$ with $n<m<N$ and $a_k<a_l<N$ such that $n-a_k=m-a_l$ is evidently of order $N\log^2 N$, we obtain

$$
D^2\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\varepsilon_r(n)\right)=O\left(\frac{\log^2 N}{N}\right),
$$

and the proof can be completed as that of Theorem 3.

Another possible stochastic analogon of Romanoff's theorem could be obtained by considering the sums $n=v_k+p$ where $v_k$ is a random sequence generated by the probabilities $p_n=c/n$ ($c>0$) (which implies according to (1.7) $\lim_{k\to+\infty}\sqrt[k]{v_k}=e^{1/c}$) and $p$ is a prime. In that case, denoting by $f(n)$ the number of representations of $n$ in the form $n=v_k+p$, we have

$$
M(f(n))=\sum_{p<n}\frac{1}{n-p},
$$

where the summation is extended over all primes $p<n$; this sum is very sensitive to the irregularities of the distribution of primes and thus this problem requires careful separate consideration. If, however, we replace the sequence of primes by a sequence $\{\mu_l\}$ of similar growth but more regular behaviour, then these difficulties disappear.

We consider the case where both sequences are random in the following section.

**§ 12. The sum of a dense and a rare sequence.** In this section we shall prove a general theorem, which is of the same type as Theorem 2 and which covers some cases in which condition (2.5) is not satisfied. This theorem runs as follows.

**THEOREM 12.** Let $\{v_k\}$ and $\{\mu_l\}$ be independent random sequences of integers, generated by the sequence of probabilities $\{p_n\}$ and $\{q_n\}$, where the sequence $q_n$ is decreasing. Let us put

$$
(12.1)\quad P(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_k\quad\text{and}\quad Q(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_k
$$

and suppose that $P(n)\to+\infty$, $Q(n)\to+\infty$ for $n\to+\infty$ and that for any $p>0$ we have

$$
(12.2)\quad \lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{Q(np)}{Q(n)}=p.
$$

Further, let us put

$$
(12.3)\quad r_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}p_kq_{n-k}
$$

and

$$
(12.4)\quad R(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n} r_k.
$$

Suppose that

$$
(12.5)\quad \lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{R(n)}{n}=\lambda>0.
$$

Let $f(n)$ denote the number of solutions of $n=v_k+\mu_i$; then the sequence $S_r$ of numbers $n$ for which $f(n)=r$ has with probability 1 the density $\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}/r!$ ($r=0,1,2,\ldots$).

Remark. Clearly it follows from (12.2) that condition (2.5) is not fulfilled. Thus Theorem 12 can be applied in cases where Theorem 2 is not applicable.

Proof of Theorem 12. For the proof we shall need some results of a Tauberian kind.

LEMMA 1. Let $d_k$ be a sequence of positive numbers, $\alpha\geq 0$. Put

$$
(12.6)\quad D(y)=\sum_{k<y} d_k
$$

and

$$
(12.7)\quad d(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} d_kx^k.
$$

If the series (12.7) is convergent for $|x|<1$ and for every $p>0$ we have

$$
(12.8)\quad \lim_{x\to1-0}\frac{d(x^p)}{d(x)}=\frac{1}{p^\alpha},
$$

then we have

$$
(12.9)\quad \lim_{x\to1-0}\frac{d(x)}{D(1/\log x^{-1})}=1
$$

and thus

$$
(12.10)\quad \lim_{y\to+\infty}\frac{D(py)}{D(y)}=p^\alpha.
$$

Conversely if (12.10) holds then (12.7) is convergent for $|x|<1$ and (12.9) and thus (12.8) is valid. In other words, (12.8) and (12.10) are equivalent.

Remark. The first assertion of Lemma 1 is due to J. Karamata [17], the second to N. G. de Bruijn and P. Erdős [18].

**LEMMA 2.** Let $\{p_k\}$ and $\{q_k\}$ be two sequences of positive numbers, and put

$$(12.11)\qquad r_k=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}p_k b_{n-k}.$$

**Put**

$$(12.12)\qquad P(n)=\sum_{k=1}^n p_k,\qquad Q(n)=\sum_{k=1}^n q_k,\qquad R(n)=\sum_{k=1}^n r_k.$$

**Suppose that for every $p>0$ we have**

$$(12.13)\qquad \lim_{y\to+\infty}\frac{Q(py)}{Q(y)}=p^\alpha\qquad\text{and}\qquad \lim_{y\to+\infty}\frac{R(py)}{R(y)}=p^\gamma$$

where $0\leq\alpha\leq\gamma$. Then we have

$$(12.14)\qquad \lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{P(n)Q(n)}{R(n)}=\frac{\Gamma(1+\gamma)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)\Gamma(1+\gamma-\alpha)}.$$

**Remark.** Lemma 2 is due to N. G. de Bruijn and P. Erdős [18]. Now we are in a position to prove Theorem 12. Let us put

$$(12.15)\qquad p(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_kx^k,\qquad q(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_kx^k\qquad\text{and}\qquad r(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}r_kx^k.$$

Applying Lemma 1 (with $\alpha=1$) we obtain with respect to (12.2) and (12.5)

$$(12.16)\qquad \lim_{x\to1-0}\frac{q(x^p)}{q(x)}=\frac{1}{p}\qquad\text{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to1-0}\frac{r(x^p)}{r(x)}=\frac{1}{p}.$$

Since by (12.3) we clearly have

$$(12.17)\qquad p(x)q(x)=r(x),$$

it follows that

$$(12.18)\qquad \lim_{x\to1-0}\frac{p(x^p)}{p(x)}=1.$$

Applying again Lemma 1 (with $\alpha=0$) we obtain

$$(12.19)\qquad \lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{P(pn)}{P(n)}=1\qquad\text{for any }p>0.$$

Thus we can apply Lemma 2 with $\gamma=1$ and $\alpha=0$ and obtain

$$(12.20)\qquad \lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{P(n)Q(n)}{n}=\lambda.$$

Denoting by $E(N)$ the number of values of $k$ for which $v_k<N$ we have, by $(1.5)$, with probability 1

$$
\tag{12.21}
\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{E(N)}{P(N)}=1.
$$

Let $\{a_k\}$ be a fixed realization of the sequence $\{v_k\}$ for which $(12.21)$ holds. Put

$$
\tag{12.22}
t_n=\sum_{a_k<n}q_{n-a_k}
$$

and

$$
\tag{12.23}
T(N)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}t_n.
$$

Then we evidently have

$$
\tag{12.24}
T(N)=\sum_{a_k<N}Q(N-a_k).
$$

On the other hand, if $e_n=1$, if $n=a_k$ and $e_n=0$ if $a_k<n<a_{k+1}$, we have

$$
\tag{12.25}
t_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}q_{n-k}e_k.
$$

Let us put

$$
\tag{12.26}
e(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e_kx^k
$$

and

$$
\tag{12.27}
t(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}t_kx^k.
$$

By virtue of $(12.21)$ and $(12.19)$ we may apply Lemma 1 with $\alpha=0$ for the sequence $e_k$; we obtain

$$
\tag{12.28}
\lim_{x\to1-0}\frac{e(x^p)}{e(x)}=1.
$$

Since by $(12.27)$ we have

$$
\tag{12.29}
t(x)=q(x)e(x),
$$

it follows from $(12.28)$ and the first relation of $(12.16)$ that

$$
\tag{12.30}
\lim_{x\to1-0}\frac{t(x^p)}{t(x)}=\frac{1}{p}.
$$

Applying again (in the opposite direction) Lemma 1 with $\alpha=1$ for the sequence $t_n$, we find that for $p>0$

$$
\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{T(pN)}{T(N)}=p. \tag{12.31}
$$

Applying again Lemma 2, by (12.31) we have

$$
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{E(n)Q(n)}{T(n)}=1, \tag{12.32}
$$

and thus with respect to (12.21) we obtain

$$
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{P(n)Q(n)}{T(n)}=1 \tag{12.33}
$$

and finally with respect to (12.20)

$$
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{T(n)}{n}=\lambda. \tag{12.34}
$$

Now, since $q_n$ is decreasing, we clearly have

$$
Q(2n)-Q(n)=\sum_{n<k\leq 2n}q_k\leq nq_n \tag{12.35}
$$

and thus

$$
\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\frac{nq_n}{Q(n)}
\geq
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{Q(2n)-Q(n)}{Q(n)}=1. \tag{12.36}
$$

On the other hand, we have by (12.25)

$$
t_n\geq q_nE(n) \tag{12.37}
$$

and thus by (12.20), (12.21) and (12.36)

$$
\liminf_{n\to+\infty}t_n\geq\lambda. \tag{12.38}
$$

Thus the sequence $t_n$, which according to (12.34) is $(C,1)$-summable to the limit $\lambda$, is such that its inferior limit is not less than $\lambda$. This clearly implies

$$
\liminf_{n\to+\infty}t_n=\lambda. \tag{12.39}
$$

Now we need the following (well-known)

LEMMA 3. If a sequence $t_n$ is $(C,1)$-summable to the limit $\lambda$ and $\liminf_{n\to+\infty}t_n=\lambda$, then for any bounded function $g(x)$ which is continuous for $x=\lambda$ the sequence $g(t_n)$ is $(C,1)$-summable to the limit $g(\lambda)$.

Proof. The proof of Lemma 3 is very simple; as a matter of fact, our suppositions imply that for any $\varepsilon>0$ the sequence of those values of $n$ for which $t_n\geq\lambda+\varepsilon$ has the density $0$, whence — in view of the sup- positions concerning $g(x)$ — the assertion of Lemma 3 immediately follows.

We can apply Lemma 3 to our sequence $t_n$ in view of (12.34) and (12.39) and for the continuous and bounded functions $g_r(x)=x^r e^{-x}/r!$ ($r=0,1,\dots$), and find that

$$
(12.40)\quad \lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{t_k^r e^{-t_k}}{r!}=\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}\quad(r=0,1,\dots).
$$

Let us now define the random variables $\varepsilon_r(n)$ as follows: $\varepsilon_r(n)=1$ if $n$ has exactly $r$ representations of the form $n=a_k+\mu_i$; otherwise we put $\varepsilon_r(n)=0$. It follows that

$$
(12.41)\quad M(\varepsilon_r(n))=\sum_{i_1<i_2<\cdots<i_r}q_{n-a_{i_1}}q_{n-a_{i_2}}\cdots q_{n-a_{i_r}}\prod_{\substack{a_k<n\\ k\ne i_1,i_2,\ldots,i_r}}(1-q_{n-a_k}).
$$

Thus we find by (12.40) that

$$
(12.42)\quad \lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N M(\varepsilon_r(n))=\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}\quad(r=0,1,\dots).
$$

Let us now consider the random variable

$$
(12.43)\quad \zeta_r(N)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N\varepsilon_r(n).
$$

It follows by (12.42) that

$$
(12.44)\quad \lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}M(\zeta_r(N))=\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}\quad(r=0,1,\dots).
$$

Let us now consider the variance of $\zeta_r(N)$.

We evidently have

$$
(12.45)\quad D^2(\zeta_r(N))=\frac{1}{N^2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^N\sum_{m=1}^N\left[M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m))-M(\varepsilon_r(n))M(\varepsilon_r(m))\right]\right).
$$

Clearly $\varepsilon_r(n)$, $\varepsilon_r(m)$ are independent if $n-m$ cannot be represented in the form $n-m=a_{k_1}-a_{k_2}$ because in that case $n-a_{k_1}$ and $m-a_{k_2}$ cannot be equal and thus a pair of representations $n=a_{k_1}+\mu_i$ and $m=a_{k_2}+\mu_i$ with the same $\mu_i$ is impossible. For a fixed pair $(k_1,k_2)$ the number of pairs $(n,m)$ for which $n\leq N, m\leq N$ and $n-m=a_{k_1}-a_{k_2}$ cannot exceed $N$, and the number of pairs $(k_1,k_2)$ with $a_{k_1}<N, a_{k_2}<N$ does not exceed $CP^2(N)$ where $c>0$. In view of

$$
\left|M(\varepsilon_r(n)\varepsilon_r(m))-M(\varepsilon_r(n))M(\varepsilon_r(m))\right|\leq 1
$$

it follows that

$$
(12.46)\quad D^2(\zeta_r(N))=o\left(\frac{P^2(N)}{N}\right).
$$

Clearly for any $\epsilon>0$ we have by (12.19)

$$
(12.47)\quad \frac{P(2n)}{P(n)}\leq 1+\epsilon\quad\text{for }n\geq n_0(\epsilon).
$$

This implies that for any $\delta>0$

$$
(12.48)\quad P(N)=o(N^\delta).
$$

Thus we obtain from (12.46) and (12.48)

$$
(12.49)\quad D^2(\zeta_r(N))=o\left(\frac{1}{N^{1-\delta}}\right)\quad\text{for any }\delta>0.
$$

Now, exactly as in proving Theorem 2, we conclude that with probability 1 we have

$$
(12.50)\quad \lim_{N\to+\infty}\zeta_r(N)=\frac{\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}}{r!}.
$$

Thus the sequence $S_r$ of those integers which have exactly $r$ representations of the form $n=a_k+\mu_l$ where $\{a_k\}$ is a fixed realization of the random sequence $\{v_k\}$, for which (12.21) holds, has with probability 1 the density $\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}/r!$.

Since almost every realization $\{a_k\}$ of the random sequence $\{v_k\}$ satisfies (12.21), it follows that the sequence $S_r$ of those integers $n$ which have exactly $r$ representations of the form $n=v_k+\mu_l$ has with probability 1 the density $\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}/r!$.

To show that this last — intuitively clear — step is really admissible, let us consider the following realization of the probabilistic set-up. Let the probability space be the unit square of the $(x,y)$ plane, and let the probability measure $P$ be the ordinary Lebesgue plane measure. A random variable is then nothing else than a measurable function $\zeta(x,y)$ of the two variables $x$ and $y$ ($0\leq x\leq 1, 0\leq y\leq 1$). Let us construct the random variables $\xi_n$ so that they depend only on $x$, i. e. $\xi_n(x,y)=\xi_n(x)$ and the random variables $\eta_n$ so that they depend only on $y$, i. e. $\eta_n(x,y)=\eta_n(y)$; suppose that the $\xi_n(x)$ are independent (with respect to the Lebesgue measure in the interval $0 \leq x \leq 1$) and, further, that the $\eta_n(y)$ are also independent (with respect to the Lebesgue measure in the interval $0 \leq y \leq 1$)); suppose that they have the prescribed distri-  
butions, i. e. $\xi_n(x)=1$ on a set of measure $p_n$ and 0 elsewhere and, further, $\eta_n(y)=1$ on a set of measure $q_n$ and 0 elsewhere. Then for each point $(x,y)$ of the square $0 \leq x \leq 1$, $0 \leq y \leq 1$ the sequences $v_k$ and $\mu_l$ are well-defined (numerical) sequences of positive integers and thus the se-  
quence $S_r=S_r(x,y)$ is also uniquely defined for all points $(x,y)$. Now let $E_r$ denote the set of those points $(x,y)$ for which the sequence $S_r$ has the density $\lambda^r e^{-\lambda}/r!$ and let $\bar{E}_r$ be the complementary set. According to  
Theorem 11 the intersection of $\bar{E}_r$ with the line $x=x_0$ ($0 \leq x_0 \leq 1$) has for almost all $x_0$ the (linear) measure 0; thus by Fubini's theorem $\bar{E}_r$ has  
itself the plane measure 0, which was to be proved.

Thus Theorem 12 is proved.

Remark. If $p_n=a/n$ ($a>0$) and $q_n=b/\log(n+1)$ ($b>0$), then all conditions of Theorem 12 are satisfied (with $\lambda=ab$) and thus the conclusion of Theorem 12 is valid. Note that in that case, according to $(1.7)$, we have with probability $1$ $\lim_{k\to+\infty}\sqrt[k]{v_k}=e^{1/a}$, and denoting by $\Pi(N)$ the number of terms of the sequence $\mu_l$ not exceeding $N$ we have by $(1.5)$ with probability $1$ $\Pi(N)\sim bN/\log N$. Thus this special case of Theorem 12 furnishes another stochastic analogon of Romanoff's theorem.

It would be possible to prove a more general theorem, which in-  
cludes both Theorem 2 and Theorem 12, but to obtain the most general theorem one has to solve some difficulties of Tauberian type.
