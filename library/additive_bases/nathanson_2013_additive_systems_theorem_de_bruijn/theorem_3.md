## Statement

Definitions (pp. 1--5). An *additive system* is a family
$\mathcal A=(A_i)_{i\in I}$, with $I$ nonempty and finite or infinite, of
sets of integers with $0\in A_i$ and $|A_i|\ge2$ for all $i$, such that the
sums $\sum_{i\in I}a_i$ with $a_i\in A_i$ for all $i$ and $a_i\ne0$ for only
finitely many $i$ are exactly the nonnegative integers, and every
nonnegative integer has exactly one such representation. A
*British number system* is the additive system
$(G_{i-1}*[0,g_i))_{i\in\mathbf N}$ generated by an infinite sequence of
integers $g_i\ge2$, where $G_0=1$ and $G_i=g_1\cdots g_i$
([[additive_bases/nathanson_2013_additive_systems_theorem_de_bruijn/theorem_2|Theorem 2]]).
A *contraction* of an additive system $(B_j)_{j\in J}$ is a system
$(\sum_{j\in J_i}B_j)_{i\in I}$ for a partition $\{J_i\}_{i\in I}$ of $J$
into pairwise disjoint nonempty sets, and it is *proper* if some block has
at least two members
([[additive_bases/nathanson_2013_additive_systems_theorem_de_bruijn/lemma_2|Lemma 2]]
and pp. 3--4).

**Theorem 3** (p. 7, quoted). "Every additive system is a British number
system or a proper contraction of a British number system."

**The partition the proof constructs** (p. 8). When the repeated application
of
[[additive_bases/nathanson_2013_additive_systems_theorem_de_bruijn/lemma_7|Lemma 7]]
never terminates, it produces an infinite sequence $(g_n)_{n\in\mathbf N}$
with $g_n\ge2$, and with $G_0=1$, $G_n=g_1\cdots g_n$ the proof sets

$$
L_i=\{n\in\mathbf N:G_{n-1}\in A_i\}\qquad(i\in I),
$$

shows that $(L_i)_{i\in I}$ is a partition of $\mathbf N$ into nonempty,
pairwise disjoint sets, and proves

$$
A_i=\sum_{n\in L_i}G_{n-1}*[0,g_n)\qquad\text{for all }i\in I, \tag{4}
$$

from the two inclusions (5) and (6). So $\mathcal A$ is a contraction of the
British number system generated by $(g_n)$. When the process stops after
finitely many steps, it ends at the one-set system $(\mathbf N_0)$, and the
proof concludes through Lemma 5 and the fact that $(\mathbf N_0)$ is a
contraction of every British number system (pp. 7--8). The one-set system
$(\mathbf N_0)$ itself, the case $|I|=1$, is a proper contraction of every
British number system (p. 7).

**Source.** Melvyn B. Nathanson, Additive systems and a theorem of de Bruijn,
Amer. Math. Monthly 121 (2014), no. 1, 5--17,
doi:10.4169/amer.math.monthly.121.01.005, read in the arXiv version
1301.6208v2 (12 April 2013) identified on the
[[additive_bases/nathanson_2013_additive_systems_theorem_de_bruijn/_index|source card]],
whose pages are numbered 1 to 12; labels and pages here are that version's.
The theorem is stated on p. 7 and proved on pp. 7--8; Lemma 4, on which the
proof rests through Lemma 5, is stated on p. 4 and proved in Appendix A
(pp. 10--12). The paper attributes the theorem to de Bruijn, On number
systems, Nieuw Arch. Wisk. (3) 4 (1956), 15--17, its reference [4].

## Proof pointer

Pp. 7--8. Starting from $\mathcal A_0=\mathcal A$, Lemma 7 is applied as long
as the current system has at least two sets, giving systems
$\mathcal A_{r}$ and radices $g_r\ge2$ with $\mathcal A_{r-1}$ a contraction
of $\mathcal A_r$ dilated by $g_r$. Lemma 5 (p. 4), the iterated form of
Lemma 4 (a contraction of a dilation of a contraction of a dilation is a
contraction of a dilation), then makes $\mathcal A$ a contraction of
$\mathcal A_n$ dilated by $(g_i)_{i\in[1,n]}$ for every $n$ reached. In the
infinite case each digit block $G_{n-1}*[0,g_n)$ lies inside exactly one
$A_i$, which gives the partition $(L_i)$; every element $x$ of $A_i$ with
$1\le x<G_N$ is then a sum of digits from blocks of $L_i$, which gives (5),
and (6) is immediate, so (4) holds.

## Bears on

- [[../wiki/problems/additive_bases/E1145/_index|Problem 1145]], as a
  classification of the exact case only. A pair $U,V$ of sets with
  $0\in U\cap V$, $|U|,|V|\ge2$ and $\mathbf N_0=U\oplus V$ is an additive
  system with two sets, so by the theorem and (4) it arises by splitting the
  digit blocks of some British number system into two classes.
  The theorem concerns unique
  representation of every nonnegative integer; it says nothing about
  representations that are unique only for large $n$, about bounded
  representation counts above $1$, or about the ratio $a_n/b_n$, and the
  paper does not mention the problem.
