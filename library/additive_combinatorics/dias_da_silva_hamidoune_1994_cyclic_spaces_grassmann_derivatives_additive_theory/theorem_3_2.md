## Statement

Setting (§ 1, p. 140): $F$ is a field, $V$ a vector space of finite
dimension $d$ over $F$, and $p$ the characteristic of $F$ when that is
nonzero and $\infty$ otherwise; $m$ is a positive integer. $\wedge^mV$ is
the $m$th Grassmann space of $V$, and a linear operator $f$ on $V$ induces
its derivative $Df$ on $\wedge^mV$, which on a decomposable element
$v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_m$ is the sum over $i$ of the products with $v_i$
replaced by $f(v_i)$ (display (1), p. 140). For an operator $g$ and a vector
$x$, $\mathcal C_g(x)$ is the cyclic subspace spanned by the $g^i(x)$,
$i\ge0$ (p. 143).

**Theorem 3.2** (p. 143). For every linear operator $f$ on $V$ and every
$v\in V$,

$$
\dim\mathcal C_{Df}\bigl(v\wedge f(v)\wedge\cdots\wedge f^{m-1}(v)\bigr)\ge\min\{p,\,(\dim\mathcal C_f(v)-m)m+1\}.
$$

When $\dim\mathcal C_f(v)<m$ the vectors $v,\ldots,f^{m-1}(v)$ are
dependent, the generator is $0$, and the right side is at most $1-m\le0$,
so the content is the case $\dim\mathcal C_f(v)\ge m$. Taking $v$ with
$\dim\mathcal C_f(v)$ largest gives the form announced in the abstract and
§ 1 (p. 140): $Df$ has a cyclic subspace of dimension at least
$\min\{p,m(n-m)+1\}$, where $n$ is the largest dimension of a cyclic
subspace of $f$. The abstract and § 1 state that this bound is sharp and
attained when $f$ has $d$ distinct eigenvalues in arithmetic progression;
the paper's worked sharpness example is
[[additive_combinatorics/dias_da_silva_hamidoune_1994_cyclic_spaces_grassmann_derivatives_additive_theory/theorem_4_1|Example 4.1]]
(p. 144), stated for sums of $m$-subsets.

**Source.** J. A. Dias da Silva and Y. O. Hamidoune, Cyclic spaces for
Grassmann derivatives and additive theory, Bull. London Math. Soc. 26
(1994), no. 2, 140--146, DOI 10.1112/blms/26.2.140: Theorem 3.2 on printed
p. 143, its proof on pp. 143--144, the setting on p. 140. The edition is

## Proof pointer

Pages 143--144. Put $n=\dim\mathcal C_f(v)$. Theorem 3.1 (p. 143) expands
$(Df)^k\bigl(v\wedge f(v)\wedge\cdots\wedge f^{m-1}(v)\bigr)$ as a sum over
the partitions $\lambda$ of $k$ into at most $m$ parts, each term the
wedge $f^{\lambda_1}(v)\wedge f^{\lambda_2+1}(v)\wedge\cdots\wedge f^{\lambda_m+m-1}(v)$
weighted by $k!$ divided by the product of the hook lengths of $\lambda$.
For $k$ up to $\min\{p-1,(n-m)m\}$ the proof takes the partition $\omega$
of $k$ into $m$ parts as equal as possible, whose weight divides $k!$ and so
is nonzero modulo $p$, and shows that its wedge is the largest basis
element, in the order by degree and then lexicographically, with a nonzero
component. A wedge whose last exponent $\lambda_m+m-1$ is at least $n$ is
rewritten by expressing $f^{\lambda_m+m-1}(v)$ in the basis
$v,\ldots,f^{n-1}(v)$ of $\mathcal C_f(v)$, which leaves a combination of
basis elements of lower degree. Distinct leading elements for distinct $k$
make the iterates linearly independent, which gives the bound. (The proof's
opening line prints $\max\{p-1,m(n-m)\}$ where its conclusion has $\min$.)

## Dependencies

Within the paper: Theorem 3.1 (p. 143), proved by induction on $k$ from
Corollary 2.3 (p. 142), the identity that $k+1$ divided by the hook-length
product of a partition $\lambda$ of $k+1$ equals the sum, over the
partitions $\mu$ obtained by removing one box from $\lambda$, of the
reciprocal hook-length products of $\mu$. Outside it: the hook-length
formula and the branching rule for the characters of $S_k$ ([7], James and
Kerber, Theorems 2.3.21 and 2.4.3).
