# analysis/edgar_2003_borel_subrings_reals

[[analysis/_index|..]]

[[analysis/edgar_2003_borel_subrings_reals/theorem_1|theorem_1]]: Edgar and Miller's main theorem: a subring of the reals that is a Borel set
either has Cartesian-Hausdorff dimension zero, so that it and all its finite
Cartesian powers have Hausdorff dimension zero, or is the whole real line;
the paper's remarks extend it to analytic sets.

[[analysis/edgar_2003_borel_subrings_reals/theorem_2|theorem_2]]: The complex analog of Edgar and Miller's main theorem: a subring of the
complex numbers that is a Borel set has Cartesian-Hausdorff dimension zero
or equals the reals or the complex numbers.

[[analysis/edgar_2003_borel_subrings_reals/theorem_3|theorem_3]]: The p-adic analog of Edgar and Miller's main theorem: a subring of the
p-adic numbers that is a Borel set has Cartesian-Hausdorff dimension zero
or equals the p-adic numbers or the p-adic integers; the proof is sketched.

## Contents

$\dim$ is Hausdorff dimension. By the Dimension Inequality
$\dim(A\times B)\ge\dim A+\dim B$ for Borel sets, $(1/n)\dim(A^n)$
converges; the limit is the Cartesian--Hausdorff (CH) dimension of $A$,
and it is $0$ iff $\dim(A^n)=0$ for every $n$, which forces $\dim A=0$
(p. 1).

- [[analysis/edgar_2003_borel_subrings_reals/theorem_1|Theorem 1]]
  (p. 1; lemmas and proof pp. 2--3): a subring of $\mathbb R$ that is a
  Borel set is either all of $\mathbb R$ or of CH dimension zero.
  Lemma 1.1 (p. 2): a Borel $A\subset\mathbb R^k$ with $\dim A>1$ has an
  image of positive Lebesgue measure under almost all linear functionals
  (the Projection Theorem). Lemma 1.2 (p. 2): a Borel additive subgroup
  $E$ of nonzero CH dimension has $\varphi(E^k)=\mathbb R$ for some $k$
  and linear functional $\varphi$ (Steinhaus's theorem). Lemma 1.3
  (pp. 2--3): for a subring $E$, such $k$ and $\varphi$ can be chosen with
  $\varphi$ injective on $E^k$ (take $k$ minimal). Lemma 1.4 (p. 3): if a
  linear functional maps $E^k$ bijectively onto $\mathbb R$ for a Borel
  additive subgroup $E$, then $k=1$ and $E=\mathbb R$ (a Borel measurable
  additive map $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ is linear).
- Remarks (pp. 3--4): Theorem 1 holds for analytic sets as well, since the
  Dimension Inequality, the Projection Theorem and Borel isomorphism
  extend to them; hence the ring $\mathbb Z[X]$ generated by an analytic
  set $X\subseteq\mathbb R$ with $\dim X^k>0$ for some $k$ is all of
  $\mathbb R$.
- Historical remarks (p. 2): Volkmann 1960 on subfields; Erdős and
  Volkmann 1966, for every $s\in[0,1]$ a Borel additive subgroup of
  dimension $s$
  ([[analysis/erdos_1966_additive_gruppen_mit_vorgegebener_hausdorffscher_dimension/_index|card]]);
  Davies (unpublished, under the Continuum Hypothesis), subrings of every
  dimension $s\in[0,1]$, not Borel and not analytic; Falconer 1984, a
  Borel or analytic subring has $\dim E\le1/2$ or $\dim E=1$; the authors'
  result on real closed subfields
  ([[analysis/edgar_2001_hausdorff_dimension_analytic_sets_transcendence/_index|Edgar and Miller 2001]]).
- Divisible groups (p. 4): the divisible hull $\bigcup_n(1/n!)G$ of a Borel
  additive subgroup $G$ is a Borel $\mathbb Q$-vector space of the same
  dimension, so the Erdős--Volkmann groups give divisible Borel subgroups
  of every dimension in $[0,1]$.
- [[analysis/edgar_2003_borel_subrings_reals/theorem_2|Theorem 2]]
  (section 2, p. 4; lemmas pp. 4--7): a Borel subring
  $E\subset\mathbb C$ has zero CH dimension or $E=\mathbb R$ or $E=\mathbb C$.
  [[analysis/edgar_2003_borel_subrings_reals/theorem_3|Theorem 3]]
  (section 3, p. 7; proof sketched pp. 7--8): a Borel subring
  $E\subset\mathbb Q_p$ has zero CH dimension or $E=\mathbb Q_p$ or
  $E=\mathbb Z_p$.

**Bears on.** [[../wiki/problems/analysis/E1154/_index|#1154]], which asks for a ring or
field in $\mathbb R$ of each Hausdorff dimension $\alpha\in[0,1]$:
[[analysis/edgar_2003_borel_subrings_reals/theorem_1|Theorem 1]] and its
analytic extension show that no Borel or analytic subring of $\mathbb R$,
and so no such subfield, has Hausdorff dimension strictly between $0$ and
$1$, so any example for $0<\alpha<1$ must lie outside those classes; the
subrings of Davies's unpublished construction under the Continuum
Hypothesis, which the paper reports (p. 2), are not Borel and not
analytic. The theorem decides no $\alpha$ in $(0,1)$.
