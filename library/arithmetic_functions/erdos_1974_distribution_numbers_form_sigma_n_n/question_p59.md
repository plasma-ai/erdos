***

**Source.** The introduction, pp. 59-60, of P. Erdős, *On the distribution
of numbers of the form $\sigma(n)/n$ and on some related questions*, Pacific
Journal of Mathematics 52 (1974), no. 1, 59-65, as identified on the
[[arithmetic_functions/erdos_1974_distribution_numbers_form_sigma_n_n/_index|source card]].
The passage is unnumbered.

## Statement

**Background recorded** (p. 59). Schoenberg proved that $\phi(n)/n$ has a
continuous distribution function (1928), and Behrend, Chowla and Davenport
that $\sigma(n)/n$ has one, where $\sigma(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d$. Erdős states
that he proved the distribution functions of $\sigma(n)/n$ and of
$\phi(n)/n$ purely singular, citing his 1939 paper in Amer. J. Math. 61.

**The questions and claims** (pp. 59-60). Let $g(c)$ be the distribution
function of $\sigma(n)/n$. Since $g$ is purely singular and monotone, its
derivative is $0$ almost everywhere. Erdős then states:

- as far as he knows, it is not known whether the derivative can take any
  other value;
- the right derivative of $g$ at $c=\sigma(n)/n$ is infinite ("easy to
  see"), while it is doubtful whether the left derivative there exists;
- he does not know whether the right (or left) derivative can take any
  value other than $0$ or infinity;
- there is a dense set of values $c$ at which the derivative exists neither
  from the left nor from the right ("easy to see").

The paper gives no proofs of these claims.

## Proof pointer

None in the paper; the singularity is cited to Erdős, On the smoothness of
the asymptotic distribution of additive arithmetical functions, Amer. J.
Math. 61 (1939), 722-725, and the claims about one-sided derivatives are
stated as easy without argument.

## Dependencies

The cited 1939 singularity theorem. Read depth: claims checked; the passage
was read clause by clause on pp. 59-60.

## Bears on

- [[../wiki/problems/arithmetic_functions/E0050/_index|Problem 50]]: the
  problem asks whether the distribution function of $\phi(n)/n$ ever has a
  positive derivative. This passage asks an analogous question for
  $\sigma(n)/n$, namely whether the derivative of its distribution function
  can take any value other than $0$, and records that the distribution
  function of $\phi(n)/n$ is purely singular, so its derivative is $0$ almost
  everywhere. Neither statement settles the problem.
