## Exact conclusion and premises

Let $B=\{m^{13}:m\in\mathbb Z\}$ and $D=B-B$. There exists one
$A\subseteq\mathbb Z$ such that, for every $n\in\mathbb Z$, there is exactly
one pair $(a,m)\in A\times\mathbb Z$ with $n=a+m^{13}$. Equivalently,
the translates $a+B$ partition $\mathbb Z$. Negative inputs and zero are
included. Injectivity of the odd-power map equates uniqueness of the
value $b$ with uniqueness of $m$.

The dependency order is the recalled genus-zero unit bounds to Lemma 1.4
to Corollary 1.5; that corollary and the journal theorem to Proposition 1.6;

## Per-result deductions

### Lemma 1.4: rational curves and the three lines

For every $N\in\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}$, the statement concerns maps
defined over $\mathbb Q$ from $\mathbb P^1_{\mathbb Q}$ to
$X^{13}+Y^{13}+Z^{13}=NW^{13}$. If $N$ is not a rational thirteenth
power, there is no nonconstant rational map. If $N=d^{13}$ with
$d\in\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}$, the geometric images of nonconstant
morphisms are exactly the three lines
$X=-Y,\ Z=dW$; $X=-Z,\ Y=dW$; and $Y=-Z,\ X=dW$.
This does not exclude maps defined only over an extension or assert that
every rational point in an image has a rational parameter preimage.

The normalization is valid. Generic rational coordinates over $\mathbb Q$
can be cleared of denominators, homogenized to their maximum degree and
divided by their common homogeneous divisor. All active coordinates retain
a common degree $e$. A common geometric zero would be a common linear
factor over the algebraic closure; its conjugates would give a common
factor over $\mathbb Q$. Therefore the reduced tuple has no base point,
including infinity, and extends to a morphism. The surface relation is a
homogeneous polynomial identity and survives cancellation. A degree-zero
tuple is constant, so nonconstancy gives $e\ge1$.

The height and support calculation needs all these normalization facts.
For the active forms $P_i$, the polynomials $p_i(s)=P_i(s,1)$ have minimum
order zero at every finite point. At least one $p_i$ has degree $e$, or
infinity would be a common zero of the homogeneous tuple. The minimum
order of the thirteenth powers at infinity is thus $-13e$, giving
projective height $13e$. A nonzero constant coefficient such as $-N$
changes no order; a common rational factor changes the sum by its
principal divisor, of degree zero.

A dehomogenized polynomial can have a pole at infinity even if its
homogeneous form does not vanish there. The applied $S$-units are instead
ratios of forms of equal degree. Their divisors are supported on the union
of the homogeneous zero divisors: the equal-degree dehomogenization terms
cancel. Thus this union $\Sigma$, of at most $re$ distinct points for
$r$ active forms, is the correct support for the normalized units.
Infinity is included whenever it is a homogeneous root.

If all normalized thirteenth-power ratios were constant, every original
coordinate ratio would have zero order everywhere and be constant,
making the map constant. At least one normalized unit is therefore
nonconstant. Its zero and pole occur at distinct points, so
$|\Sigma|\ge2$ as required. This checks normalized nonconstancy, not merely
nonconstancy of an unreduced coordinate.

The active-coordinate and zero-subsum cases exhaust the possibilities:

- With four active terms and no proper vanishing subsum, the four-term
  bound gives $13e\le3(|\Sigma|-2)\le12e-6$, impossible for $e\ge1$.
- With three active terms and no proper vanishing subsum, the three-term
  bound gives $13e\le|\Sigma|-2\le3e-2$, also impossible. A proper
  vanishing subsum would have two terms and leave a single nonzero
  complementary term equal to zero.
- Zero active terms are impossible in projective coordinates; one active
  term cannot sum to zero. With exactly two, the coordinate ratio has
  constant thirteenth power and hence is constant, so the map is constant.
- The remaining case is four active terms split into two vanishing pairs.
  The term $-NR^{13}$ must pair with one of the other three.

For example, $F^{13}+G^{13}=0$ makes $F/G$ constant and rational: it lies
in $\mathbb Q(s)$, whose constant field is $\mathbb Q$. The only rational
thirteenth root of $-1$ is $-1$. The complementary pair $H^{13}=NR^{13}$
similarly gives $H/R=d\in\mathbb Q^\times$ and $N=d^{13}$. The other
pairings give exactly the other two lines. Roots of unity in an extension
cannot produce additional lines for these $\mathbb Q$-defined maps.

Containment in a line is upgraded to equality of its geometric image.
On the first line the map is $[F:R]$, with no common zero. For any
geometric $[a:b]$, the nonzero homogeneous form $bF-aR$ has positive
degree and a root; the no-common-zero property makes that root map to
$[a:b]$. Identically zero $bF-aR$ would make the map constant. The three
explicit maps conversely parametrize the lines and satisfy the surface
equation.

**Reviewer verdict:** every case and deduction is accepted relative to
the two identified unit inequalities, including coordinate normalization,
complete support, height, rational constants and full image classification.

### Corollary 1.5: affine rational-family exclusion

For every $c\in\mathbb Z\setminus B$, there is no nonconstant rational
one-parameter map over $\mathbb Q$ into
$u^{13}-v^{13}-t^{13}=-c$. In particular, there is no nonconstant
polynomial triple over $\mathbb Q$ satisfying that identity.

For an integer $c\notin B$, $c\ne0$. If a reduced fraction $(p/q)^{13}$
is an integer, $q^{13}$ divides $p^{13}$ and coprimality forces $q=1$.
Hence an integer is a rational thirteenth power exactly when it is in
$B$. Oddness preserves this property under negation, so $-c$ is not a
rational thirteenth power.

The invertible substitution $(X,Y,Z)=(u,-v,-t)$ gives the projective
surface with $N=-c$. A nonconstant rational one-parameter map to the affine
surface stays nonconstant in the projective closure and extends as
established in Lemma 1.4. That lemma excludes it. Polynomial triples over
$\mathbb Q$ are a special case. No dominance hypothesis for parametrizing
the whole surface is inserted.

**Reviewer verdict:** the entire stated exclusion is accepted for precisely
$c\in\mathbb Z\setminus B$.

### Proposition 1.6: the fixed-shift counting estimate

Define $S_c(T)=\{t\in\mathbb Z:|t|\le T,\ t^{13}-c\in D\}$.
For each fixed $c\in\mathbb Z\setminus B$ and every real $T\ge1$, the
claim is $|S_c(T)|\le K_cT^{5/6}$, with $K_c$ independent of $T$, and
consequently $|S_c(T)|/T\to0$. No uniformity in $c$ is required.

Every witness $t^{13}-c=u^{13}-v^{13}$ has $u\ne v$. The quotient
polynomial $Q(u,v)$ is positive for every real nonzero pair: strict
increase of the odd-power map proves this off the diagonal; on the
nonzero diagonal, $Q(u,u)=13u^{12}>0$. Its minimum $\kappa$ on the
compact max-norm unit boundary is positive. Homogeneity and integer
separation $|u-v|\ge1$ yield

$$
\kappa\max(|u|,|v|)^{12}\le |t^{13}-c|\le(1+|c|)T^{13}.
$$

This bounds every witness, not just a selected representative. It includes
opposite signs and zero coordinates. With $C_c\ge1$,
$X=C_cT^{13/12}$ also bounds $|t|$.

Set $(x,y,z)=(u,-v,-t)$. The equation is
$x^{13}+y^{13}+z^{13}=-c$. Multiplication by $\operatorname{sgn}(-c)$
gives a homogeneous integral ternary form $F_c$ with positive right side
$N_c=|c|$. Its first derivatives are nonzero constants times $x_i^{12}$,
whose only common geometric zero is the origin. Thus the required
projective nonsingularity holds.

The degree threshold is $\lfloor13/10\rfloor=1$. Under the disclosed
source convention, a removed family has positive maximum degree. Any
such integer-polynomial identity would give a nonconstant rational map to
Corollary 1.5's affine surface after the inverse sign change. None exists.
This excludes every such family before its parameter-value domain matters.

For the local whole-box deduction, fix $c$ and choose $R_c\ge1$ above
which the source range $N_c\ll_{F_c}R$ holds. The theorem's shell constant
is uniform in $R$ above this threshold. If $X\ge R_c$, take shells with
upper radii $X/2^j$, where $0\le j\le J$ and $J$ is maximal with
$X/2^J\ge R_c$. They are disjoint and cover the interval of heights
$(X/2^{J+1},X]$. Their strict lower and inclusive upper endpoints place
each shared boundary in exactly the smaller shell. Their bounds total
at most

$$
\frac{K_{F_c}X^{10/13}}{1-2^{-10/13}}.
$$

The remaining box has height less than $R_c$ and no more than
$(2\lceil R_c\rceil+1)^3$ integer points. The same constant bounds the
whole box when $1\le X<R_c$. Since $X^{10/13}\ge1$, this gives the
claimed $O_c(X^{10/13})$ bound for every $X\ge1$. No small shell outside
the source range is used, and no logarithmic loss appears.

Projection of the finite set of witnessing triples onto $-z$ covers
$S_c(T)$. Different parameters have different third coordinates, so there
are no more parameters than triples. Finally,
$(13/12)(10/13)=5/6$, and division by $T$ leaves a fixed multiple of
$T^{-1/6}$, which tends to zero.

**Reviewer verdict:** the entire estimate is accepted, including the witness
bound for all integer signs, theorem application, exceptional-family
exclusion, shell sum, finite lower scales, projection and limit.
The box estimate is a local fixed-shift deduction, not the journal's
statement as printed or a uniform estimate in $c$.

### Lemma 1.7: the greedy criterion for arbitrary B

For arbitrary $B\subseteq\mathbb Z$, the hypothesis is that, for every
finite $C\subseteq\mathbb Z\setminus B$, there exists $b\in B$ such that
$(C-b)\cap(B-B)=\varnothing$. The conclusion is one
$A\subseteq\mathbb Z$ such that, for every $n\in\mathbb Z$, exactly one
pair $(a,b)\in A\times B$ satisfies $n=a+b$. The explicit pair domain
restores a quantifier omitted in the original report's abbreviated
restatement; it does not change the frozen lemma.

The hypothesis includes the empty finite obstruction set, so it implies
$B\ne\varnothing$. Enumerate all integers. At an uncovered $n$, let $A'$
be the finite set of previously selected indices and use $C=n-A'$.
The uncovered condition ensures $C\subseteq\mathbb Z\setminus B$.
The chosen $b\in B$ permits $a_{\mathrm{new}}=n-b$ and covers $n$.
For every old $a$,

$$
a_{\mathrm{new}}-a=(n-a)-b\notin B-B.
$$

An intersection of the old and new translates would force that same
difference into $B-B$. The new index cannot already be old, because $n$
was uncovered. The finite-stage induction therefore preserves coverage
and disjointness.

At the union, every integer is covered at its own finite stage. Any two
final indices coexist in one finite stage, so their translates remain
disjoint. Once $a$ is fixed, $b=n-a$ is unique. Selecting the first
available $b$ in an enumeration suffices for existence without asserting
an effective decision procedure.

**Reviewer verdict:** accepted at the stated generality for arbitrary
$B\subseteq\mathbb Z$, including the empty-$C$ edge case. No additional
symmetry or polynomial premise on $B$ is used.

### Proposition 1.8: finite avoidance

For the full integer thirteenth-power set $B$, every finite
$C\subseteq\mathbb Z\setminus B$ admits $b\in B$ with
$(C-b)\cap D=\varnothing$, where $D=B-B$.

Fix the finite set $C$ before choosing the height. For $C=\varnothing$,
use $b=0$. Otherwise a bad parameter at $c$ satisfies $c-t^{13}\in D$,
or equivalently $t^{13}-c\in D$, since every difference set is symmetric.
Each $c$ has its own finite constant $K_c$ from Proposition 1.6.
Their finite sum $K_C$ bounds the union of bad sets by $K_CT^{5/6}$.

Choose integral $T$. There are exactly $2T+1$ candidates, and the ratio
of this union bound to $2T+1$ tends to zero. Some candidate therefore
escapes every obstruction for sufficiently large $T$. Its thirteenth
power is the required $b$. Neither an infinite union nor a constant
uniform over all shifts is used.

**Reviewer verdict:** accepted. Using integral $T$ makes the exact $2T+1$
count valid; it suffices for the source's existence deduction.

### Theorem 1.1: composition and formulation

Proposition 1.8 supplies exactly Lemma 1.7's hypothesis for the full image
of integer thirteenth powers. The lemma supplies a unique pair $(a,b)$
for every integer. The odd-power map is injective, so each $b$ has exactly
one integer input $m$. Thus one $A$ works for every integer with no finite
exceptional set and no positivity restriction. The polynomial $X^{13}$
has integer coefficients and degree at least two, so the consumer's
existence question follows.

**Reviewer verdict:** accepted. This is an existence and uniqueness
conclusion, not effectiveness or an exponent classification.
