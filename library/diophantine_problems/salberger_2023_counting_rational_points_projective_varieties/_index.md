primes p_1,...,p_t and non-singular F_{p_i}-points P_i on the reductions of
X, the points of height at most B reducing to every P_i lie on one
hypersurface not containing X of degree
O_{d,r}(q^{-1}B^{(r+1)/rd^{1/r}} log Bq+log Bq+1), q = p_1...p_t.

O_{d,n,eps}(B^{dim X+eps}) when d is at least 4, and
O_{n,eps}(B^{dim X-1+2/sqrt 3+eps}) when d = 3.

coordinates in [-B,B] number O_{d,n,eps}(B^{n-2+eps}) if d is at least 4

O_d(B^{3/sqrt d}(log B)^4+1), and those off all lines number

## Overview

Salberger studies the counting function $N(W;B)$ for rational points of standard
projective height at most $B$ on quasi-projective varieties $W/\mathbb Q$. The
central question is the dimension-growth conjecture: for an integral projective
variety $X\subset \mathbb P^n$ of degree $d\ge 2$, should $N(X;B)$ have order at
most $B^{\dim X+\varepsilon}$? The paper proves the non-uniform form

$$
N(X;B)=O_{X,\varepsilon}(B^{\dim X+\varepsilon})
$$

for every such $X$ (Theorem 0.1, p. 1093; its geometrically integral case is
Theorem 8.13, p. 1130).
The uniform conjecture is stated separately as Conjecture 0.2 (p. 1093), and is
proved here for $d\ge4$:

$$
N(X;B)=O_{d,n,\varepsilon}(B^{\dim X+\varepsilon}).
$$

For $d=3$ the paper obtains the weaker uniform exponent
$\dim X-1+2/\sqrt3+\varepsilon$ (Theorem 0.3, p. 1093; Theorem 7.5, p. 1125).
Thus the fully uniform cubic case is not proved.

The principal innovation is a global determinant method using congruences
simultaneously at many small primes. For a geometrically integral degree-$d$
hypersurface $X\subset\mathbb P^{m+1}$ and a coordinate box
$(B_0,\ldots,B_{m+1})$, Notation 1.1 defines $V=\prod B_i$ and the largest
monomial weight $T$ of a defining form (p. 1098). Theorem 1.2 (pp. 1098–1102)
constructs an auxiliary hypersurface, not containing $X$ but containing all
boxed rational points, of degree

$$
O_{d,m}\!\left(\left(\frac{V}{T^{1/d}}\right)^{1/(m d^{1/m})}\log V+1\right).
$$

Its proof compares the archimedean determinant estimate (1.12) with divisibility
accumulated over all good small primes, especially (1.15) and (1.16) (pp.
1100–1101). Lemma 1.4 supplies the local $p$-adic determinant divisibility,
Lemmas 1.5 and 1.9 control good reduction, and Lemma 1.11 supplies the monomial
determinant estimate. The congruence-refined Theorem 2.2 (pp. 1103–1104) gains a
factor $q^{-1}$ in the auxiliary degree for points specializing to prescribed
nonsingular points modulo the prime factors of $q$; Lemma 2.8 (pp. 1104–1105)
also controls the auxiliary hypersurface's height.

Main Lemma 3.2 (pp. 1107–1111) is the technical core. For $r\ge2$ it covers
the non-singular boxed points of a hypersurface by a controlled collection of
prime divisors together with the supports of effective codimension-two cycles
(part (h)). Parts (a)–(h) quantify the admissible
square-free moduli, the number and degrees of divisors, and the total degree of
the residual cycles; part (i) gives a sharper total-degree estimate when $X$ is
nonsingular. For surfaces, Theorem 3.16 (pp. 1112–1113) turns this into a
covering by bounded-degree curves plus a small exceptional set. In the
equal-height box, Corollaries 3.22 and 3.23 (p. 1114) give
$O_{d,n}(B^{3/(2\sqrt d)}\log B+1)$ bounded-degree curves and leave
$O_{d,n}(B^{3/\sqrt d+o(1)})$ points uncovered. The curve estimate used
throughout is

$$
N(C;B)=O_{d,n}(B^{2/d}\log B+1)
$$

for an integral degree-$d$ curve, with sharper statements for $d\le2$ and for
non-geometrically-integral curves (Theorem 1.17, p. 1102).

Sections 4–5 analyze the exceptional conics. Lemma 4.1 computes the hyperplane
class of the $k$th Hilbert embedding of the conic Hilbert scheme (p. 1115),
while Lemmas 4.2 and 4.3 constrain one- and two-dimensional families of conics
(pp. 1116–1118). Lemma 5.1 bounds points on an individual conic in terms of its
Hilbert height, and Lemma 5.3 sums these bounds over families (pp. 1118–1120).
This geometry is combined with the surface covering theorem in Theorem 6.1 (pp.
1120–1121). In particular, for a nonsingular degree-$d$ surface
$X\subset\mathbb P^3$, Theorem 6.3 and Corollary 6.4 (pp. 1121–1122) prove

$$N(U;B)=O_d(B^{3/\sqrt d}(\log B)^4+1),\qquad
N(X';B)=O_d(B^{3/\sqrt d}(\log B)^4+B),$$

where $U$ omits all curves of degree at most $d-2$ and $X'$ omits all lines. The
analogous complete-intersection estimates in $\mathbb P^4$ are Theorem 6.6 and
Corollary 6.7 (pp. 1122–1123). The exceptional case of a quartic surface with a
two-dimensional conic family receives the exponent $43/28$ in Theorem 6.1.

For affine hypersurfaces, Theorem 7.4 (pp. 1124–1125) states that if
$f\in\mathbb Z[y_1,y_2,y_3]$ has highest homogeneous part irreducible over
$\overline{\mathbb Q}$ of degree $d$, then the number of zeros in $[-B,B]^3$ is
$O_{d,\varepsilon}(B^{1+\varepsilon})$ for $d\ge4$, and
$O_\varepsilon(B^{2/\sqrt3+\varepsilon})$ for $d=3$. Hyperplane-section and
birational-projection arguments then yield the uniform projective bounds of
Theorem 7.5.

The remaining non-uniform cubic case is handled by methods distinct from the
determinant argument. Section 8 analyzes cubic forms containing rational lines,
reducing suitable affine counts to families of binary quadratics or to divisor
estimates; see Theorem 8.11 (p. 1129). Together with cited results for the
complementary cases, this gives

$$
n(G;B)=O_{G,\varepsilon}(B^{n-2+\varepsilon})
$$

for every absolutely irreducible cubic form $G$ (Theorem 8.12, p. 1129), and
hence Theorem 8.13. These cubic conclusions are non-uniform in the form.

Finally, Section 9 is an independent geometric application to diagonal surfaces.
Theorem 9.4 (p. 1131) identifies the $3d^2$ standard lines on a nonsingular
diagonal degree-$d$ surface and proves, for $d\ge3$, that every other integral
curve has degree at least $(d+1)/3$. Combined with the surface estimates, this
yields the coefficient-uniform diagonal bound in Corollary 6.5 (p. 1122).
Results attributed to earlier papers—such as the local determinant bounds
recalled as Theorems 0.10 and 0.11—are background rather than new theorems of
this paper.

## Relation to E940

This source bears on [[../wiki/problems/diophantine_problems/E0940/_index|Problem 940]].

Salberger's bounds count points of bounded height on varieties; they do not
bound the number of integers up to $X$ that are sums of at most $r$
$r$-powerful numbers, which is what E940 asks about.
