**Read depth.** Claims checked: the statement and the definitions it uses were

Nothing here is independently reviewed.

## Statement

Setting (p. 2). Let $\Lambda=\{\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d\}\subset\mathbb C$ be
linearly independent over $\mathbb Z$. It is a *nice basis* when every product
$\lambda_i\lambda_j$, $1\le i,j\le d$, is a $\mathbb Z$-linear combination of
elements of $\Lambda$. For a positive integer $m$ the paper sets

$$
A_m(\Lambda)=\Bigl\{a_1\lambda_1+\cdots+a_d\lambda_d:\ a_i\in\mathbb Z,\
|a_i|\le\frac{m^{1/d}}{3}\Bigr\},
$$

and notes that $|A_m(\Lambda)|=\Theta_d(m)$, with $|A_m(\Lambda)|\le m$ once $m$
is large enough. The theorem and its proof apply this notation with a real
index, such as $m=n^\alpha$. Integral bases of algebraic number fields, and
power bases generated by algebraic integers, are nice bases (p. 2, citing the
author's earlier paper). A line is *$r$-rich* when it contains at least $r$
points of the set (p. 1). Here $F=O(G)$, equivalently $G=\Omega(F)$, means
$F\le CG$ for an absolute constant $C$, and a subscript marks a constant that
depends on a parameter (footnote, p. 1).

**Theorem 1.3** (p. 2, quoted). "Let $\Lambda$ be a nice basis,
$0<\alpha\leq 1/2$, and let
$P=A_{n^\alpha}(\Lambda)\times A_{n^{1-\alpha}}(\Lambda)$. Then, there exists
$C^{\prime}>0$ (depending on $d,\Lambda$) such that for any
$r\leq C^{\prime}n^\alpha$, $P$ determines $\Omega_\Lambda(n^2/r^3)$ $r$-rich
lines."

So one point set serves every $r$ in the range. By Theorem 1.2 of the paper
(the Szemerédi-Trotter bound in its rich-line form, recalled on p. 1), a set of
$n$ points in the plane determines at most $O(n^2/r^3)$ $r$-rich lines for
$r\le n^{1/2}$, so the count is of the optimal order. The paper notes (p. 2)
that $\alpha=1/2$, $\Lambda=\{1\}$ gives Erdős's grid construction and
$\alpha=1/2$, $\Lambda=\{1,\sqrt k\}$ gives the construction of Guth and
Silier.

Section 2 (p. 3) derives from the theorem point-line configurations of $n$
points and $m$ lines with $\Omega(n^{2/3}m^{2/3})$ incidences, matching the
incidence form of Szemerédi-Trotter (Theorem 1.1 of the paper): for, say,
$\Omega(m^{1/2})=n=O(m^2)$ it sets $r=n^{2/3}/m^{1/3}$ and picks any
$0<\alpha<1/2$ with $r\le C'n^\alpha$, which the paper says "will generally
exist" (p. 3).

## Proof pointer

Section 3, pp. 4-5. The proof takes a small product
$P'=A_{C_1n^\alpha/r}(\Lambda)\times A_{C_1n^{1-\alpha}/r}(\Lambda)$ and
$\Omega(r^2)$ disjoint translates of it inside $P$. Beck's theorem (Theorem 2.2,
p. 3) gives each translate $\Omega(n^2/r^4)$ lines through two of its points
(Claim 1). Proposition 2.3 (p. 3), closure of the sets $A_m(\Lambda)$ under sums
and products up to the index, shows that each such line meets $P$ in at least
$r$ points (Claim 2). Summing gives $\Omega(n^2/r^2)$ incidences (Claim 3), and
Theorem 1.1 then forces $\Omega(n^2/r^3)$ lines (Claim 4). No number theory is
used.

## Dependencies

Theorem 1.1 (Szemerédi-Trotter, extended to $\mathbb C^2$ by Tóth and by Zahl),
Theorem 2.2 (Beck) and Proposition 2.3 of the paper, which have no pages here.

## Bears on

- [[../wiki/problems/discrete_geometry/E0102/_index|Problem 102]]: the paper
  does not mention the problem. With $\Lambda\subset\mathbb R$, so that
  $P\subset\mathbb R^2$, $\alpha=1/2$ and the fixed value $r=4$ (lines with
  more than three points), the theorem gives, for all large $n$, sets of
  $\Theta_d(n)$ points with at least $c|P|^2$ such lines for some $c>0$
  depending on $\Lambda$, while every line meets $P$ in $O_d(n^{1/2})$ points
  (an observation of this page: a non-vertical line meets $P$ at most once
  for each first coordinate, a vertical one at most once for each second
  coordinate, and with $\alpha=1/2$ both factors are $A_{n^{1/2}}(\Lambda)$,
  of size $\Theta_d(n^{1/2})$). This bounds
  $h_c$ above by $O(|P|^{1/2})$ at those sizes only for $c$ below that
  constant, the order already given by Erdős's integer grid, which the theorem
  includes. It gives no lower bound on $h_c(n)$ and does not decide whether
  $h_c(n)\to\infty$.
