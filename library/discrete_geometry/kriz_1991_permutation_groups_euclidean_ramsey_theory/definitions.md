## Configurations and colorings

A configuration is a finite subset of a Euclidean space. We use nonempty
configurations in the proofs; the empty configuration, if admitted, satisfies
all the Ramsey conclusions trivially. Dimensions are nonnegative integers,
and a number of colors is a positive integer. Write
$[m]=\{0,\ldots,m-1\}$, with $[0]=\varnothing$.

An isometrical embedding is an injective map preserving all pairwise
Euclidean distances. An isometry of a finite configuration onto itself is a
permutation of its points preserving distance. Its group of isometries is
therefore finite. A group of ambient isometries acting on a configuration
may be replaced by its finite induced permutation group.
A group is **soluble** (or **solvable**) if its derived series,
obtained by repeatedly taking the subgroup generated by commutators,
reaches the identity subgroup after finitely many steps.

Let $E$ be an equivalence relation on $F$. The configuration is
**$E$-Ramsey** if, for every integer $k\ge1$, there is a dimension $N$ such
that every coloring $c:\mathbb R^N\to[k]$ admits an isometrical embedding
$\phi:F\to\mathbb R^N$ with

$$
xEy\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c(\phi(x))=c(\phi(y)).
$$

Different $E$-classes may receive the same color. Ordinary Ramsey means
$E=F\times F$. For an integer $s\ge1$, **$s$-Ramsey** means that the image
of some such embedding uses at most $s$ colors. The partition in this last
definition may initially depend on the coloring; Proposition 2.4.1 removes
that dependence.

If $F_i$ carries $E_i$, the Euclidean product has squared distance

$$
d((x_1,x_2),(y_1,y_2))^2
=d(x_1,y_1)^2+d(x_2,y_2)^2,
$$

and $(x_1,x_2)(E_1\times E_2)(y_1,y_2)$ means $x_iE_iy_i$ for both
coordinates. Powers $F^m$ and $E^m$ use the corresponding coordinatewise
definitions.

## Group actions and mergers

For a group $G$ acting on a finite set $F$, put

$$
xE_Gy\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
gx=y\text{ for some }g\in G.
$$

A permutation $b$ **respects** $E$ if $xEy$ implies $b(x)E b(y)$.
Because $b$ has finite order, its inverse also respects $E$, so it acts on
$F/E$. If every element of $G$ respects $E$, define

$$
\operatorname{St}_G(E)=\{g\in G:gxEx\text{ for every }x\in F\},
\qquad G/E=G/\operatorname{St}_G(E).
$$

The stabilizer here is the kernel of the action on $F/E$, not the
stabilizer of one point. The group $G/E$ is the induced permutation group
of the equivalence classes.

Write $U(E;G)$ for the equivalence relation that joins all $E$-classes in
each orbit of this quotient action. Explicitly,

$$
x\,U(E;G)\,y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
gxEy\text{ for some }g\in G. \tag{1}
$$

This is an equivalence relation because it is orbit equivalence on $F/E$,
pulled back to $F$.

For $z\in F$, a permutation $b$ respecting $E$, and an integer $n\ge0$,
put

$$
A_n=\bigcup_{i=0}^{n-1}[b^iz]_E,
\qquad U(E;z,b,n)=E\cup(A_n\times A_n). \tag{2}
$$

Thus exactly the classes meeting $z,bz,\ldots,b^{n-1}z$ are merged.
For $n=0$ or $1$ the relation is $E$. The merger need not be preserved by
$b$ until an entire orbit of classes has been merged.

## Source conventions

The printed norm convention on p. 900 writes $\|x\|=x\cdot x$. We use
the ordinary Euclidean convention $\|x\|^2=x\cdot x$, consistent with
the distance and scalar-product arguments throughout the paper. All
inequalities defining $s$-Ramsey and the two-class results are inclusive:
at most $s$ colors, at most two classes, or at most two orbits.
