## Statement

**Theorem 1** (p. 458, quoted). "Let $x$, $y$, $z$ and $w$ be four distinct
points lying on a circle such that

$$
w=z+\alpha(x-z)+\beta(y-z),
$$

where $\alpha\ne1$ and $\beta$ is transcendental over $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$.
Then $xyzw$ does not embed into a transitive set."

Here a set is transitive when its isometry group acts transitively on it
(p. 457), and the transitive sets meant are finite ones in a Euclidean space of
any dimension: the abstract states the aim "in any dimension", and the proof
works in the finite symmetry group of the transitive set. The numbers
$\alpha,\beta$ are the quadrilateral's parameters in the sense of
[[discrete_geometry/leader_2011_transitive_sets_cyclic_quadrilaterals/parameter_projection|the parameter page]];
since three distinct points of a circle are not collinear, they are unique.

**Remark after the theorem** (p. 458, unlabeled). The paper states that the
condition $\alpha\ne1$ is necessary: for every $\beta$ there is a trapezium with
parameters $1$ and $\beta$ that embeds in a transitive set with symmetry group
$D_8$. It also notes that the cyclic hypothesis is redundant, since every
quadrilateral embedding in a transitive set is cyclic.

**Read depth.** Claims checked: the statement, its hypotheses, the remark, the
label and the page were read clause by clause against the print. The proof
sketch below was checked against the printed proof and the cited lemma.

## Proof sketch

Pages 460--461. Suppose the quadrilateral sits in a finite transitive set $T$.
Put the centroid of $T$ at the origin, so that the symmetries of $T$ act as a
finite orthogonal group, and pick symmetries sending $w$ to $x$, $y$, $z$; let
$H$ be the group they generate. The parameter relation becomes a linear relation
among $w$ and its three images. The parameter $\alpha$ is not $0$, since
$\alpha=0$ would put three distinct points of the circle on a line. Splitting
the space into irreducible $H$-summands preserves the relation, and some summand
carries a nontrivial projected quadrilateral; that summand's representation is
nontrivial. On it,
[[discrete_geometry/leader_2011_transitive_sets_cyclic_quadrilaterals/lemma_4|Lemma 4]]
gives a polynomial with algebraic coefficients that vanishes at
$(\alpha,\beta)$ and is not identically zero in its second variable. So $\beta$
is algebraic over the field generated by $\alpha$ and the algebraic numbers,
hence over $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$, against the hypothesis.

Two steps the print leaves brief are supplied here. The restricted symmetries
form a finite group because a linear isometry fixing a spanning set pointwise is
the identity, so they inject into the permutations of $T$. And algebraicity passes
down to $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$: the finitely many algebraic coefficients of the
polynomial generate a finite extension $K$ of $\mathbb Q$, so $\beta$ is
algebraic over $K(\alpha)$, which is algebraic over $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$.

## Bears on

- [[../wiki/problems/discrete_geometry/E0174/_index|Problem 174]]: the problem
  asks for a characterization of the Ramsey sets. Theorem 1 gives a sufficient
  condition for a cyclic quadrilateral, which is a spherical set, not to embed
  in any finite transitive set. It says nothing about whether such a
  quadrilateral is Ramsey. Together with
  [[discrete_geometry/leader_2011_transitive_sets_cyclic_quadrilaterals/corollary_2|Corollary 2]],
  which exhibits quadrilaterals meeting the condition, it separates the
  spherical sets from the subtransitive ones, the two classes that the
  conjectures of Graham and of Leader, Russell and Walters propose as the
  Ramsey sets.
