**Source.** Shaw, arXiv:2608.19183v1,
Section 5, p. 8.
The source says that its proof can be modified to give a divisor
normal form. It does not print that modification. The proof below is
a complete compilation expansion of that statement.

For every integer $q\ge2$ and positive integer $k$, there is a
positive integer $n$ such that every coloring of
$q^{-q/2}C_q^n$ has a congruent copy of $C_q^k$ whose coloring,
in cyclic labels, has the following form for some divisor $d$ of $q$:
$$
 a\text{ and }b\text{ have the same color}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 a_i\equiv b_i\pmod d\ \text{for every }i\in[k]. \tag{1}
$$
The divisor can depend on the coloring. The possibilities $d=1$ and
$d=q$ mean monochromatic and rainbow, respectively.

**Complete proof.** Set $L=\max\{qk+1,4\}$ and $n=F_q(L)$
from [[discrete_geometry/shaw_2026_regular_pentagon_canonically_ramsey/lemma_5|Lemma 5]].
As in the main theorem, the colored host has an invariant copy of
$q^{-1/2}C_q^L$, with local relations $E_m$. Its standard
emulation $\sigma_{k,L}$ is a congruent $C_q^k$. Let $F$ be the
induced equivalence relation on the labels $[q]^k$ of this copy.

Let $D\subseteq\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z$ contain every difference
$b_i-a_i$ for which $a\,F\,b$ and $i\in[k]$, and let $H$ be
the subgroup generated by $D$. For each $\delta\in D$, choose
such a pair and coordinate. Delete the constant padding, then use
[[discrete_geometry/shaw_2026_regular_pentagon_canonically_ramsey/lemma_6|Lemma 6]]
at each position of the corresponding cyclic block. This proves
$$
 t(t+\delta)\,E_2\,(t+\delta)t
 \quad(t\in\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z). \tag{2}
$$
The relation $uRv$ defined by $uv\,E_2\,vu$ is an equivalence
relation by [[discrete_geometry/shaw_2026_regular_pentagon_canonically_ramsey/lemma_7|Lemma 7]].
Equation (2), its symmetry, and transitivity along successive
translated steps show
$$
 v-u\in H\quad\Longrightarrow\quad uRv. \tag{3}
$$
Indeed, every element of $H$ is a finite sum of elements of $D$
and their negatives, and (2) is available at every starting letter.

By the definition of $D$,
$$
 a\,F\,b\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b_i-a_i\in H
 \ \text{for all }i. \tag{4}
$$
For the reverse implication, suppose all these differences lie in
$H$. In the $i$th block of $\sigma_k(a)$ and $\sigma_k(b)$,
the letters occupying each fixed position differ by $b_i-a_i\in H$.
Both blocks contain every letter exactly once. For each coset of
$H$, the positions occupied by its letters therefore agree in the
two blocks. Permute the letters within each such set of positions
to turn the first block into the second.

Every permutation of a finite set is a product of transpositions,
as one can place the correct entry at each position in succession.
Here each required transposition exchanges two letters in the same
coset, so (3) says that their two-letter words are $E_2$-equivalent.
Fixing all other positions pulls $E_L$ back to $E_2$, even when
the two chosen positions are not adjacent. Thus each transposition
preserves the full word's color. Apply these finite permutations
to every block and leave the padding fixed. It follows that
$\sigma_{k,L}(a)\,E_L\,\sigma_{k,L}(b)$, or $a\,F\,b$.
Together with (4), this proves that $F$ is coordinatewise equality
modulo $H$.

To identify $H$, take its inverse image $\widetilde H$ under
$\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z$. This is an additive subgroup
of $\mathbb Z$ containing $q\mathbb Z$. Let $d$ be its least
positive element, which exists because $q\in\widetilde H$.
Division by $d$ shows that every element of $\widetilde H$ is a
multiple of $d$: the remainder also belongs to the subgroup and
cannot be a smaller positive element. Hence
$\widetilde H=d\mathbb Z$ and $d\mid q$.
Membership of a difference in $H$ is exactly divisibility of its
integer representatives by $d$, proving (1). $\square$

The normal form concerns one suitably chosen copy in an arbitrary
coloring. It does not say that every coloring of the ambient power
already has that form. The distinct
[[discrete_geometry/shaw_2026_regular_pentagon_canonically_ramsey/composite_obstruction|composite obstruction]]
constructs a particular coloring that has a stable form on every
scaled copy, and requires additional geometry.

**Bears on.** [[../wiki/problems/discrete_geometry/E0174/_index|Problem 174]],
as context only. The problem asks for a characterisation of the Ramsey
sets, where the number of colours is fixed before the host is chosen. This page concerns
the distinct canonical Ramsey property, one finite host for colourings
with any number of colours, and shows no set Ramsey or non-Ramsey; the
regular polygons are already Ramsey by Kříž's theorem, as the paper
recalls (p. 2).
