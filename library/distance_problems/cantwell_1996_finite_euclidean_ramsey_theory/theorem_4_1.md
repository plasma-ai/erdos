## Statement

Setting (p. 279). The chromatic number of $n$-space is the least positive
integer $t$ such that $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be partitioned into $t$ subsets, no
subset containing two points at unit distance; any fixed distance $b>0$ may
replace unit distance.

**Theorem 4.1** (p. 281, quoted). "The chromatic number of 5-space is at
least 9."

The weaker bound, Theorem 4.2 (p. 281, credited to Kříž), states that the
chromatic number of $\mathbb{R}^5$ is at least 8; the paper reproves it
because its argument is the base of the new one. In the abstract's notation
Theorem 4.1 is $R(K,5,8)$ for $K$ a pair of points at unit distance.

## Proof pointer

Pp. 281--283. The proof of Theorem 4.2, Lemmas 4.5 and 4.6 and Corollaries
4.7 to 4.9 forbid distance $\sqrt2$; the proof of Theorem 4.1 opens by
assuming no two points of one color at unit distance and then argues with
distances $\sqrt2$ and $2\sqrt2$. The half cube is the set of the 16 points
of $\{0,1\}^5$ with an even number of coordinates equal to 1; its distances
are $2$ and $\sqrt2$, and in an 8-coloring with no monochromatic pair at
distance $\sqrt2$ it has exactly two points of each color (proof of Theorem
4.2, pp. 281--282). Definitions 4.3 and 4.4 (p. 282) introduce the half cube
sum and its translated copies of the half cube. Lemmas 4.5 and 4.6 and
Corollaries 4.7 to 4.9 (pp. 282--283) derive that two points of one color
cannot be $2\sqrt2$ apart and that no color holds three of the ten points
with two coordinates equal to $2$ and the rest $0$. The proof of Theorem 4.1
(p. 283) then counts colors over the origin, the five points with four
coordinates $1$ and one $2$, and those ten points, and reaches a
contradiction for an 8-coloring.
