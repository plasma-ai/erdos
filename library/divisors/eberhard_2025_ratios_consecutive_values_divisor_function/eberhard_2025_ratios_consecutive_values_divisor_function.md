#### ABSTRACT

We show that the sequence of ratios $d(n + 1)/d(n)$ of consecutive values of the divisor function attains every positive rational infinitely many times. This confirms a prediction of Erdős.

Erdős conjectured that the sequence of ratios $d(n + 1)/d(n)$ of consecutive values of the divisor function is “everywhere dense in $(0,\infty)$” [Erd86]. In fact it follows easily from the generalized prime $k$-tuple conjecture that for every rational $x > 0$ there are infinitely many solutions to $d(n + 1)/d(n) = x$. We prove this unconditionally by building on the idea of Hasanalizade [Has21] (see also Schlage-Puchta [Sch25]), based on the following sieve theory result of Goldston, Graham, Pintz, and Yıldırım.

**Theorem 1** (*[GGPY11, Corollary 2.1, special case $b_1 = b_2 = b_3 = 1$]*). *Let $a_1, a_2, a_3, r_1, r_2, r_3$ be positive integers with $(r_i, a_i) = (r_i, a_i - a_j) = (r_i, r_j) = 1$ for all $i \ne j$. Let $L_i(x) = a_i x + 1$. Let $C$ be any positive integer. Then there are indices*

$i, j$ with $1 \leq i < j \leq 3$ such that there are infinitely many positive integers $x$ with both $L_i(x)/r_i, L_j(x)/r_j \in E_2(C)$, where $E_2(C)$ denote the set of products $p_1p_2$ where $p_1$ and $p_2$ are distinct primes and $p_1,p_2 > C$.

Set $(a_1,a_2,a_3)=(a,a+1,a+2)$, where $a$ is even. Then

$$
a_2L_1-a_1L_2=a_3L_2-a_2L_3=\frac{1}{2}a_3L_1-\frac{1}{2}a_1L_3=1.
$$

Let $r_1,r_2,r_3$ be pairwise coprime odd positive integers with $(r_i,a_i)=1$ for each $i=1,2,3$ and let $C$ be the largest prime factor of $a_1a_2a_3r_1r_2r_3$. If $x$ is an integer such that $L_1(x)/r_1,L_2(x)/r_2 \in E_2(C)$ then $n=a_1L_2(x)$ satisfies

$$
\frac{d(n+1)}{d(n)}=\frac{d(a_2L_1(x))}{d(a_1L_2(x))}=\frac{d(a_2r_1)}{d(a_1r_2)}.
$$

Similarly, if $L_2(x)/r_2,L_3(x)/r_3 \in E_2(C)$ then $n=a_2L_3(x)$ satisfies

$$
\frac{d(n+1)}{d(n)}=\frac{d(a_3L_2(x))}{d(a_2L_3(x))}=\frac{d(a_3r_2)}{d(a_2r_3)},
$$

while if $L_1(x)/r_1,L_3(x)/r_3 \in E_2(C)$ then $n=\frac{1}{2}a_1L_3(x)$ satisfies

$$
\frac{d(n+1)}{d(n)}=\frac{d(\frac{1}{2}a_3L_1(x))}{d(\frac{1}{2}a_1L_3(x))}=\frac{d(\frac{1}{2}a_3r_1)}{d(\frac{1}{2}a_1r_3)}.
$$

Thus, if $R$ denotes the set of values attained infinitely many times by the sequence $d(n+1)/d(n)$, then Theorem 1 implies that $R$ contains at least one of the three values

$$
\frac{d(a_2r_1)}{d(a_1r_2)}, \qquad \frac{d(a_3r_2)}{d(a_2r_3)}, \qquad \frac{d(\frac{1}{2}a_3r_1)}{d(\frac{1}{2}a_1r_3)}.
$$

Let $p_1,p_2,p_3$ be distinct primes such that $(a_1a_2a_3r_1r_2r_3,p_1p_2p_3)=1$ and apply the fact just stated with $r_i'=r_ip_i^{e_i-1}$ in place of $r_i$, where $e_1,e_2,e_3>0$ are arbitrary positive integers. We deduce that $R$ contains one of

$$
\frac{d(a_2r_1)e_1}{d(a_1r_2)e_2}, \qquad \frac{d(a_3r_2)e_2}{d(a_2r_3)e_3}, \qquad \frac{d(\frac{1}{2}a_3r_1)e_1}{d(\frac{1}{2}a_1r_3)e_3}.
$$

We can make the three expression above equal by setting

$$
\begin{aligned}
e_1 &= d(a_2r_1)d(a_3r_2)d(\frac{1}{2}a_1r_3)^2,\\
e_2 &= d(a_2r_1)d(a_2r_3)d(\frac{1}{2}a_3r_1)d(\frac{1}{2}a_1r_3),\\
e_3 &= d(a_1r_2)d(a_2r_3)d(\frac{1}{2}a_3r_1)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

It follows that

$$
\frac{d(a_2r_1)d(a_3r_2)d\left(\frac{1}{2}a_1r_3\right)}{d(a_1r_2)d(a_2r_3)d\left(\frac{1}{2}a_3r_1\right)}\in R.
$$

Let $p_1,\ldots,p_k,q_1,\ldots,q_l$ be distinct odd primes. Let $x_i,y_i>0$ ($1\leq i\leq k$) and $u_i,v_i>0$ ($1\leq i\leq l$) be positive integers and let

$$
a=4p_1^{x_1}\cdots p_k^{x_k}q_1^{u_1}\cdots q_l^{u_l},\qquad (r_1,r_2,r_3)=(1,p_1^{y_1}\cdots p_k^{y_k},q_1^{v_1}\cdots q_l^{v_l}).
$$

Then $(r_i,a_i)=(r_i,2)=(r_i,r_j)=1$ for $i\ne j$, as required. Thus $R$ contains

$$
\frac{d(a_2r_1)d(a_3r_2)d\left(\frac{1}{2}a_1r_3\right)}{d(a_1r_2)d(a_2r_3)d\left(\frac{1}{2}a_3r_1\right)}
=\frac{4}{3}\prod_{i=1}^k\frac{(x_i+1)(y_i+1)}{x_i+y_i+1}\prod_{i=1}^l\frac{u_i+v_i+1}{(u_i+1)(v_i+1)}.
$$

Let $f(x,y)=(x+1)(y+1)/(x+y+1)$. Noting that $4/3=f(1,1)$, we deduce that $R$ contains the subgroup $G$ of $\mathbf{Q}_{>0}^{\times}$ generated by $\{f(x,y):x,y\geq1\}$.

We claim that $G=\mathbf{Q}_{>0}^{\times}$. It suffices to prove that $G$ contains every prime number $p$, which we prove by induction on $p$. First, $2=f(2,3)\in G$. Next, if $p=2x+1>2$ then, since $x+1$ is a product of primes smaller than $p$, we have $p=(x+1)^2/f(x,x)\in G$ by induction. This completes the proof.
