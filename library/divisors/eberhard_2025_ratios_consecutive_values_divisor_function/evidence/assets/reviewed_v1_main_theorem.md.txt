**Source.** Sean Eberhard, *Ratios of consecutive values of the divisor
function*, Journal of Number Theory 281 (2026), 426--428, pp. 426--428
(PDF pp. 1--3), DOI
<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnt.2025.10.002>. The proof below is a
complete rewrite of the published argument. Its only external input is the
quoted sieve result recorded as [[library/divisors/eberhard_2025_ratios_consecutive_values_divisor_function/theorem_1|Theorem 1]].

## Statement

Let $d(n)=\tau(n)$ be the number of positive divisors of $n$. For every
$q\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$, there are infinitely many positive integers $n$ such
that

$$
\frac{d(n+1)}{d(n)}=q.
$$

In particular, the ratios $d(n+1)/d(n)$ are everywhere dense in
$(0,\infty)$.

## Proof

Let $R$ be the set of positive rational values that occur infinitely often
as $d(n+1)/d(n)$. We show that $R$ contains every positive rational.

### Three reductions from the sieve input

Take an even positive integer $a$ and set

$$
a_1=a,\qquad a_2=a+1,\qquad a_3=a+2,
$$

and $L_i(x)=a_i x+1$. The identities

$$
a_2L_1(x)-a_1L_2(x)=1,
\qquad
a_3L_2(x)-a_2L_3(x)=1,
$$

and

$$
\frac{a_3}{2}L_1(x)-\frac{a_1}{2}L_3(x)=1
$$

hold for every $x$. Let $r_1,r_2,r_3$ be pairwise coprime odd positive
integers with $(r_i,a_i)=1$, and let $C$ be the largest prime factor of
$a_1a_2a_3r_1r_2r_3$. Theorem 1 supplies infinitely many $x$ for at least
one of the three pairs $(1,2)$, $(2,3)$, and $(1,3)$, with both members of
that pair satisfying $L_i(x)/r_i\in E_2(C)$.

Here $E_2(C)$ consists of products of two distinct primes, both larger than
$C$. Hence each such product is coprime to every fixed multiplier occurring
below, and it has four divisors.

For the pair $(1,2)$, put $n=a_1L_2(x)$. The first identity gives
$n+1=a_2L_1(x)$, and multiplicativity of $d$ gives

$$
\frac{d(n+1)}{d(n)}
=\frac{d(a_2L_1(x))}{d(a_1L_2(x))}
=\frac{d(a_2r_1)}{d(a_1r_2)}.
$$

For $(2,3)$, put $n=a_2L_3(x)$. The second identity similarly gives

$$
\frac{d(n+1)}{d(n)}
=\frac{d(a_3r_2)}{d(a_2r_3)}.
$$

For $(1,3)$, put $n=(a_1/2)L_3(x)$. This is an integer because $a_1$
is even, and the third identity gives

$$
\frac{d(n+1)}{d(n)}
=\frac{d((a_3/2)r_1)}{d((a_1/2)r_3)}.
$$

Since the applicable pair occurs for infinitely many $x$, at least one of

$$
\frac{d(a_2r_1)}{d(a_1r_2)},\qquad
\frac{d(a_3r_2)}{d(a_2r_3)},\qquad
\frac{d((a_3/2)r_1)}{d((a_1/2)r_3)}
$$

belongs to $R$.

### Equalizing the three candidates

For readability put

$$
A=d(a_2r_1),\quad B=d(a_1r_2),\quad C_0=d(a_3r_2),
$$

$$
D=d(a_2r_3),\quad E=d((a_3/2)r_1),\quad
F=d((a_1/2)r_3).
$$

Choose distinct primes $\pi_1,\pi_2,\pi_3$ coprime to
$a_1a_2a_3r_1r_2r_3$. They are odd because that product is even. Replace
$r_i$ by $r_i\pi_i^{e_i-1}$, where $e_i$ are arbitrary positive integers.
The hypotheses of Theorem 1 remain true: the new primes are distinct,
avoid the coefficients and the old $r_i$, and the only nontrivial
coefficient differences here are $1$ and $2$.

Applying the preceding conclusion to the new $r_i$ shows that $R$ contains
one of

$$
\frac{A e_1}{B e_2},\qquad
\frac{C_0 e_2}{D e_3},\qquad
\frac{E e_1}{F e_3}.
$$

Indeed, adjoining $\pi_i^{e_i-1}$ multiplies the relevant divisor count by
$e_i$. Take

$$
e_1=A C_0 F^2,\qquad
e_2=A D E F,\qquad
e_3=B D E^2.
$$

All three displayed values are then equal to

$$
\frac{A C_0 F}{B D E}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\frac{d(a_2r_1)d(a_3r_2)d((a_1/2)r_3)}
     {d(a_1r_2)d(a_2r_3)d((a_3/2)r_1)}\in R.
\tag{1}
$$

### Producing a subgroup of rational values

Let $k,\ell\geq0$, let
$p_1,\ldots,p_k,q_1,\ldots,q_\ell$ be distinct odd primes, and let
$x_i,y_i,u_i,v_i$ be positive integers for the displayed ranges. Empty
products are understood to be $1$. Set

$$
a=4\prod_{i=1}^k p_i^{x_i}
       \prod_{i=1}^{\ell}q_i^{u_i},
\qquad
(r_1,r_2,r_3)=\left(1,\prod_{i=1}^k p_i^{y_i},
                         \prod_{i=1}^{\ell}q_i^{v_i}\right).
$$

The $r_i$ are odd and pairwise coprime. Also each $r_i$ is coprime to
$a_i$ and to the differences $a_i-a_j$, which are $\pm1$ or $\pm2$.
Thus (1) applies. The prime factorization formula
$d(\prod p^{m_p})=\prod(m_p+1)$, together with cancellation of the
$a+1$ and $(a+2)/2$ factors, gives

$$
\frac{d(a_2r_1)d(a_3r_2)d((a_1/2)r_3)}
     {d(a_1r_2)d(a_2r_3)d((a_3/2)r_1)}
=\frac{4}{3}
  \prod_{i=1}^k
  \frac{(x_i+1)(y_i+1)}{x_i+y_i+1}
  \prod_{i=1}^{\ell}
  \frac{u_i+v_i+1}{(u_i+1)(v_i+1)}.
\tag{2}
$$

Define

$$
f(x,y)=\frac{(x+1)(y+1)}{x+y+1},
\qquad x,y\geq1.
$$

Since $4/3=f(1,1)$, formula (2), with the $p$-blocks realizing positive
powers and the $q$-blocks realizing inverse powers, shows directly that $R$
contains the multiplicative subgroup $G$ of $\mathbb Q_{>0}$ generated by
all $f(x,y)$. Indeed, given any finite signed word in these generators, put
positive exponents in $p$-blocks and negative exponents in $q$-blocks; adjust
the exponent of $f(1,1)$ by one to absorb the fixed factor $4/3$.

### The subgroup is all positive rationals

We prove by induction on primes that every prime belongs to $G$. First,

$$
2=f(2,3)\in G.
$$

If $p>2$ is prime, write $p=2x+1$. Every prime factor of $x+1$ is smaller
than $p$, so the induction hypothesis gives $x+1\in G$. Since

$$
f(x,x)=\frac{(x+1)^2}{2x+1}=\frac{(x+1)^2}{p},
$$

we get $p=(x+1)^2/f(x,x)\in G$. Thus $G$ contains every prime and hence
all of $\mathbb Q_{>0}$. Consequently $R=\mathbb Q_{>0}$, proving the
statement.

**Proof coverage.** The reductions, equalization, divisor-count calculation,
and prime induction above rewrite the complete argument on Eberhard's
published pp. 426--428 (PDF pp. 1--3). The proof of the external GGPY sieve
statement is not recopied; its exact hypotheses and application to the three
linear forms are recorded in [[library/divisors/eberhard_2025_ratios_consecutive_values_divisor_function/theorem_1|Theorem 1]].
