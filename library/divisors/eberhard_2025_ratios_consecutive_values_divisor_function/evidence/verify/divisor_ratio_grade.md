## 1. (a) My own derivation, formed before reading the report

### 1.1 The theorem and E0964's exact question

`main_theorem.md` states: with $d(n)=\tau(n)$ the number of positive
divisors, **for every $q\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}$ there are infinitely many
positive integers $n$ with $d(n+1)/d(n)=q$**, and in particular the ratios
are everywhere dense in $(0,\infty)$. The quantifier order is
$\forall q\,\exists^{\infty} n$. Nothing is asymptotic, averaged, or
almost-all; there is no exceptional set and no condition on $q$.

`E0964.md` lines 16-23 asks: *is the sequence $\tau(n+1)/\tau(n)$ everywhere
dense in $(0,\infty)$?* Frontmatter `status: solved`.

**Implication.** Let $V=\{d(n+1)/d(n):n\ge1\}$. Every element of $V$ is a
positive rational, and the theorem gives $\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\subseteq V$, so
$V=\mathbb{Q}_{>0}$, which is dense in $(0,\infty)$. The answer is yes. The
theorem is strictly stronger than the question needs; the surplus
("infinitely often") is exactly what is needed if "dense" is read of the
sequence's accumulation values rather than of its value set, so the
"In particular" sentence is safe under either reading. Valid and complete.

**Later correction, attributed to the commissioning role.**
One attainment of every positive rational already suffices for dense tails:
each nonempty positive open interval contains infinitely many distinct
rationals, hence infinitely many distinct attaining indices. Removing a
finite prefix leaves every interval hit, and increasing indices in shrinking
neighborhoods give a subsequence converging to every positive real. Exact
infinite repetition is stronger arithmetic content, not necessary for either
density conclusion. The original necessity claim above is retained as the
historical grader's wording, not endorsed. See the separately attributed
[commentary correction](divisor_ratio_commentary_correction.md).

### 1.2 The three shifted values and the identities

Take $a$ even positive, $a_1=a$, $a_2=a+1$, $a_3=a+2$, $L_i(x)=a_ix+1$.
The $x$ terms cancel identically in each of

- $a_2L_1-a_1L_2=a_2-a_1=1$;
- $a_3L_2-a_2L_3=a_3-a_2=1$;
- $\tfrac{a_3}{2}L_1-\tfrac{a_1}{2}L_3=\tfrac{a_3-a_1}{2}=1$;

so all three hold for every $x$. $a_1/2$ and $a_3/2$ are integers because
$a$ is even. **Confirmed.**

### 1.3 Sieve hypotheses, admissibility, coprimality

`theorem_1.md` requires $(r_i,a_i)=(r_i,a_i-a_j)=(r_i,r_j)=1$ for all
$i\ne j$, with $L_i(x)=a_ix+1$ and $C$ any positive integer. With
$r_1,r_2,r_3$ pairwise coprime odd positive integers satisfying
$(r_i,a_i)=1$: pairwise coprimality is assumed; $(r_i,a_i)=1$ is assumed;
the six differences are $\pm1,\pm1,\pm2$, so coprimality with them follows
from oddness alone — this is the only place oddness is used. $C$ exists:
$a_1a_2a_3\ge 2\cdot3\cdot4=24$. **All hypotheses met.**

Admissibility is *not* a hypothesis of the statement as recorded. The
page's own check ($L_i(0)=1$ for all $i$, so no prime divides
$L_1L_2L_3$ at $x=0$) is correct but not load-bearing. Its accompanying
claims about GGPY's general notation are a separate problem — §3, D1.

### 1.4 The three divisor-function computations

$E_2(C)$ elements are $p_1p_2$ with $p_1\ne p_2$ both $>C$: they have
$d=4$, and since every fixed multiplier ($a_2r_1$, $a_1r_2$, $a_3r_2$,
$a_2r_3$, $(a_3/2)r_1$, $(a_1/2)r_3$) divides $a_1a_2a_3r_1r_2r_3$ whose
largest prime factor is $C$, they are coprime to all of them
($a_3/2\mid a_3$, $a_1/2\mid a_1$). Membership in $E_2(C)$ already asserts
$r_i\mid L_i(x)$.

- Pair $(1,2)$: $n=a_1L_2(x)$, so $n+1=a_2L_1(x)$;
  $d(n)=4d(a_1r_2)$, $d(n+1)=4d(a_2r_1)$, ratio $d(a_2r_1)/d(a_1r_2)$.
- Pair $(2,3)$: $n=a_2L_3(x)$, $n+1=a_3L_2(x)$, ratio
  $d(a_3r_2)/d(a_2r_3)$.
- Pair $(1,3)$: $n=(a_1/2)L_3(x)$ (integer), $n+1=(a_3/2)L_1(x)$, ratio
  $d((a_3/2)r_1)/d((a_1/2)r_3)$.

**All three confirmed.** $n>0$ for positive $x$, and $n$ is a strictly
increasing affine function of $x$ in each case (e.g. $n=a_1a_2x+a_1$), so
infinitely many $x$ give infinitely many distinct $n$ — hence the value
lies in $R$. Neither page states the injectivity clause; neither does the
source; it is immediate.

### 1.5 Equalization

$A=d(a_2r_1)$, $B=d(a_1r_2)$, $C_0=d(a_3r_2)$, $D=d(a_2r_3)$,
$E=d((a_3/2)r_1)$, $F=d((a_1/2)r_3)$, all positive integers. Choose
distinct primes $\pi_1,\pi_2,\pi_3$ coprime to $a_1a_2a_3r_1r_2r_3$; they
are odd because that product is even. With $r_i'=r_i\pi_i^{e_i-1}$ every
hypothesis survives ($r_i'$ odd, pairwise coprime, coprime to $a_i$ and to
$\pm1,\pm2$), and $C$ is recomputed for the new $r_i'$ because the whole
preceding paragraph, including the definition of $C$, is re-applied.
Since $\pi_i$ divides none of the fixed multipliers,
$d(\pi_i^{e_i-1})=e_i$ multiplies each count by $e_i$, giving candidates
$Ae_1/(Be_2)$, $C_0e_2/(De_3)$, $Ee_1/(Fe_3)$.

With $e_1=AC_0F^2$, $e_2=ADEF$, $e_3=BDE^2$:

- $Ae_1/(Be_2)=A^2C_0F^2/(ABDEF)=AC_0F/(BDE)$;
- $C_0e_2/(De_3)=AC_0DEF/(BD^2E^2)=AC_0F/(BDE)$;
- $Ee_1/(Fe_3)=AC_0EF^2/(BDE^2F)=AC_0F/(BDE)$.

**All three equal; equation (1) follows.** The pair the sieve supplies is
not under the author's control, which is exactly why this step exists.

### 1.6 Formula (2), derived independently

With $a=4PU$, $P=\prod_1^k p_i^{x_i}$, $U=\prod_1^{\ell}q_i^{u_i}$
(distinct odd primes), $(r_1,r_2,r_3)=(1,\ P'=\prod p_i^{y_i},\
Q'=\prod q_i^{v_i})$:

$r_i$ odd, pairwise coprime; $(r_1,a_1)=1$; $p_i\mid a$ so $p_i\nmid a+1$;
$q_i\mid a$ and $q_i$ odd so $q_i\nmid a+2$. Hypotheses hold, (1) applies.

- $A=d(a+1)$
- $B=d(2^2\prod p_i^{x_i+y_i}\prod q_i^{u_i})=3\prod(x_i+y_i+1)\prod(u_i+1)$
- $C_0=d(a+2)\prod(y_i+1)$
- $D=d(a+1)\prod(v_i+1)$
- $E=d((a+2)/2)$
- $F=d(2\prod p_i^{x_i}\prod q_i^{u_i+v_i})=2\prod(x_i+1)\prod(u_i+v_i+1)$

Because $4\mid a$, $(a+2)/2=2PU+1$ is odd, so $d(a+2)=2\,d((a+2)/2)$.
Hence

$$
\frac{AC_0F}{BDE}=\frac43
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(x_i+1)(y_i+1)}{x_i+y_i+1}
\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{u_i+v_i+1}{(u_i+1)(v_i+1)},
$$

**which is formula (2) exactly.** The constant $4/3$ is
$2\cdot 2/3$: one $2$ from $d(a+2)=2d((a+2)/2)$, one $2$ from
$v_2((a_1/2)r_3)=1$, and the $3$ from $v_2(a_1r_2)=2$.

### 1.8 The word argument and the prime induction

$f(x,y)=(x+1)(y+1)/(x+y+1)$, $x,y\ge1$; $f(1,1)=4/3$. Formula (2)
realizes $\tfrac43\prod f(x_i,y_i)\prod f(u_j,v_j)^{-1}$ for freely chosen
$k,\ell\ge0$, freely chosen distinct odd primes, and freely chosen positive
exponents. Given $g\in G$, write $g\,f(1,1)^{-1}$ as a finite signed word,
put positive occurrences in $p$-blocks and negative ones in $q$-blocks
(repetition is fine — the *primes* must be distinct, not the exponent
pairs, and enough distinct odd primes exist); (2) then evaluates to
$f(1,1)\cdot g\cdot f(1,1)^{-1}=g$. **$R\supseteq G$, and $R$ is never
assumed to be a group.** The empty word gives $1\in R$.

Induction: $f(2,3)=12/6=2\in G$. For prime $p>2$, $x=(p-1)/2\ge1$ and
$x+1=(p+1)/2<p$, so every prime factor of $x+1$ is $<p$; strong induction
and group closure give $x+1\in G$; $f(x,x)=(x+1)^2/(2x+1)=(x+1)^2/p$, so
$p=(x+1)^2/f(x,x)\in G$. Checked for $p=3,5,7$. Not circular, well-founded.
$G$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}_{>0}$ containing every prime, so
$G=\mathbb{Q}_{>0}$ by unique factorization, and $R=\mathbb{Q}_{>0}$.
