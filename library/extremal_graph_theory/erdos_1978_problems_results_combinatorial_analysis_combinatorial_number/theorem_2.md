## Statement

As printed on p. 39, after the Erdős--Ulam problem and the Rado--Sanders
remark, where it is introduced as a more general result showing that $n_k$
grows exponentially:

**Theorem 2.** "Let $|S|=n$. There is a division of the subsets of $S$ into
two classes so that if $A_i\subseteq S$, $1\le i\le k$ are such that all
the $2^k-1$ unions $A_{i_1}\ldots A_{i_r}$ [sic] are distinct and belong
to the same class then $k\le(1+o(1))\log n/\log2$." The print omits the
union signs; the unions meant are $A_{i_1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{i_r}$.

The theorem is stated with the pronoun "we" of the joint work with Ulam
that the section describes ("Ulam and I considered the following
combinatorial problem", p. 39); the introduction of the paper (p. 29) speaks of
"a recent theorem of Selfridge and myself and of Ulam and myself".

What it bounds. The $k$ sets are generators whose nonempty unions are
pairwise distinct (the 2026 manuscript on Problem 1183 calls such a tuple
"free"); the theorem says that under a suitable coloring no monochromatic
free $k$-tuple exists for $k>(1+\varepsilon)\log_2n$. It does not by itself
bound the size $F(n)$ of a monochromatic union-closed family, since such a
family need not be generated by sets with distinct unions; the closing
remark of p. 40 says that no better upper bound was available "even if we
assume that the $A$'s are disjoint, and in neither case has it been possible
to obtain an acceptable lower bound".

**Source.** P. Erdős, Problems and results in combinatorial analysis and
combinatorial number theory, Proc. Ninth Southeastern Conf. on
Combinatorics, Graph Theory, and Computing (1978), 29--40; Theorem 2 on
printed p. 39 (PDF p. 11 of the scan), proof on printed pp. 39--40
(PDF pp. 11--12). The scan's text layer is unreliable; the statement and
proof were read on the rendered page images.

**Read depth.** Claims checked: the statement and the sentences around it
were read clause by clause on the page image. The proof (half
a page) was read in full and its counting followed, not checked line by
line.

## Proof pointer

Pp. 39--40, a count. Leaving out the empty set, there are $2^{2^n-1}$
two-class splittings of the subsets of $S$, and at most
$\binom{2^n}k<2^{kn}$ choices of the $k$ sets. For a choice whose $2^k-1$
unions are distinct, picking the class that receives all of those unions
(2 ways) and splitting the other $2^n-2^k$ nonempty subsets freely gives
$2\cdot2^{2^n-1}\cdot2^{-(2^k-1)}=2^{2^n-2^k+1}$ splittings in which all
its unions share a class. So fewer than $2^{kn}2^{2^n-2^k+1}$ splittings
admit such $k$ sets, and this is below $2^{2^n-1}$ when (2)
$2^{2^k-2}>2^{kn}$, which a short computation shows holds once
$k>(1+o(1))\log n/\log2$. Thus some splitting has no such $k$ sets for
$k>(1+\varepsilon)\log n/\log2$.
