# extremal_graph_theory/fan_2005_path_decompositions_gallai_s_conjecture

[[extremal_graph_theory/_index|..]]

[[extremal_graph_theory/fan_2005_path_decompositions_gallai_s_conjecture/corollary|corollary]]: Fan's block case of Gallai's conjecture: a graph on n vertices, connected
or not, each block of whose even-degree subgraph is a triangle-free graph of
maximum degree at most 3 decomposes into floor(n/2) paths, from the Main
theorem and Proposition 2.6; the row Problem 583's table records for the
paper.

[[extremal_graph_theory/fan_2005_path_decompositions_gallai_s_conjecture/main_theorem|main_theorem]]: Fan's Main theorem: a graph on n vertices, connected or not, whose
even-degree subgraph is an alpha-graph, one built from the empty graph by
adding isolated vertices and vertices joined to independent alpha-pairs,
decomposes into floor(n/2) paths; alpha-graphs include the forests and the
graphs of the Corollary.

## Contents

- Abstract and § 1, Introduction (pp. 117--118). The abstract states
  Gallai's conjecture for a connected simple graph $G$ on $n$ vertices, a
  decomposition of its edges into $\lceil\frac n2\rceil$ paths, writes $H$
  for the subgraph induced by the vertices of even degree, and recalls that
  Lovász proved the conjecture when $H$ has at most one vertex and Pyber
  when $H$ is a forest, that is, when every block of $H$ is a vertex or an
  edge and so has maximum degree at most 1. It then says that the
  conjecture holds when $H$ can be obtained from the empty set by the
  paper's $\alpha$-operations (the Main theorem) and, as a corollary, when
  each block of $H$ is a triangle-free graph of maximum degree at most 3.
  Graphs are finite, undirected and simple; a block is a maximal
  nonseparable subgraph (printed "maximum", p. 117); "The E-subgraph of $G$
  is the subgraph induced by the vertices of even degree in $G$" (p. 118);
  a path-decomposition of $G$ is a set of edge-disjoint paths whose edge
  sets cover $E(G)$, trivial paths (single vertices) allowed, so a
  decomposition into at most $k$ paths can be padded to one into exactly
  $k$ (p. 118). The paper attributes the question, how many paths suffice
  to decompose every connected graph on $n$ vertices, to Erdős, and the
  conjectured answer $\lceil\frac n2\rceil$ to Gallai, citing Lovász [4]
  for both (p. 118). Gallai's conjecture
  (p. 118, quoted): "If $G$ is a connected graph on $n$ vertices, then $G$
  can be decomposed into $\lceil\frac n2\rceil$ paths." The survey
  paragraph (p. 118): Lovász [4] decomposes every graph on $n$ vertices,
  connected or not, into $\lfloor\frac n2\rfloor$ paths and circuits;
  Donald [2] into $\lfloor\frac34n\rfloor$ paths, and Dean and Kouider [1]
  and Yan [7] independently lowered this to $\lfloor\frac23n\rfloor$;
  Lovász's theorem gives $\lfloor\frac n2\rfloor$ paths when at most
  one vertex of $G$ has even degree, that is, when the E-subgraph has at
  most one vertex, and Pyber [6] extended this to every $G$ whose
  E-subgraph is a forest. The paper then states its Corollary in advance,
  $\lfloor\frac n2\rfloor$ paths for a graph on $n$ vertices, connected or
  not, each block of whose E-subgraph is a triangle-free graph of maximum
  degree at most 3, and shows that triangle-freeness cannot be dropped: a
  graph made of $k$ vertex-disjoint triangles has $3k$ vertices and is its
  own E-subgraph, and since a triangle needs at least two paths, any
  path-decomposition of it needs at least $2k=\frac23|V(G)|$ paths (the
  print says "$3k$ vertex-disjoint triangles" but counts $|V(G)|=3k$ and
  $2k$ paths, which fit $k$ triangles; the ratio $\frac23$ holds either
  way). The introduction closes by announcing the Main theorem,
  $\lfloor\frac n2\rfloor$ paths whenever the E-subgraph can be obtained
  from the empty set by $\alpha$-operations, proved with Lovász's path
  sequence technique from [4].
- § 2, $\alpha$-operations and $\alpha$-graphs (pp. 118--120). Definition
  2.1 (p. 118), restated: in a graph $H$, a pair $(S,y)$ with $S$ an
  independent set and $y\in S$ is an $\alpha$-pair when every vertex
  $v\in S\setminus\{y\}$ with $d_H(v)\ge2$ satisfies both (a) $d_H(u)\le3$
  for every $u\in N_H(v)$ and (b) $d_H(u)=3$ for at most two
  $u\in N_H(v)$; the conditions bind only the vertices $v\ne y$ of $S$ of
  degree at least 2. An $\alpha$-operation on $H$ is either (i) the
  addition of an isolated vertex or (ii) the choice of an $\alpha$-pair
  $(S,y)$ and the addition of a new vertex $x$ adjacent to each vertex of
  $S$; in case (ii) the ordered triple $(x,S,y)$ is the $\alpha$-triple of
  the operation. Definition 2.2
  (p. 119, quoted): "An $\alpha$-graph is a graph that can be obtained from
  the empty set via a sequence of $\alpha$-operations." The empty set is an
  $\alpha$-graph; a graph is an $\alpha$-graph iff its vertices have an
  $\alpha$-ordering $x_1x_2\ldots x_n$, each initial segment inducing an
  $\alpha$-graph obtained by an $\alpha$-operation from the previous one;
  "by the definition, an $\alpha$-graph is triangle-free" (p. 119).
  Proposition 2.3: "Any subgraph of an $\alpha$-graph is an $\alpha$-graph"
  (the restriction of an $\alpha$-ordering). Proposition 2.4: "Any
  subdivision of an $\alpha$-graph is an $\alpha$-graph" (insert the new
  vertices of a subdivided edge $xy$ just before $y$ in the ordering).
  Proposition 2.5: "Forests are $\alpha$-graphs" (remove a leaf $x$ with
  neighbor $y$; the $\alpha$-triple is $(x,\{y\},y)$). A circuit of length
  at least $4$ is an $\alpha$-graph, and more generally Proposition 2.6
  (p. 119, quoted): "If each block of $G$ is a triangle-free graph of
  maximum degree at most 3, then $G$ is an $\alpha$-graph." Its proof
  (pp. 119--120), by induction on $|V(G)|$: in an end-block $B$ let $b$ be
  its cut vertex (any vertex if $B=G$), $x$ a neighbor of $b$ in $B$,
  $S=N_G(x)=N_B(x)$ and $H=G-x$; $S$ is independent since $B$ is
  triangle-free, and the degree bounds of Definition 2.1 hold for
  $v\in S\setminus\{b\}$ since $B$ has maximum degree at most $3$, so $G$
  is obtained from $H$ by the $\alpha$-operation with $\alpha$-triple
  $(x,S,b)$.
- § 3, Technical lemmas (pp. 120--123). Definition 3.1: for a
  path-decomposition $\mathcal D$ of $G$, $\mathcal D(v)$ is the number of
  nontrivial paths of $\mathcal D$ with $v$ as an end; an odd-degree vertex
  has $\mathcal D(v)\ge1$. Definition 3.2: for a set $B$ of edges at a
  vertex $a$, $H=G\setminus B$ and a path-decomposition $\mathcal D$ of $H$,
  a set $A=\{ax_i:1\le i\le k\}\subseteq B$ is called addible at $a$
  (relative to $\mathcal D$) when some path-decomposition $\mathcal D^*$
  of $H\cup A$ has $|\mathcal D^*|=|\mathcal D|$,
  $\mathcal D^*(a)=\mathcal D(a)+|A|$, $\mathcal D^*(x_i)=\mathcal D(x_i)-1$
  for each $i$ and $\mathcal D^*(v)=\mathcal D(v)$ at every other vertex;
  such a $\mathcal D^*$ is what the paper calls a transformation of
  $\mathcal D$ obtained by adding $A$ at $a$. Lemma
  3.3 (p. 120), for $H=G\setminus\{ax_1,\ldots,ax_s\}$: either some $ax_i$
  is addible at $a$, or
  $\sum_{i=1}^s\mathcal D(x_i)\le|\{v\in N_H(a):\mathcal D(v)=0\}|$; its
  proof (pp. 120--121) is Lovász's path sequence technique, following from
  each pair (end $x_i$, path $P$) the chain of paths through $a$ and showing
  the chains that end at a vertex $b_t$ with $\mathcal D(b_t)=0$ end at
  distinct vertices. Lemma 3.4 is the case $s=1$: if
  $\mathcal D(b)>|\{v\in N_H(a):\mathcal D(v)=0\}|$ then $ab$ is addible at
  $a$. Lemma 3.5 (pp. 121--122): if $\mathcal D(x_i)\ge1$ for all $i$, some
  $A\subseteq\{ax_1,\ldots,ax_s\}$ with $|A|\ge\lceil\frac{s-r}2\rceil$ is
  addible at $a$, $r=|\{v\in N_H(a):\mathcal D(v)=0\}|$. Lemma 3.6
  (p. 122): if $\mathcal D(v)\ge1$ for all $v\in N_G(a)$, then for any
  $x\in\{x_1,\ldots,x_h\}$ some $B\ni ax$ with $|B|\ge\lceil\frac h2\rceil$
  is addible at $a$. Lemma 3.7 (p. 122): for
  $H=G\setminus\{bx_1,\ldots,bx_k\}$, if
  $|\{v\in N_H(x_i):\mathcal D(v)=0\}|\le m$ for each $i$ and
  $\mathcal D(b)\ge k+m$, then $G$ has a path-decomposition of size
  $|\mathcal D|$ (adding the edges one at a time at the $x_i$ by Lemma 3.4).
  The paper notes (p. 120) that Lemmas 3.3 and 3.5 are special cases of
  Lemmas 4.3 and 4.6 of its [3], the 2002 covering paper, "whose proofs are
  rather complicated", and proves them afresh.
- § 4, Main theorem (pp. 123--125). Lemma 4.1 (p. 123, quoted): "Let $F$ be
  the E-subgraph of a graph $G$. For $a\in V(F)$ and
  $\{x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_s\}\subseteq N_F(a)$, where $s$ is odd and
  $d_F(x_i)\le3$, $2\le i\le s$, if $G\setminus\{ax_1,ax_2,\ldots,ax_s\}$
  has a path decomposition $\mathcal D$ such that $\mathcal D(v)\ge1$ for
  all $v\in N_G(a)\cup\{a\}$, then $G$ has a path decomposition
  $\mathcal D^*$ with $|\mathcal D^*|=|\mathcal D|$." Its proof adds
  $t\ge\lceil\frac s2\rceil=k+1$ of the edges at $a$ by Lemma 3.6 ($s=2k+1$)
  and the remaining at most $k$ edges by Lemma 3.7 with $m=2$, since each
  remaining $x_i$ has at most two even-degree neighbors. Main theorem
  (p. 124, quoted): "Let $G$ be a graph on $n$ vertices (not necessarily
  connected). If the E-subgraph of $G$ is an $\alpha$-graph, then $G$ can be
  decomposed into $\lfloor\frac n2\rfloor$ paths." The proof
  (pp. 124--125) is summarized on the result page. Corollary (p. 125,
  quoted): "Let $G$ be a graph on $n$ vertices (not necessarily connected).
  If each block of the E-subgraph of $G$ is a triangle-free graph with
  maximum degree at most 3, then $G$ can be decomposed into
  $\lfloor\frac n2\rfloor$ paths", introduced as "a combination of
  Proposition 2.6 and the Main theorem". A filing observation, not a review
  verdict: the edgeless case $E(F)=\emptyset$ of the Main theorem's proof
  cites Pyber's forest result as "[Theorem 0, 4]" (p. 124), while the reference
  list's [4] is Lovász's paper; Pyber's 1996 paper, whose Theorem 0 it is,
  is [6], as the introduction (p. 118) and the opening of § 4 (p. 123) cite
  it.

**Bears on.** [[../wiki/problems/extremal_graph_theory/E0583/_index|#583]]: the Corollary
(printed p. 125, PDF p. 9), "Let $G$ be a graph on $n$ vertices (not
necessarily connected). If each block of the E-subgraph of $G$ is a
triangle-free graph with maximum degree at most 3, then $G$ can be
decomposed into $\lfloor\frac n2\rfloor$ paths", is the block-condition
row of that page's table, and the quotation as Theorem 1.2 of
[[extremal_graph_theory/bonamy_2019_gallai_s_path_decomposition_conjecture_graphs/theorem_1_3|Bonamy and Perrett]]
agrees with the printed statement. The Main theorem (printed p. 124, PDF
p. 8), the table's $\alpha$-graph row, gives the same $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$
paths for the wider class of graphs whose E-subgraph is an $\alpha$-graph,
which by Propositions 2.3--2.6 (p. 119) contains every forest, every
subdivision and subgraph of an $\alpha$-graph, and every graph whose
blocks are triangle-free of maximum degree at most $3$, and consists of
triangle-free graphs; it strengthens Pyber's
[[extremal_graph_theory/pyber_1996_covering_edges_connected_graph_paths/theorem_0|Theorem 0]],
which its proof invokes for the graphs whose E-subgraph has no edges. Both
statements hold for every graph, connected or not, and give
$\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ paths, one fewer than the conjecture's
$\lceil n/2\rceil$ when $n$ is odd; the disjoint triangles of p. 118 show
that the triangle-free requirement cannot be dropped and that no bound near
$n/2$ holds for disconnected graphs without a restriction on the
E-subgraph. Page 118 also states the consequence of Lovász's theorem for
graphs with at most one vertex of even degree with $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$
paths, a floor where that page's Lovász row, taken from other second-hand
quotations, has $\lceil n/2\rceil$. The paper states Gallai's conjecture
with $\lceil n/2\rceil$ for connected graphs (p. 118) and does not settle
it.
