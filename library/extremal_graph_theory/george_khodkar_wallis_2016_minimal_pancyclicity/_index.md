# extremal_graph_theory/george_khodkar_wallis_2016_minimal_pancyclicity

[[extremal_graph_theory/_index|..]]

[[extremal_graph_theory/george_khodkar_wallis_2016_minimal_pancyclicity/small_orders|small_orders]]: The chapter's determination of the least excess m(n) of a pancyclic graph on
n vertices for all n ≤ 37, by chord-pattern case analysis for at most three
chords, two explicit constructions for four and five chords, and the
exhaustive search it cites from Griffin for the four-chord bound.

[[extremal_graph_theory/george_khodkar_wallis_2016_minimal_pancyclicity/theorem_17|theorem_17]]: The chapter's one-step bound on the least excess of a pancyclic graph: the
least excess on n vertices exceeds the least excess on n − 1 vertices by at
most one, with the reverse inequality recorded only as a conjecture.

[[extremal_graph_theory/george_khodkar_wallis_2016_minimal_pancyclicity/theorem_19|theorem_19]]: The chapter's general upper bounds on the least excess of a pancyclic graph:
Theorem 18, excess j + 2 for 2^j + 21 ≤ n ≤ 2^{j+1} + 20 and j ≤ 21, and
Theorem 19, excess at most 2^h + 2h on the window 2^{2^h+h+1} + 2^h + h + 2
≤ n ≤ 2^{2^h+h+2} + 2^h + h + 1, both by explicit chord constructions.

## Contents

- § 4.1, Introduction (p. 35, page image). For each $n\ge3$ the chapter
  defines $m(n)$ as the largest integer such that every pancyclic graph
  on $n$ vertices has at least $n+m(n)$ edges (equivalently, the minimum
  of $e(G)-n$ over pancyclic $G$ on $n$ vertices), and calls a pancyclic
  graph on $n$ vertices with exactly $n+m(n)$ edges *minimal*. Quoted
  definition: "The difference $e(G)-v(G)$ is called the *excess* of the
  graph $G$, so $m(n)$ is the minimum excess for an $n$-vertex pancyclic
  graph." The chapter's $m(n)$ is therefore the problem's $h(n)$, and
  differs from Griffin's $m(n)$, the edge count, by $n$. The chapter
  attributes the notion of minimal pancyclicity to Bondy's paper [3], the
  one that defined pancyclic graphs, and credits Bondy with the question,
  quoted there, "What is the minimum number of edges in a pancyclic graph"
  on a given number of vertices. Conjecture 4.1 (quoted):
  "$m(n)\ge m(n-1)$", called a popular conjecture. Display (4.1), from
  Corollary 12.3 of Chapter 3: $m(n)\le\frac{n^2-3n}2+3$. A new vertex
  joined to both ends of a Hamilton-cycle edge of a minimal pancyclic graph
  on $n-1$ vertices, that edge kept, gives Theorem 17 (p. 36, quoted):
  "$m(n)\le m(n-1)+1$"; the chapter notes that under Conjecture 4.1 this
  leaves $m(n)\in\{m(n-1),m(n-1)+1\}$. Theorem 17 is Griffin's Proposition 2
  and Conjecture 4.1 is Griffin's Conjecture 1, each in the other's letters.
- § 4.2, Minimal Pancyclic Graphs: Small Orders (pp. 36--41). The section
  opens by crediting its results to [13, 16] and announcing the value of
  $m(n)$ for every $n\le37$. A pancyclic graph is drawn as its Hamilton
  cycle $(a_1,\ldots,a_n,a_1)$ with chords; a chord of deficiency $d$ (the
  number of bypassed vertices) yields cycles of lengths $d+2$ and $n-d$;
  two chords *intersect* or *enclose*, pairs of chords are of type A
  (disjoint, not crossing), B (a common endpoint) or C (crossing, "skew").
  § 4.2.1 (p. 37, quoted): "$m(n)=0$ if and only if $n=3$, and $m(n)=1$ if
  and only if $n=4$ or 5". § 4.2.2: two chords give at most seven cycles;
  the nine-vertex case is excluded by citing Shi [30], giving (p. 37,
  quoted) "$m(n)=2$ if and only if $6\le n\le8$", with a direct
  segment-length argument for $n=9$ on p. 38 ending "Therefore $m(9)=3$".
  § 4.2.3: the fourteen three-chord configurations (Fig. 4.4) and a table
  of their cycle counts, at most 15 (type CCC); examples with three chords
  for $10\le n\le14$; type CCC has no 3-cycle, BCC's fifteen-vertex case
  would have to be uniquely pancyclic, which [23] excludes, and a
  segment-length case analysis of type ACC concludes (p. 40) that both
  $n=15$ and $n=16$ need at least four chords. Fig. 4.5 (p. 41) draws
  minimal pancyclic graphs up to order 14.
- § 4.3, Four Chords (pp. 41--42). Fig. 4.6, a graph on $x+13$ vertices
  with four chords, and a table of the cycle lengths it contains, which
  cover every length from 3 to $n$ for $1\le x\le10$; hence $m(n)=4$ for
  $15\le n\le24$ (p. 42). The lower bound beyond that range is cited, not
  proved (p. 42, quoted): "It is reported in [16] that an exhaustive
  search was conducted that shows there is no pancyclic graph on 25 or
  more vertices with four or fewer chords", and the section closes by
  recording $m(n)$ as established for all $n\le24$.
- § 4.4, Five Chords (pp. 42--43). Fig. 4.7, a graph on $n=x+21$ vertices
  with five chords, and its cycle-length table, lengths 3--19 and $n$ down
  to $n-17$: "This proves that $m(n)=5$ for $25\le n\le37$" (p. 42). The
  lower half of that equality is the four-chord exclusion cited from [16].
  Paged with §§ 4.2--4.3 on
  [[extremal_graph_theory/george_khodkar_wallis_2016_minimal_pancyclicity/small_orders|small_orders]].
- § 4.5, More General Bounds for Pancyclics (pp. 42--47, page images).
  Sridharan [32] is described as claiming the value of $m(n)$ for every
  order, and the chapter's critique (pp. 42--43, quoted) is that "it appears
  that the author assumed Conjecture 4.1 to fill in the gaps" left by
  omitted orders, and that "it is never proven that the graphs constructed
  are minimal"; Bondy's Mathematical Reviews review of that paper is
  quoted. Sridharan's graphs $S_n^k$ carry chords $A_0,\ldots,A_k$ of
  deficiencies $1,2,4,\ldots,2^k$, pairwise non-intersecting and
  non-enclosing, giving cycles of lengths $2^i+2$ and $n-j$ for $j$ a sum
  of distinct deficiencies, which the chapter says suffices for
  pancyclicity when $n-(2^{k+1}-1)\le5$ (p. 43); the chapter's computer
  analysis finds
  Sridharan's construction non-pancyclic at $n=17$ (no 5-cycle; the full
  cycle list is printed, p. 44) and at all of $54\le n\le68$ except 60 and
  65 (a table of the missing lengths, p. 45), because for $n<69$ the chord
  of deficiency $2^5$ intersects or encloses earlier chords. The chapter
  then modifies Sridharan's construction slightly (p. 45): vertices
  $x_0=a_1$, $x_k=a_{2^k+k}$; chords $A_k=(x_k,x_{k+1})$ of deficiency
  $2^k$, each opening at the previous chord's closing vertex, defined when
  $n>2^{k+1}+k$; and $A_k^*=(x_k,x_0)$ of deficiency $n-2^k-k$. With
  $A_0,\ldots,A_{k-1}$ the graph has cycles of lengths $n-d$ for
  $1\le d\le2^k-1$, $n$, and $2^i+2$ for $i<k$; adding $A_k^*$ gives
  $n-2^k-k+2$, $2^k+k$ and, for $k>1$, every length from $k+1$ to
  $2^k+k-1$. $G_k=H_n\cup A_0\cup\cdots\cup A_{k-1}\cup A_k^*$ for
  $2^k+k+1\le n\le2^{k+1}+k$, and $H_n\cup A_0\cup\cdots\cup A_{k-1}\cup
  A_k$ for $n\ge2^{k+1}+k+1$, is pancyclic for $2\le k\le4$ on the orders
  of the table of p. 46, which records excess $k+1$ for $G_k$: excess 3
  for $9\le n\le12$, 4 for $13\le n\le20$, 5 for $21\le n\le36$ (the
  chapter notes that $G_4$ fails to be pancyclic at $n=37$, the largest
  case). Adding both $A_4$ and $A_4^*$ gives excess 6 for
  $37\le n\le52$, and, for $5\le j\le20$, $G_j\cup A_4^*$, excess $j+2$, is
  pancyclic for $2^j+j+1\le n\le2^{j+1}+20$ while the chords do not overlap,
  whence Theorem 18 (p. 46, quoted): "When $j\le21$, there is a pancyclic
  graph on $n$ vertices with $n+j+2$ edges whenever
  $2^j+21\le n\le2^{j+1}+20$."
  "So we have a bound for all orders up to $n=2^{22}+20=4{,}194{,}234$ [sic]"
  (p. 46; a filing observation, not a review verdict: $2^{22}+20$ is
  $4{,}194{,}324$). A second table (p. 47) lists excess 6 for
  $37\le n\le52$ through excess 12 for $1045\le n\le2068$. For $j=22$ the
  21-cycle is missing and the chord $A_5^*$ supplies every length from 6
  to 37; in general $A_j^*$ with $A_0,\ldots,A_{j-1}$ gives all lengths
  from $j+1$ to $2^j+j$, and the chapter's recipe adds $A_{h+1}^*$, to
  supply the cycle of length $2^h+h+1$, once $n>2^j+j+1$ with
  $j=2^h+h+1$, so on the stated window the chords $A_0,\ldots,A_{2^h+h+1}$
  and $A_4^*,\ldots,A_{h+1}^*$ give a pancyclic graph. Theorem 19 (p. 47,
  quoted): "When $2^{(2^h+h+1)}+2^h+h+2\le n\le2^{(2^h+h+2)}+2^h+h+1$, there
  is a pancyclic graph on $n$ vertices with $n+2^h+2h$ edges. So the excess
  for a minimal pancyclic graph with $n$ as stated is at most $2^h+2h$."
  This is the chapter's last sentence; it states no lower bound for general
  $n$, no logarithmic form of any bound, and does not mention the bounds of
  p. 84 of [3]. A filing observation, not a review verdict: neither theorem
  states a lower bound on its parameter, and each fails for its smallest
  values against the values of $m(n)$ the chapter itself records on
  pp. 37--42 (those for $25\le n\le37$ resting on Griffin's search).
  Theorem 18 at $j=0$ (excess 2 at $n=22$), $j=1$ (excess 3 at $n=23$, 24)
  and $j=2$ (excess 4 for $25\le n\le28$) contradicts $m(n)=4$ for
  $15\le n\le24$ and $m(n)=5$ for $25\le n\le37$; two or three chords give
  at most 7 or 15 cycles, fewer than the $n-2$ lengths needed. The argument
  before the theorem is stated for $5\le j\le20$, and the cases $j=3$ and
  $j=4$ are the graphs $G_4$ and the excess-6 graph. At $j=21$ the same
  chords ($A_0,\ldots,A_{20}$, $A_{21}^*$ and $A_4^*$) give a 21-cycle only
  at $n=2^{21}+23$ and $n=2^{21}+40$, so on the rest of the theorem's last
  window, $2^{21}+21\le n\le2^{22}+20$, the printed construction does not
  give excess 23. Theorem 19 at $h=0$
  (excess 1 for $7\le n\le10$) contradicts $m(n)\ge2$ for $n\ge6$, and at
  $h=1$ (excess 4 for $21\le n\le36$) contradicts $m(n)=5$ for
  $25\le n\le36$ and falls below the excess 5 of the chapter's own table
  (p. 46); in both cases the listed chords ($A_0,\ldots,A_2$ and
  $A_0,\ldots,A_4$, three and five chords) do not number $2^h+2h$, because
  that count adds the $h-2$ starred chords $A_4^*,\ldots,A_{h+1}^*$ and so
  presupposes $h\ge2$. At $h=2$ ($136\le n\le263$, excess 8) the last
  listed chord $A_7$ is defined only for $n>263$ and no starred chord is
  listed, so with $A_7^*$ in its place the eight chords give every length
  from 8 to $n$ and 3, 4, 6, but no 5-cycle unless $n=138$ and no 7-cycle
  unless $n=140$ (the short cycle of $A_7^*$ has length $n-133$), so never
  both; whether excess 8
  suffices there is not settled by the chapter, whose Theorem 18 gives 8
  for $n\le148$ and 9 for $149\le n\le263$. From $h=3$ on, with $A_j^*$ in
  place of the likewise undefined $A_j$, $j=2^h+h+1$, the listed chords do
  give a pancyclic graph, checked at both ends of the $h=3$ window. Read on
  the page images of PDF pp. 57--59 (printed pp. 45--47) and checked by a
  short cycle-length computation for the small cases, not retained, on
  2026-09-22; the $j=21$ and $h=2$ counts were checked the same way, not

**Bears on.** [[../wiki/problems/extremal_graph_theory/E1016/_index|#1016]]: the chapter's
$m(n)$, the minimum excess of a pancyclic graph on $n$ vertices (p. 35), is
the problem's $h(n)$ exactly. Its Sections 4.2--4.4 establish $h(n)$ for
$n\le37$ ("$m(n)=0$ if and only if $n=3$", p. 37, through "$m(n)=5$ for
$25\le n\le37$", p. 42), the values of Griffin's Table 1, with the
four-chord exclusion for $n\ge25$ cited from Griffin's exhaustive search.
Its Section 4.5 is the passage the site names for "the first published
proof of the upper bound" $h(n)\le\log_2n+\log_*n+O(1)$: its general
results are Theorem 18 (p. 46), excess at most $j+2$ for
$2^j+21\le n\le2^{j+1}+20$ and $j\le21$, that is $h(n)\le\log_2n+2$ for
$53\le n\le2^{22}+20$, and Theorem 19 (p. 47), excess at most $2^h+2h$ for
$2^{(2^h+h+1)}+2^h+h+2\le n\le2^{(2^h+h+2)}+2^h+h+1$. A filing observation,
not a review verdict: on Theorem 19's window $j=2^h+h+1<\log_2n<j+2$, so
its bound $2^h+2h=j+h-1$ reads $h(n)<\log_2n+h$ with $h<\log_2\log_2n$,
the form $\log_2n+\log_2\log_2n+O(1)$ the problem's thread reports from
Jia 1996, and the theorem is stated only on those
windows, which for successive $h$ leave $n$ between $2^{(2^h+h+2)}+2^h+h+1$
and $2^{(2^{h+1}+h+2)}+2^{h+1}+h+3$ uncovered (for $h\ge4$, beyond the reach
of Theorem 18); the preceding sentence describes the chord recipe for a
general $j$ but the theorem does not state it. The chapter prints no bound
of the form $\log_2n+\log_*n+O(1)$ and does not restate or cite the bounds
Bondy claimed on p. 84 of the 1971 paper, so the chapter does not, on the
reading here, supply a proof of the upper bound in the site's form; what it
proves is recorded on the result page. The chapter also lists Griffin's
preprint as "(to appear)" in 2016 and gives the George--Marr--Wallis values
paper a journal reference. Theorem 17, $h(n)\le h(n-1)+1$
([[extremal_graph_theory/george_khodkar_wallis_2016_minimal_pancyclicity/theorem_17|theorem_17]]),
and Conjecture 4.1, $h(n)\ge h(n-1)$, left open, are Griffin's
Proposition 2 and Conjecture 1, which Griffin states for the edge count
$n+h(n)$. The
problem page reads Theorems 18 and 19 on the page images at statement depth
with their constructions followed.
