B y^{x-1} log log y / log y for x at least 3, in the paper's convention in
which R(x,y) is the largest order of a graph with no K_x and no y
independent points, by induction on x from Proposition 9; the Ramsey

[[graph_coloring/graver_yackel_1968_graph_theoretic_results_associated_ramsey_theorem/proposition_9|proposition_9]]: Graver and Yackel's Proposition 9: R(3,y) at most B y^2 log log y / log y,
the largest order of a triangle-free graph with no y independent points,
by counting the points of the graph above the subsets of a preferred
independent set; the 1968 upper bound whose log log factor Ajtai, Komlós

h_3(k) >> k^2 log k / log log k on Problem 1013.

## Conventions

The paper's $R(x,y)$ is not the usual Ramsey number. Definition 3 (p. 126,
quoted): "$G$ is an $(x,y)$-graph if $x>C(G)$ and $y>I(G)$", with $C(G)$
the clique number and $I(G)$ the independence number (Definitions 1--2,
p. 125). Definition 4 (p. 126, quoted): "$R(x,y)$ is the largest integer
such that there is an $(x,y)$-graph on $R(x,y)$ points (the existence of
$R(x,y)$ is assured by Ramsey's theorem)." An $(x,y)$-graph on $n$ points
exists exactly when $n$ is below the usual Ramsey number $r(x,y)$, the
least order forcing a $K_x$ or $y$ independent points, so the paper's
$R(x,y)$ equals $r(x,y)-1$ throughout; the table of numerical results
(p. 126) prints $R(3,3)=5$, $R(3,4)=8$, $R(3,5)=13$, $R(3,6)=17$,
$R(3,7)=22$, $R(3,8)\in[26,29]$, $R(3,9)\in[35,36]$, $R(4,3)=8$,
$R(4,4)=17$ and $R(4,5)\le29$, each one less than the usual value. The
problem pages use the usual $R(3,k)$; the translation adds one and does
not affect any asymptotic statement. "Valence" is degree; a "preferred"
point $p$ splits $G$ into $H_1$, the subgraph spanned by its neighbors,
and $H_2$, the subgraph spanned by the other non-neighbors (p. 128); a
point of $H_2$ with exactly $j$ edges to $H_1$ is a $j$-point, and a set
$S\subseteq H_1$ supports the points of $H_2$ all of whose edges to $H_1$
land in $S$ (pp. 139--141). $e(x,y,n)$ is the least number of edges of an
$(x,y)$-graph on $n$ points (Definition 6, p. 130).

## Contents

- Abstract and Introduction (pp. 125--126, page images). The abstract
  describes a single treatment of counting methods that give sharp upper
  bounds on the two-color Ramsey numbers, with constructions for the lower
  bounds, names $R(3,6)$ and $R(3,7)$ among the values computed, and
  claims, quoted: "Our computational techniques yield the upper bound
  $R(x,y)\le cy^{x-1}\log\log y/\log y$ for $x\ge3$." The introduction
  restates it as $R(x,y)\le Cy^{x-1}\log\log y/\log y$ "for some constant
  $C$, and all $x\ge3$" (p. 126) and prints the table of numerical results
  recorded under Conventions.
- Section 1 (pp. 126--147, text layer). Lemma 1: complementation swaps
  $(x,y)$ and $(y,x)$. Lemma 2: in an $(x,y)$-graph on $n$ points the
  valences lie between $(n-1)-R(x,y-1)$ and $R(x-1,y)$. Definition 5 and
  Proposition 1 give $n\le R(x-1,y)+R(x,y-1)+1-\sigma(G)$, and Corollary 1
  (p. 127) is the Erdős--Szekeres recursion in the paper's convention,
  $R(x,y)\le R(x,y-1)+R(x-1,y)+1$, strict when both terms are odd.
  Computations A--F (pp. 127--128): $R(2,y)=y-1$, $R(3,3)=5$ with the
  pentagon unique, $R(3,4)\le8$, $R(3,5)\le13$, $R(3,6)\le18$,
  $R(4,4)\le17$. Propositions 2--4 (pp. 128--131) count edges through a
  preferred point and give lower bounds for $e(3,y,n)$; Computations G--M
  (pp. 131--136) evaluate $e(3,y,n)$ for small parameters, prove
  $R(4,5)\le29$ (Computation J, p. 132) and $R(3,6)\le17$ (Computation K,
  pp. 133--134, by counting pentagons through a point). Definition 7,
  Lemma 3 and Proposition 5 (pp. 136--139) bound the independence number
  of a graph of girth $z$ by alternating sums of connected subgraph counts
  with at most $w<z$ edges, by an induction on edges with positive and
  negative subgraphs. Proposition 6 (p. 140) sums these bounds over the
  $k$-subsets of a preferred independent set $H_1$, with Remark 2 (p. 141)
  on how many points can lie above a $k$-set (at most $R(3,k+1)-k$) and
  Proposition 7 (p. 142) evaluating the error term for $k=3$ as the number
  of configurations of types $C_1$--$C_4$ of Figure 1, its proof finding
  the net contribution of $C_5$ to be $0$. Computation R (p. 144) reproves
  $R(3,6)\le17$; Computation L continued (pp. 144--145) gives
  $e(3,6,17)\ge40$; Computation O (pp. 145--147) proves $R(3,7)\le22$
  through Lemmas 4--8, with Lemmas 7 and 8 deferred to Appendix A and the
  proof closed by counting quadrilaterals.
- Section 2 (pp. 147--153; p. 149 on the page image, the rest in the text
  layer). Definition 8: a cyclic graph on $n$ points joins $r$ and $s$ when
  $|r-s|\equiv\pm i_j\pmod n$ for one of the chosen $i_1<\cdots<i_k\le[n/2]$.
  Computation F (p. 148): $R(3,4)=8$ from the octagon with antipodal points
  joined ($i_1=1$, $i_2=4$), "Clearly there are no triangles", and Remark 2
  that every $(3,4)$-graph on eight points is this graph or the same with
  one or two diagonals removed. Computation Q (p. 148): $R(3,5)=13$ from
  $i_1=1$, $i_2=5$ ($H_{13}$, Figure 3). Computation R (p. 148, by
  computer search over cyclic graphs): $R(3,6)\ge16$, $R(3,7)\ge21$,
  $R(3,8)\ge26$, $R(3,9)\ge35$, $R(4,4)=17$, with (1) and (5) credited to
  Greenwood and Gleason and all five to Kalbfleisch independently.
  Proposition 8 (p. 149): for a $(3,y)$-graph $G$ with $n$ points and
  $e$ edges, and points $p_1$, $p_2$ of $G$ at distance at least 5 (every
  path joining them has at least 5 edges), of valences $v_1$ and $v_2$,
  with $K_i$ the set of the $v_i$ neighbors of $p_i$: delete $p_1$ and
  $p_2$ with all their edges and join every point of $K_1$ to every point
  of $K_2$; the result $G'$ has $n-2$ points, is a $(3,y-1)$-graph, and
  its edge count is $e+(v_1-1)(v_2-1)-1$.
  The paper calls this "a method of welding two disjoint graphs together"
  (p. 149). Corollary 4 (p. 149) welds a pentagon on and is paged below;
  Corollary 5 (p. 150) welds $H_8$ on to gain four points and one unit of
  independence number, and Corollary 6 (p. 150) welds a pentagon on at two
  points at least four apart to gain one point at the same independence

## Translation to the problem pages' functions

The paper states its results for $R(x,y)$ and prints no chromatic-number
statement. The problem pages cite it for chromatic numbers through Erdős's
1969 survey (its p. 29), which prints, as (4),
"$g_l(n)<c_1(n\log\log n/\log n)^{(l-2)(l-1)}$" for the largest chromatic
number of a graph on $n$ vertices with no $K_l$, its exponent misprinted
without the slash and read below as $(l-2)/(l-1)$, and then, as (5),
"$I(G)>c(n\log n/\log\log n)^{1/(l-1)}$" for such a graph, saying "(4) is
an easy consequence of (5)". The steps behind these forms, made here and
not stated in the paper:

- Independence number. If $G$ has $n$ points, no $K_x$ and $I(G)<y$, then
  $G$ is an $(x,y)$-graph and $n\le R(x,y)\le By^{x-1}\log\log y/\log y$ by
  the Corollary. Hence every $K_x$-free graph on $n$ points has
  $I(G)\ge y$ as soon as $By^{x-1}\log\log y/\log y<n$, that is
  $I(G)\ge c_x(n\log n/\log\log n)^{1/(x-1)}$ for $n\ge n_x$, Erdős's (5)
  with $l=x$.
- Chromatic number. Remove a largest independent set repeatedly from a
  $K_x$-free graph on $n$ points. While more than $n/2$ points remain, each
  removal takes at least $c_x(\frac n2\log\frac n2/\log\log\frac n2)^{1/(x-1)}$
  points, so at most $\ll n^{1-1/(x-1)}(\log\log n/\log n)^{1/(x-1)}$
  colors are spent before the graph has at most $n/2$ points; the same
  bound for $n/2$, $n/4$, $\ldots$ sums to a geometric series since
  $1-1/(x-1)>0$, and the last $n_x$ points take at most $n_x$ colors. So
  $\chi(G)\ll n^{1-1/(x-1)}(\log\log n/\log n)^{1/(x-1)}$. For $x=3$ this
  is $\chi(G)\ll(n\log\log n/\log n)^{1/2}$, the site's bound on Problem
  920 at $k=3$ and Erdős's (4) at $l=3$. A filing observation, not a
  review verdict: for $l\ge4$ Erdős's (4), and the site's display
  $f_k(n)\ll(n\log\log n/\log n)^{1-1/(k-1)}$ for Problem 920, put the
  exponent $(l-2)/(l-1)$ on the logarithmic factor, where this derivation
  from the Corollary gives $1/(l-1)$; the two agree for $l=3$ and the form
  with exponent $(l-2)/(l-1)$ is the stronger for $l\ge4$. The Corollary
  itself carries one factor $\log\log y/\log y$ for every $x$, since its
  induction on $x$
  (p. 157) bounds the valences of the preferred independent set by the
  case $x-1$ and does not iterate the counting of Proposition 9, so the
  stronger exponent is not what the paper proves. The compiler of Problem
  920 decides how to record the site's display.
- Order of a triangle-free $k$-chromatic graph. If $G$ is triangle-free on
  $n$ points with $\chi(G)=k$, the $x=3$ case gives
  $k\ll(n\log\log n/\log n)^{1/2}$, so $n\gg k^2\log n/\log\log n\ge
  k^2\log k/\log\log k$ (as $n\ge k$), the site's
  $h_3(k)\gg k^2\log k/\log\log k$ on Problem 1013.
- Consecutive Ramsey numbers. Corollary 4 applied to a $(3,y)$-graph on
  $R(3,y)$ points (which has a point of some valence $v\ge0$) gives a
  $(3,y+1)$-graph on $R(3,y)+3$ points, so $R(3,y+1)\ge R(3,y)+3$, and in
  the usual notation $R(3,k+1)\ge R(3,k)+3$ for every $k\ge2$; the
  paper does not print this inequality, and the 1989 paper of Burr, Erdős,
  Faudree and Schelp states it as the case $m=3$ of its Theorem 1 with the
  credit to Corollary 4.
