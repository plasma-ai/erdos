## Statement

Setting (pp. 447--448). $P=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}$, $N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, and
$\langle mx+ny:1\rangle$ is the least subset of $P$ containing $1$ and closed
under $(x,y)\mapsto mx+ny$.

**Conjecture 2** (p. 457, quoted). "The set $\langle mx+ny:1\rangle$
contains an infinite arithmetic progression for all $m,n\in P$."

## Status in the paper

The paper motivates the conjecture (p. 457) by a statement it says can be
proved along the lines of Theorem 4 (no proof is printed): if
$a,d,r-1,m_1,\ldots,m_r\in P$ with $(a,d)=(m_1,\ldots,m_r)=1$ and
$a+dN\subseteq\langle m_1x_1+\cdots+m_rx_r:1\rangle=S$, then $S$ is a per-set.
It adds, in parentheses, that the conjecture has now been proved, citing the
authors' reference [2]; that proof is not in this paper.

The paper also records (p. 457) that the conjecture would not make
$\langle mx+ny:1\rangle$ a per-set in general: it reports that R. Graham has
shown $\langle 3x+3y:1\rangle$ is not a near per-set, though it contains
$36+45N$. It then cites "Theorem 12", giving arbitrarily long arithmetic
progressions in $\langle mx+ny:1\rangle$ for all $m,n\in P$, as evidence for
the conjecture (pp. 457--458); no Theorem 12 is printed in the paper, whose
last numbered results are Theorem 11 and Lemma 5 (pp. 460--463).

## Proof pointer

None in this paper; the paper attributes the proof to its reference [2].
