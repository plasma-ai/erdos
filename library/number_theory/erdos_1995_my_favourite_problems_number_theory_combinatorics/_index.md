Item 11 of Part I (p. 7, read on the page image) is the origin of the
site's prize for Problem 54. Erdős opens by calling his paper with Burr,
his reference [11], undeservedly forgotten. With $P(A)$ the set of integers
representable as sums of distinct terms of $A$, the item defines a sequence
$A=\{a_1<a_2<\ldots\}$ to be *Ramsey $r$-complete* when, for every
partition $A=A_1\cup\dots\cup A_r$ into $r$ classes, every sufficiently
large positive integer lies in $\bigcup_{i=1}^rP(A_i)$, and *entirely*
Ramsey $r$-complete when every positive integer does. The Burr--Erdős
paper treats $r=2$ and proves two things: there is an entirely Ramsey
$2$-complete sequence with $a_x>\exp\{\frac12(\log2)x^{1/3}\}$ for all
sufficiently large $x$ (display (8)), and there is a $C>0$ such that no
infinite sequence with $a_x>\exp\{Cx^{1/2}\}$ for all sufficiently large $x$
(display (9)) is Ramsey $2$-complete. Erdős says much is left open: he and Burr
proved nothing for $r>2$, a case for which he offers a prize for any
non-trivial result, and he asks, "could (8) and (9) be improved (100
dollars)?" The item ends: "Burr has a proof that for every $k$ the
sequence $t^k$ ($1\le t<\infty$) is Ramsey $r$-complete" (p. 7). The site
cites p. 172 of the journal for Problem 54; the typescript's running-head
page 7 is the locator here.

A late three-part problem collection with prize offers throughout. In number
theory it opens with the sum-distinct question f(n) < log n/log 2 + c_1,
then covers Euler-function and sigma questions, the Erdos-Szemeredi sum-product
conjecture f(n) > n^{2-eps} with two prize offers, and Carmichael's
multiplicity conjecture. For #50, section I records Schoenberg's theorem that
phi(n)/n has a distribution function f(x), states Erdos's own result that f is
purely singular, and asks with a prize whether f can have a finite positive
derivative at any x, referring only generally to Elliott's Probabilistic Number
Theory for details; no proof is given. For #74, combinatorics section 7 restates
the Erdos-Hajnal-Szemeredi question in its own words: for f(n) -> infinity
arbitrarily slowly, is there a graph of infinite chromatic number in which
every n-vertex subgraph can be made bipartite by deleting fewer than f(n)
edges, with a prize offered, Rodl's 1982 hypergraph analogue cited, and the
weaker variants f(n) = o(n^eps) or f(n) < (log n)^c asked separately; their
proofs are not reproduced.
For #87, combinatorics section 15 (pp. 13--14) recalls the conjecture
(18) $r(G,G)\ge r(n)=r(K(n),K(n))$ for $n$-chromatic $G$, stated in the book of
Bondy and Murty (Problem 26, p. 250). Erdős notes that (18) is trivial for
$n=3$ but false already for $n=4$, since Faudree and McKay proved that the
Ramsey number of the pentagonal wheel is 17; he expects the conjecture to
fail for every $n>4$ while $r(G,G)$ should not be much smaller than $r(n)$,
and proposes two weakenings in its place: "$r(G,G)>(1-\varepsilon)^nr(n)$
should hold for some $0<\varepsilon<1$ and perhaps even
$\lim_{n\to0}r(G,G)/r(n)>0$ [sic]" (p. 14; the printed subscript $n\to0$ is a
misprint for $n\to\infty$), adding that both may be beyond present methods.
Section II.9 ("Some Ramsey type problems", pp. 11--12) states for #77 the bounds
(14) $ct2^{t/2}<r(t)<t^{-1/2}\binom{2t-2}{t-1}$ with $r(t)=r(K(t),K(t))$,
asks for a proof that $c=\lim_{t\to\infty}r(t)^{1/t}$ exists and for its
value, notes that by (14) the limit, if it exists, lies between $\sqrt2$
and 4, and offers prizes for the existence of $c$, for its
value and for its nonexistence, "but this is really a joke as
$c$ certainly exists"; for #78 it adds that the lower bound of (14) is
proved probabilistically and offers a prize for a constructive proof of
$r(t)>(1+c)^t$ for some $c>0$ (p. 11). The same section (p. 12) defines,
after Erdős, Faudree, Rousseau and Schelp, $G$ to be Ramsey size linear if
$r(G,H_n)<Cn$ (15) for every graph $H_n$ with $n$ edges and states two of
that paper's problems: $K(4)$ is known not to be Ramsey size linear
although all its subgraphs are, and Erdős asks "Are there other such
graphs and in fact are there infinitely many such graphs?" (#79) and "Is
$K(3,3)$ Ramsey size linear?" (#567). Section II.16 (p. 14) is the
Erdős--McKay conjecture of #88: if a graph $G(n,c_1n^2)$ on $n$ vertices
with $c_1n^2$ edges contains no trivial (complete or empty) subgraph on
$c_2\log n$ vertices, then there is a $t>\varepsilon n^2$, for a constant
$\varepsilon>0$, such that the graph has an induced subgraph with exactly
$i$ edges for every $i\le t$. Erdős offers a prize for a proof or
disproof, remarks that the problem has dropped almost entirely out of sight
and may turn out to be easy, and reports that he and McKay proved it only
for some $t<c(\log n)^2$. For #99, geometry section 4 conjectures that
a minimum-diameter configuration of n points with minimum distance 1 must
contain three points forming a unit equilateral triangle, reports that
Simonovits, Vesztergombi and Sendov all doubted it, offers a prize, and adds the
stronger unproved belief that such a set has very large intersection with the
triangular lattice; the accompanying claim that the optimal sets are
asymptotically similar to the triangular lattice but never subsets of it for
large n is also stated as unproved.

Item 8 of Part I ("One last Ramsey type problem", p. 6, read on the page
image; the item stands under the heading "I. Number theory") has three
unit-fraction questions. First, $n_k$ is the smallest integer, if one
exists, such that however the proper divisors of $n_k$ are colored with $k$
colors, $n_k$ is a sum of distinct divisors all of one color; Erdős is sure
that $n_k$ exists for every $k$ but thinks it "not even known if $n_2$
exists", and would like at least $n_2$ determined. Second, the old
Erdős--Graham question: "Is it true that if $m_k$ is sufficiently large and
we colour the integers $2\le t\le m_k$ by $k$ colours then $1=\sum1/t_i$ is
always solvable monochromatically?" He would like to see a proof that $m_2$
exists, and notes that clearly $m_k\ge n_k$. Third, suggesting that the
question is really of Turán type rather than Ramsey type, he asks whether
for sufficiently large $m$ every sequence of integers
$1<a_1<a_2<\dots<a_\ell\le m$ with $\sum_\ell1/a_\ell>\delta\log m$ admits a
solution of $1=\sum\varepsilon_i/a_i$ with $\varepsilon_i\in\{0,1\}$,
offering a prize for a proof or disproof, and suggests that the weaker
hypothesis $\sum_{a_i<m}1/a_i>C(\log\log m)^2$, for some large enough $C$,
may already suffice. The section closes by referring to the book with
Graham [34]. The page states no result on the largest subset of
$\{1,\dots,n\}$ with no subset of reciprocals summing to $1$ (the site's
Problem 300), so no row is added for it.

Item 9 of Part I (p. 6) is the source of Problem 49. For the longest
$a_1<\dots<a_t\le n$ with $\varphi(a_1)<\dots<\varphi(a_t)$ (display (7)),
Erdős expects $t=\pi(n)$. He asks whether one can prove
$t<(1+o(1))\pi(n)$ or at least $t=o(n)$, expects the last to be easy, and
notes that similar questions arise for $\sigma(n)$.

**Results to transcribe.**

- Item 11 of Part I, p. 7: the Ramsey $r$-complete and entirely Ramsey
  $r$-complete definitions; (8) an entirely Ramsey $2$-complete sequence with
  $a_x>\exp\{\frac12(\log2)x^{1/3}\}$; (9) no Ramsey $2$-complete sequence
  with $a_x>\exp\{Cx^{1/2}\}$; a prize for any non-trivial result for
  $r>2$ and a prize for improving (8) and (9); Burr's announced proof
  that the $k$th powers are Ramsey $r$-complete for every $k$.

- Section I.1: For sum-distinct sets, asks with a prize whether f(n) < log n/log
  2 + c_1; the weaker bound with an extra log log n/log 2 term is immediate
  and Erdos-Moser gave a second-moment improvement.
- Section I (Euler function distribution): Schoenberg proved phi(n)/n has a
  distribution function f; Erdos proved f purely singular and asks with a prize
  whether f has a finite positive derivative at any point.
- Section II.7: For f(n) -> infinity arbitrarily slowly, asks with a prize for a
  graph of infinite chromatic number whose every n-vertex subgraph becomes
  bipartite after deleting fewer than f(n) edges; Rodl proved the hypergraph
  analog in 1982.
- Item 8 of Part I, p. 6: $n_k$ (monochromatic sums of distinct proper divisors
  under $k$ colors; "not even known if $n_2$ exists"); the Erdős--Graham
  question for $m_k$ ($1=\sum1/t_i$ solvable in one color class of
  $2\le t\le m_k$); the Turán-type form with $\sum1/a_i>\delta\log m$
  (a prize) and its $(\log\log m)^2$ weakening.
- Item 10 of Part I, p. 7: Erdos-Szemeredi proved f(n) > n^{1+eps} for the
  number of distinct sums and products of n integers and conjecture f(n) >
  n^{2-eps}, with two prizes offered; Nathanson proved f(n) > n^{1+1/31}.
- Section II.9, p. 11: (14) $ct2^{t/2}<r(t)<t^{-1/2}\binom{2t-2}{t-1}$; the
  existence and value of $c=\lim r(t)^{1/t}$ (prizes; a prize for
  nonexistence "really a joke"); a prize for a constructive
  proof of $r(t)>(1+c)^t$.
- Section II.9, p. 12: Sidorenko's $r(K_3,H_n)\le2n+1$ for every $H_n$ with
  $n$ edges and no isolated vertices (Harary's conjecture); the Ramsey size
  linear definition (15) $r(G,H_n)<Cn$; the questions whether infinitely
  many graphs are, like $K(4)$, not Ramsey size linear with all proper
  subgraphs Ramsey size linear, and whether $K(3,3)$ is Ramsey size linear.
- Section II.15, pp. 13--14: the conjecture (18) $r(G,G)\ge r(n)$ for
  $n$-chromatic $G$ fails at $n=4$ (Faudree--McKay: the pentagonal wheel has
  Ramsey number 17); Erdős proposes $r(G,G)>(1-\varepsilon)^nr(n)$ for some
  $0<\varepsilon<1$ and $\lim r(G,G)/r(n)>0$, and says both may be
  beyond present methods.
- Section II.16, p. 14: the Erdős--McKay conjecture (a prize): a graph
  with $c_1n^2$ edges and no trivial subgraph on $c_2\log n$ vertices has
  induced subgraphs with exactly $i$ edges for every $i\le t$, for some
  $t>\varepsilon n^2$; proved only for some $t<c(\log n)^2$.
- Item 8 of Part II, p. 11 (page image): the Faudree--Ordman--Erdős
  conjecture (13) $h(n)=(1+o(1))n^2/12$ for the largest guaranteed family
  of edge-disjoint monochromatic triangles in a two-colored $K(n)$, best
  possible by the balanced two-part coloring; the same-color companion
  (more than $(1+c)n^2/24$ monochromatic triangles of one color) with
  Jacobson's conjectured value $n^2/20$; Goodman's count without
  edge-disjointness.
- Item 14 of Part II, p. 13 (page image): $f(n)$ the least minimum degree
  forcing a $C_4$ in a graph on $n$ vertices; "Is it true that
  $f(n+1)\ge f(n)$?", the weakening $f(m)>f(n)-c$ for all $m>n$, and the
  same question for other graphs in place of $C_4$; followed by the
  hypercube conjecture (17), $g(n)<(\frac12+o(1))n2^{n-1}$ with a prize
  offer.
- Section III.4: Conjectures that a diameter-minimizing set of n points with
  minimum distance 1 contains a unit equilateral triangle (a prize offered,
  doubted by Simonovits, Vesztergombi and Sendov), and believes the set meets
  the triangular lattice in a very large subset.
