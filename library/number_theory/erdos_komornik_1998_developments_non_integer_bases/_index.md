  with digits 0, ..., m: Pisot numbers are the bases whose signed-digit sums

# number_theory/erdos_komornik_1998_developments_non_integer_bases

[[number_theory/_index|..]]

[[number_theory/erdos_komornik_1998_developments_non_integer_bases/theorem_i|theorem_i]]: Erdős and Komornik's characterization of the Pisot numbers by the set of
finite sums of powers of q with integer digits of modulus at most m: no
finite accumulation point for every m when q is Pisot, and one for every
integer m >= q - 1/q when q is not; its Part (b) supplies the first
hypothesis of Lemma 3.2 in the proof of Theorem IV.

[[number_theory/erdos_komornik_1998_developments_non_integer_bases/theorem_ii|theorem_ii]]: Erdős and Komornik's theorem on the smallest gaps of the ordered finite
sums of powers of q with digits 0, ..., m: the lim inf of the gaps is
positive for every m when q is a Pisot number, and zero for every
m >= [q - 1/q]' + [q - 1]' (upper integer parts) when q is not.

[[number_theory/erdos_komornik_1998_developments_non_integer_bases/theorem_iii|theorem_iii]]: Erdős and Komornik's theorem that the consecutive gaps of the ordered
finite sums of powers of q with digits 0, ..., m tend to zero for every
non-Pisot q once m >= [q - 1/q]' + 2[q - 1]' (upper integer parts), a
bound that is never as small as 1 or 2.

[[number_theory/erdos_komornik_1998_developments_non_integer_bases/theorem_iv|theorem_iv]]: Erdős and Komornik's theorem that the consecutive gaps of the ordered finite
sums of powers of q with digits 0, ..., m tend to zero for every m when
1 < q <= 2^(1/4) and q is not the square root of the second Pisot number; the
m = 1 case is the first resolution of Problem 1096, on a range whose two
second-hand accounts the printed statement reconciles.

[[number_theory/erdos_komornik_1998_developments_non_integer_bases/theorem_v|theorem_v]]: Erdős and Komornik's theorem that when the gaps of the ordered finite sums
of powers of q with digits 0, ..., m tend to zero, every alpha in
(0, m/(q - 1)) has a development in base q with digits 0, ..., m that
contains every finite string of those digits as a block.

## Contents

- Abstract and § 0, Introduction (pp. 57--60, page images). Fix $q>1$ and
  let $0=y_0<y_1<y_2<\cdots$ be the increasing sequence of the real numbers
  with at least one representation $y=\varepsilon_0+\varepsilon_1q+\cdots+
  \varepsilon_nq^n$, $n\ge0$, $\varepsilon_i\in\{0,1\}$. The sequence
  tends to infinity, more slowly as $q$ approaches 1, and the introduction
  (p. 57) names the first question, attributed to [4], Problem 4: do the
  gaps $y_{k+1}-y_k$ tend to 0 for every $q$ close enough to 1? The paper
  points out one difficulty: for some algebraic $q$ a number $y$ has several
  representations, while $(y_k)$ lists each value once. It then claims the
  answer, "One of the purposes of this paper is to give an affirmative
  answer to this question" (p. 57), and announces the special case of
  Theorem IV that delivers it, $y_{k+1}-y_k\to0$ for every $q$ between 1
  and $2^{1/4}$ "except possibly the square root of the second Pisot number
  $\sqrt{p_2}\approx1.175$". A parenthetical note of the second author adds
  that the same property "probably holds also if $q=\sqrt{p_2}$" and that
  the first author's sudden death prevented the study. The second question
  (p. 58), from Bogmér, Horváth and Sövegjártó [2]: whether for each $q$
  close to 1 some development $1=\sum_{i\ge1}\varepsilon_iq^{-i}$ contains
  arbitrarily long runs of the digit 0; Theorem IV with Part c) of Theorem 4
  of [4] gives such developments for every $q$ between 1 and $2^{1/4}$
  except possibly $\sqrt{p_2}$, and Theorem V gives developments containing
  every finite variation of the digits. For $q>1$ and an integer $m\ge1$,
  $Y=Y^{q,m}$ is the set of the sums $s_0+s_1q+\cdots+s_nq^n$ with
  $s_i\in\{0,\pm1,\ldots,\pm m\}$, and Pisot numbers are the algebraic
  integers $q>1$ all of whose conjugates lie in $|z|<1$. Theorem I
  (p. 58, quoted): "(a) If $q$ is a Pisot number, then $Y$ has no finite
  accumulation points for any $m$. (b) If $q$ is not a Pisot number, then
  $Y$ has finite accumulation points for every integer $m\ge q-q^{-1}$."
  Remark: the bound on $m$ is probably not sharp, and Proposition 2.3 shows
  that for $m\le(q-1)/2$ there are none, whatever $q$. Then $y_k^{q,m}$ is
  the increasing sequence of the sums with digits $\varepsilon_i\in
  \{0,1,\ldots,m\}$ (pp. 58--59), and $Y$ is the set of the differences
  $y_k-y_l$. Theorem II (p. 59, quoted): "(a) If $q$ is a Pisot number, then
  $\liminf(y_{k+1}-y_k)>0$ for every $m$. (b) If $q$ is not a Pisot number,
  then $\liminf(y_{k+1}-y_k)=0$ for every $m\ge[q-q^{-1}]'+[q-1]'$ where we
  denote by $[\alpha]'$ the upper integer part of $\alpha$ (i.e. the
  smallest integer $\ge\alpha$)." Remarks: Part (a) for $1<q<2$ was proved
  earlier by Bugeaud [7] by a different approach; Bugeaud also proved that a
  non-Pisot $1<q<2$ has some $m$ with $\liminf=0$ without an estimate, and
  Part (b) shows $m=3$ suffices there; Lemma 2.1 (b) gives
  $\liminf(y_{k+1}-y_k)=1$ whenever $m\le q-1$. The paper calls the
  $\limsup$ the harder quantity. Theorem III
  (p. 59, quoted): "If $q$ is not a Pisot number, then $y_{k+1}-y_k\to0$
  for every $m\ge[q-q^{-1}]'+2[q-1]'$." Remark: Proposition 3.3 gives
  $\limsup(y_{k+1}-y_k)\ge1$ whenever $m\le q-q^{-1}$. The paper notes
  that Theorem III never applies for $m=1$ or $m=2$, so it leaves the first
  question open, which the next theorem settles.
  Theorem IV (p. 59, quoted): "If $1<q\le2^{1/4}$ and if $q$ is
  different from the square root of the second Pisot number, then
  $y_{k+1}-y_k\to0$ for every $m\ge1$." Remarks (pp. 59--60): (a) the same
  property probably holds at $\sqrt{p_2}$, a question the authors meant to
  study during a visit in October 1996; (b) on the convergence rate,
  Proposition 3.4 shows an exponential rate for $q=\sqrt2$ and $m=1$. A
  development $\alpha=\sum_{i\ge1}\varepsilon_iq^{-i}$ with digits in
  $\{0,1,\ldots,m\}$ is *universal* if every finite variation
  $\delta_1\ldots\delta_k$ of the integers $0,1,\ldots,m$ occurs as a block
  of $(\varepsilon_i)$. Theorem V (p. 60, quoted): "Let $q$ and $m$ be such
  that $y_{k+1}-y_k\to0$. Then every $0<\alpha<m/(q-1)$ has a universal
  development." Combined with Theorem IV: for $1<q\le2^{1/4}$,
  $q\ne\sqrt{p_2}$, every $m$ and every $0<\alpha<m/(q-1)$ has a universal
  development, "the last joint result of the authors, obtained in July
  1996". Remark (b): for fixed $m$, the bases $q$ in which $\alpha=1$ has a
  universal development form a dense subset of $[0,m+1]$, and whether
  almost every $q$ in that interval is such a base is left open.
- § 1, Accumulation points of the set $Y$; proof of Theorem I (pp. 60--72,
  page images, statements checked, proofs for structure). Lemma 1.1: for
  Pisot $q$, 0 is not an accumulation point of $Y$ for any $m$ (the
  distances $d(q^n)$ to the nearest integer decay exponentially, so
  $|y|>(q+1)^{-1}q^{1-N}$ for every nonzero $y\in Y$). Lemma 1.2: a finite
  accumulation point of $Y^{q,m}$ makes 0 an accumulation point of
  $Y^{q,2m}$. Part (a) of Theorem I follows. Lemma 1.3: if $Y$ has no
  finite accumulation point and $m\ge q-1$, then $q$ is an algebraic
  integer. Lemmas 1.4 and 1.5, which the paper presents as generalizing
  results of Frougny [8] and of Erdős, Joó and Schnitzer [6]: if $Y$ has no
  finite accumulation point and $q$ is algebraic, then for a conjugate $p$
  of $q$ a series $\sum s_iq^{-i}=0$ with integers $|s_i|\le m$ also
  vanishes at $p$ when $|p|>1$ and has partial sums on finitely many
  circles centered at 0 when $|p|=1$ (Lemma 1.4); for $m\ge q-q^{-1}$ and a
  complex $p\ne q$ there is such a series vanishing at $q$ but not at $p$
  when $|p|>1$, and one whose partial sums at $p$ take infinitely many
  absolute values when $|p|=1$ (Lemma 1.5). Part (b) of Theorem I follows from
  Lemmas 1.3--1.5 (p. 64). Lemmas 1.6--1.8 (pp. 65--69): a "lazy" digit
  algorithm for subseries of a convergent positive series, signed series
  $\sum s_ia_i=0$ with prescribed sign pattern, and the case
  $a_i=q^{-[i/m]}$; the proof of Lemma 1.5 (pp. 69--72) treats $p$
  nonnegative real, negative real and non-real in turn.
- § 2, Study of $\liminf(y_{k+1}-y_k)$; proof of Theorem II (pp. 72--74).
  $Y$ is the set of differences $y_k-y_l$, so $\liminf(y_{k+1}-y_k)>0$ iff
  0 is not an accumulation point of $Y$, and Part (a) follows from Theorem
  I. Lemma 2.1 (p. 72, quoted): "(a) If $m\ge q-1$, then $y_{k+1}-y_k\le1$
  for every $k$. (b) If $m\le q-1$, then $y_{k+1}-y_k\ge1$ for every $k$.
  Furthermore, $y_{k+1}-y_k=1$ for infinitely many $k$ and hence
  $\liminf(y_{k+1}-y_k)=1$." Lemma 2.2 (p. 73): if the set of all
  differences $u_k-u_l$ of one increasing sequence has a finite
  accumulation point and another, $(v_k)$, tends to infinity with gaps
  bounded above, then an increasing sequence containing all the sums
  $u_k+v_l$ has $\liminf$ of its gaps equal to 0. Part (b) of
  Theorem II (p. 74) applies Lemma 2.2 with $u=y^{q,n}$, $n=[q-q^{-1}]'$,
  and $v=y^{q,p}$, $p=[q-1]'$. Proposition 2.3 (p. 74): for $m\le(q-1)/2$,
  $Y^{q,m}$ has no finite accumulation point.
- § 3, Study of $\limsup(y_{k+1}-y_k)$; proof of Theorems III and IV
  (pp. 74--80). For a sequence $A=(A_i)$ of nonnegative integers,
  $y_k^{q,A}$ is the increasing sequence of the sums with
  $\varepsilon_i\in\{0,1,\ldots,A_i\}$; sums of an $A$-term and a $B$-term
  lie in the $(A+B)$-sequence. Lemma 3.1 (p. 75, quoted): "If $B$ is
  periodical with period $d$ and if $q^d\le B_a+1$ for some index $a$, then
  $y_{k+1}^{q,B}-y_k^{q,B}\le q^a$ for all $k$." Lemma 3.2 (pp. 75--76,
  "the following important" lemma): for periodic $A,B,C,D$ with (3.1)
  $Y^{q,A}=\{y_l^{q,A}-y_k^{q,A}\}$ having a finite accumulation point,
  (3.2) and (3.3) the gaps of $y^{q,B}$ and $y^{q,C}$ bounded, and (3.4)
  $A+B+C\le D$, the gaps $y_{k+1}^{q,D}-y_k^{q,D}\to0$. Its proof
  (pp. 76--77) has three steps: from Lemma 2.2, a subsequence $(z_i)$ of
  $y^{q,A+B}$ with pairwise disjoint power supports and paired differences
  $z_{2i}-z_{2i-1}$ in $[\delta',q^M\delta')$; from it, finite chains
  $w_0<\cdots<w_j$ in $y^{q,A+B}$ with steps below $\delta$ and total length
  above any $\Delta$; then, with the bounded gaps of $y^{q,C}$, every
  interval of length $\delta$ far enough out meets $y^{q,A+B+C}$, hence
  $y^{q,D}$. Proof of Theorem III (p. 77): Lemma 3.2 with the constant
  sequences $A\equiv[q-q^{-1}]'$, $B=C\equiv[q-1]'$, $D\equiv m$, (3.1) by
  Part (b) of Theorem I and (3.2)--(3.3) by Lemma 2.1 (a). Proof of
  Theorem IV (pp. 77--78): see
  [[number_theory/erdos_komornik_1998_developments_non_integer_bases/theorem_iv|theorem_iv]].
  Proposition 3.3 (pp. 78--79): for $m\le q-q^{-1}$, $y_{k+1}-y_k=1$ for
  infinitely many $k$, so $\limsup(y_{k+1}-y_k)\ge1$ (the open unit
  intervals $I_j=(m(1+q^2+\cdots+q^{2j})-1,\,m(1+q^2+\cdots+q^{2j}))$
  contain no $y_k$). Proposition 3.4 (p. 80): for $q=\sqrt{m+1}$ with $m+1$
  not a square, some $c>0$ has every interval $(x-cx^{-1},x+cx^{-1})$ with
  $x$ large meeting $y^{q,m}$, by the quadratic-irrational approximation
  theorem cited to Khintchine [9], Theorem 26.
- § 4, Universal developments; proof of Theorem V (pp. 80--82). Lemma 4.1:
  if $y_{k+1}-y_k\to0$ and $0<\alpha\le1$, every finite block
  $\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_N$ of digits extends to the left to a finite
  digit string whose value falls within $q^{-n-N}$ below $\alpha$. The proof
  of Theorem V (pp. 81--82) applies it in turn to an enumeration
  $B_1,B_2,\ldots$ of all finite variations, with the rescaled remainders
  $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots$ in $(0,1)$, and lets the construction run to
  infinity.
- References (p. 83), nine items, listed above.

**Bears on.** [[../wiki/problems/number_theory/E1096/_index|#1096]]: Theorem IV (printed
p. 59, PDF p. 3), the gaps $y_{k+1}-y_k$ tending to 0 for every $m\ge1$
when $1<q\le2^{1/4}$ and $q$ is not the square root of the second Pisot
number, with $m=1$ is the problem's sequence and question: the
introduction (p. 57) names the question as [4], Problem 4, the 1990
Bulletin paper's Problem 4, and says "One of the purposes of this paper is
to give an affirmative answer to this question", announcing the $m=1$ case
there for every $q$ between 1 and $2^{1/4}$ other than $\sqrt{p_2}$, whose
value it prints as $\approx1.175$. Every $q$ in $(1,\sqrt{p_2})$ is
covered, so the problem's $\epsilon$ may be any number at most
$\sqrt{p_2}-1\approx0.175$, and every $q$ in $(\sqrt{p_2},2^{1/4}]$ as
well. Of the two second-hand
accounts the problem page recorded, Feng's ($1<q\le2^{1/4}$ with the
possible exception of $\sqrt{p_2}$) is the printed statement, and the
site's ($1<q<\sqrt{q_1}\approx1.175$) is its part below the excluded point.
The proof (pp. 77--78) covers $q=\sqrt{p_1}\approx1.151$, where Feng's
Theorem 1.4 is silent, by a separate case through $q^3$. The paper's
[[number_theory/erdos_komornik_1998_developments_non_integer_bases/theorem_i|Theorem I]]
(b) (p. 58), applied with $m=1$ at the base $q^2$ (at $q^3$ for
$q=\sqrt{p_1}$), supplies the first hypothesis of Lemma 3.2 in that proof
(p. 78); Theorem I and Lemma 3.2 were read for structure only. The other
main results are context for the problem, not resolutions of it:
[[number_theory/erdos_komornik_1998_developments_non_integer_bases/theorem_ii|Theorem II]]
(a) (p. 59) gives $\liminf(y_{k+1}-y_k)>0$ for every $m$, hence for $m=1$,
at every Pisot $q$, and every Pisot number is at least $q_0\approx1.3247$,
above the range of Theorem IV, while Part (b) never applies with $m=1$;
[[number_theory/erdos_komornik_1998_developments_non_integer_bases/theorem_iii|Theorem III]]
(p. 59) never applies for $m=1$ or $m=2$, as the paper says; and
[[number_theory/erdos_komornik_1998_developments_non_integer_bases/theorem_v|Theorem V]]
(p. 60) takes the gaps tending to 0 as its hypothesis and draws universal
developments from it.
