# ramsey_theory/erdos_1997_some_recent_problems_results_graph_theory

[[ramsey_theory/_index|..]]

[[ramsey_theory/erdos_1997_some_recent_problems_results_graph_theory/problem_10|problem_10]]: Erdős's item 10, the statement that with Rousseau and Schelp he proved
that a two-coloring of the edges of the complete graph on n vertices leaves
at most n squared over four edges on no monochromatic triangle for large n;
the unpublished result Problem 639 records, printed without proof.

[[ramsey_theory/erdos_1997_some_recent_problems_results_graph_theory/problem_14|problem_14]]: Erdős's item 14, the Erdős–Faudree–Ordman question whether every
two-coloring of the edges of the complete graph on n vertices has
(1+o(1)) n squared over twelve edge-disjoint monochromatic triangles, with
the one-color question and the expectation (1+ε) n squared over 24; the
origin wording of Problem 76.

[[ramsey_theory/erdos_1997_some_recent_problems_results_graph_theory/problem_9|problem_9]]: Erdős's item 9, the question whether a family of finite graphs forcing
a monochromatic triangle under every finite number of colors must, for
every infinite cardinal, contain the finite subgraphs of a graph forcing
one under that many colors; the original wording of Problem 638.

## Contents

The opening (p. 81) announces two of Erdős's favorite problems on which
important progress had recently been made and says that the remaining
questions are probably less well known. The items are numbered 1 to 16
without a heading word; the later literature calls them "Problem 10",
"Problem 14".

- Item 1 (p. 81, page image): the Erdős--Faber--Lovász conjecture, made
  with Faber and Lovász more than twenty years earlier, quoted as posed:
  "if $G_i$, $1\le i\le n$ are $n$ edge disjoint complete graphs of size
  $n$ then $\bigcup_{i=1}^nG_i$ has chromatic number $n$." Erdős records
  that the conjecture proved hard, that he had long offered a prize for a
  proof or disproof, and that about three years earlier Jeff Kahn had shown
  the chromatic number of $\bigcup_{i=1}^nG_i$ to be at most $n(1+o(1))$,
  earning a consolation prize and the hope that he would eventually reach
  $n$. Two variants are asked: how large the
  chromatic number of $\bigcup_{i=1}^nG_i$ can be when every $G_i\cap G_j$
  is triangle-free, or when every $G_i\cap G_j$ has at most one edge.
- Item 2 (pp. 81--82): strong and weak $\Delta$-systems (sunflowers);
  $g_r^{(s)}(n)$ and $g_r^{(w)}(n)$; Erdős and Rado's $2^n<g_3^{(s)}(n)<2^nn!$;
  the conjecture $g_r^{(s)}(n)<C_r^n$ (1) with a prize offered for
  $r=3$; Spencer's $g_3^{(s)}(n)<(1+\varepsilon)^nn!$; Abbott and Hanson's
  $g_3^{(s)}(n)>10^{n/2}$; Kostochka's
  $g_3^{(s)}(n)<C_3^nn!/(\log\log n/\log\log\log n)^n$ (2), rewarded with a
  consolation prize; Axenovich, Fon-Der-Flaass and Kostochka's
  $g_3^{(w)}(n)<n!^{(1/2)+\varepsilon}$ (3); the infinite $\Delta$-system
  papers with Rado and Milner.
- Item 3 (p. 82): if every $m$-vertex subgraph of $G(n)$ has an independent
  set of size at least $\lfloor m/2\rfloor-k$, is $V=V_1\cup V_2\cup V_3$
  with $V_1$, $V_2$ independent and $|V_3|<f(k)$? Open even for $k=0$; the
  chromatic number is bounded; the Erdős--Hajnal graphs of infinite
  chromatic number with independent sets of size $>(m/2)(1-\varepsilon)$
  and $>(m/2)-f(m)$ in every $m$-vertex subgraph.
- Item 4 (p. 82): the Erdős--Hajnal--Szemerédi problem: if $f(n)\to\infty$
  arbitrarily slowly, is there a graph of infinite chromatic number in which,
  for every $n$, the subgraph spanned by any $n$ vertices becomes bipartite
  after deleting fewer than $f(n)$ of its edges? Unproved even for
  $f(n)=n^\lambda$ with $\lambda$ small; the analogue for chromatic number
  $m>\aleph_0$ with $nf(n)$, or perhaps $cn$, deletions; and $o(n)$
  deletions are known not to be enough, because chromatic number
  $\ge\aleph_1$ forces $\aleph_1$ pairwise vertex-disjoint odd cycles sharing
  a single length $2r+1$.
- Item 5 (pp. 82--83): with Faudree, the number $g(n)$ of sequences
  $3\le a_1<\cdots<a_k\le n$ (4) of cycle lengths occurring in $n$-vertex
  graphs: $g(n)<2^{n-2}$, $g(n)>2^{n/2}$, "very likely" $g(n)^{1/n}\to c$
  (5), and the unproved $g(n)/2^{n/2}\to\infty$, $g(n)/2^n\to0$ (6);
  Flandrin, Jackson, Lesniak and Schelp's $n^{1/5}$ even lengths when all
  odd lengths occur.
- Item 6 (p. 83, page image): a graph is called trivial when it is
  complete or empty (Erdős attributes the term to Bollobás), so that
  Ramsey's theorem says every $G(n)$ contains a trivial subgraph of size
  $c\log n$; the exact $c$ is unknown, the printed bounds being
  $\frac{\log2}2\le c\le2\log2$. Writing $k(n)$ for the largest $k$ such
  that every $G(n)$ has a trivial subgraph on $k$ vertices, Erdős asserts
  that no doubt $\frac{k(n)}{\log n}\to c$ (7), and then, quoted: "I offer
  100 dollars for a proof and 250 dollars for the value of $c$. I offer 1000
  dollars for a disproof of (7), but this is cheating since (7) clearly
  holds." Then the question with Fajtlowicz and Stanton: if $H(n)$ is the
  largest $h$ such that every $n$-vertex graph has a regular induced
  subgraph on $h$ vertices, is $H(n)/\log n\to\infty$ (8)? Bollobás's
  $H(n)<c\sqrt n$, $H(5)=3$, and "surely" $H(n)-k(n)\to\infty$ (9). A
  filing observation, not a review verdict: (7) is the inverse form of the
  Ramsey limit, since if $R(k)^{1/k}\to C$ then $k(n)/\log n\to1/\log C$,
  and the known $\sqrt2\le C\le4$ gives $1/(2\log2)\le c\le2/\log2$ with
  natural logarithms ($1/2\le c\le2$ with base $2$); the printed bounds
  equal the base-$2$ reading, since $\log2=1$ there, and with natural
  logarithms they are the reciprocals of the correct bounds; they are
  recorded as printed.
- Item 7 (p. 83, page image): the conjecture with V.T. Sós and Faudree,
  quoted as posed: "if $G(n)$ is a graph which contains no trivial subgraph
  of size $c\log n$ then it contains at least $cn^{5/2}$ induced subgraphs
  $G_1,G_2,\ldots,G_t$, where any two of our $G$'s differ either in the
  number of vertices or in the number of edges." Their own proof reaches
  only $cn^{3/2}$, and Erdős adds that $cn^{5/2}$ could not be improved if
  the conjecture holds. The second conjecture, quoted: "our $G(n)$ has an
  induced subgraph of size $c_1n$ where the vertices have $c_2n^{1/2}$
  different degrees"; an earlier Erdős--Hajnal result guarantees only that
  the number of distinct degree values grows to infinity with $n$. Both
  conjectures grew out of a problem of Alon and Bollobás. The first
  conjecture is the site's Problem 636.
- Item 8 (p. 83, page image), the conjecture quoted as printed: "Brandon,
  McKay and I conjectured that if $G(n;cn^2)$ has no trivial subgraph of
  size $>c\log n$ then for every $t<\varepsilon n^2$ our $G$ has an induced
  subgraph of exactly $t$ edges." Erdős adds, with some annoyance, that
  they could prove it only for $t<c(\log n)^2$. The comma after "Brandon"
  is printed; the 1992 Catania paper writes "Brandon Mc Kay", one person.
- Item 9 (pp. 83--84, page images): the infinite-cardinal triangle question,
  quoted on
  [[ramsey_theory/erdos_1997_some_recent_problems_results_graph_theory/problem_9|problem_9]].
- Item 10 (p. 84, page image): the Erdős--Rousseau--Schelp statement on
  edges in no monochromatic triangle, quoted on
  [[ramsey_theory/erdos_1997_some_recent_problems_results_graph_theory/problem_10|problem_10]].
- Item 11 (p. 84, page image): every $G(n;\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor+1)$ has at
  least $2n^2/9$ edges lying in an odd cycle, and $2n^2/9$ is best
  possible; then the conjecture, quoted: "Perhaps there are at least
  $2n^2/9$ edges which occur in a pentagon." Erdős notes that this would be
  implied if every $G(n;(n^2/4)+1)$ had a triangle with $(n/2)-O(1)$
  vertices each adjacent to two or more of its three vertices. Erdős
  and Faudree ("Fandree" is printed) observed that in every $G(2n;n^2+1)$
  some triangle has at least $n+2$ vertices joined to it, and that $n+2$ is
  best possible. The first sentence is stated as a result with no
  attribution or reference; Grzesik, Hu and Volec (arXiv:1605.09055v3, PDF
  p. 2) and the site's Problem 608 commentary credit it to Erdős, citing
  this paper; Problem 608 cites Erdős, Faudree and Rousseau's 1992 paper
  [EFR92], which was not read here, for the triangle count
  $2\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$ and the $C_{2k+1}$ conjecture.
- Item 12 (p. 84): the Erdős--Hajnal questions: is there $f(k)$ such that
  chromatic number $\ge f(k)$ forces an odd cycle whose vertex set spans a
  subgraph of chromatic number $\ge k$; and does chromatic number $\ge F(r)$
  force $r$ edge-disjoint cycles on the same vertex set ("more difficult
  (and more interesting)").
- Item 13 (p. 84): Bollobás, Chen and Erdős: every $G(n;c_kn)$ contains $k$
  edge-disjoint cycles $C_i$ with $V(C_{i+1})\subseteq V(C_i)$; $f(n)$, the
  least size forcing two edge-disjoint cycles on the same vertex set, has
  $f(n)/n\to\infty$; Hamburger and Szegedy's question whether some
  $G(n;cn)$ always contains a cycle with at least as many diagonals as
  vertices.
- Item 14 (p. 84, page image): the Erdős--Faudree--Ordman edge-disjoint
  monochromatic triangles question, quoted on
  [[ramsey_theory/erdos_1997_some_recent_problems_results_graph_theory/problem_14|problem_14]].
- Item 15 (pp. 84--85): with Katona, $f(n;t)$, the least number of $t$-subsets
  of an $n$-set forcing $A_1,A_2,A_3,A_4$ with
  $A_1\cap A_2=A_3\cap A_4=\emptyset$ and $A_1\cup A_2=A_3\cup A_4$ (10);
  $f(n;2)=(\frac12+o(1))n^{3/2}$ (no $C_4$); Füredi's proof of $f(n;3)<cn^2$
  (11) and $f(n,3)>\binom n2$ for infinitely many $n$; the conjecture
  $f(n;3)<\binom n2+1$ with equality infinitely often; Füredi's
  $f(n;t)<3.5\binom n{t-1}$ and "perhaps $f(n;t)=(1+o(1))\binom n{t-1}$".
- Item 16 (p. 85): from [1], $f(k,r,s)$, the least size of a transversal
  of a family of $k$-sets every $r$ of which have a transversal of $s$
  elements: $f(k,3,2)=2k$, $f(k,4,2)=\lceil3k/2\rceil$,
  $f(k,5,2)=\lceil5k/4\rceil$ (printed with square brackets, $[3k/2]$ and
  $[5k/4]$, which the site reads as floors; read here as ceilings because
  floors fail at $k=3$: all 3-subsets of a 7-set satisfy the condition for
  $r=4$ and need 5 points, and all 3-subsets of a 6-set satisfy it for $r=5$
  and need 4), $f(k,6,2)=k$ ("quite tricky for odd $k$");
  the expectations $f(k,7,2)=(1+o(1))\frac34k$ and
  $f(k,r,2)=(1+o(1))c_rk$; the case $s>2$ is left uninvestigated.

## Relation to E642

This source bears on [[../wiki/problems/extremal_graph_theory/E0642/_index|Problem 642]].

For E642, a cycle's diagonals are edges of the ambient graph joining
nonconsecutive vertices of that cycle, so the cycle Hamburger and Szegedy
ask for in item 13 (p. 84), one with at least as many diagonals as
vertices, is exactly a cycle forbidden by E642. If $F(n)$ denotes E642's
maximum number of edges in an $n$-vertex graph in which every cycle has
fewer diagonals than vertices, the question there asks whether $F(n)=O(n)$;
equivalently, for $n\ge5$, the first edge count forcing such a cycle is
$F(n)+1$.

The nested-cycle theorem of Bollobás, Chen and Erdős reported in item 13,
that for each fixed $k$ some $c_k$ makes every $G(n;c_kn)$ contain $k$
edge-disjoint cycles with nested vertex sets, does not supply that bound:
containment of one cycle's vertex set in another's need not give enough
diagonals. Two edge-disjoint cycles on the *same* vertex set would suffice,
since every edge of one cycle is a diagonal of the other, giving at least
as many diagonals as vertices; but item 13 says that forcing such a pair
requires a threshold whose ratio to $n$ tends to infinity. The paper
collects these attributed results and poses the question: it identifies
E642's precise linear-threshold question and a sufficient, stronger
configuration, but does not answer it.
