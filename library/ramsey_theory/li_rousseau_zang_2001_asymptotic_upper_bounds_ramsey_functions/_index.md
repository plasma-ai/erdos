## Contents

- Abstract and § 1, Introduction (pp. 123--124, page images). The abstract
  states the two results: for a graph $G$ on $N$ vertices with average
  degree $d$ in which every neighborhood induces a subgraph of average
  degree at most $a$, the independence number is at least $Nf_{a+1}(d)$,
  with $f_{a+1}(d)=\int_0^1((1-t)^{1/(a+1)})/(a+1+(d-a-1)t)\,dt$; and from
  this, for fixed $k$ and $l$,
  $r(K_k+\bar K_l,K_n)\le(l+o(1))n^k/(\log n)^{k-1}$, and in particular
  $r(K_k,K_n)\le(1+o(1))n^{k-1}/(\log n)^{k-2}$. Key words: Ramsey number,
  Independence number, Average degree, Convex function. Definitions
  (p. 123): $r(F_1,F_2)$ is the least $N$ such that every graph $G$ on $N$
  vertices contains $F_1$ or has $F_2$ in its complement $\bar G$, and
  $r(k,n)$ abbreviates $r(K_k,K_n)$. Recalled bounds (p. 123): the
  Erdős--Szekeres bound $r(k,n)\le\binom{n+k-2}{k-1}$ [6]; for fixed $k$ and
  large $n$, the Ajtai--Komlós--Szemerédi bounds [1] $r(3,n)\le100n^2/\log n$
  and $r(k,n)\le(5000)^kn^{k-1}/(\log n)^{k-2}$ for $k\ge4$; Griggs's [7]
  reduction of the coefficient 100 to 2.4 and Bollobás's [2] of $(5000)^k$
  to $2(20)^{k-3}$; and Shearer's [9] $r(3,n)\le(1+o(1))n^2/\log n$ as
  $n\to\infty$. The authors announce that a special case of their main
  result gives $r(k,n)\le(1+o(1))n^{k-1}/(\log n)^{k-2}$ for $k\ge1$, which
  improves the earlier bounds when $k\ge4$. The route (p. 123): an upper
  bound on $r(k,n)$ follows from a lower bound on the independence number
  of $K_k$-free graphs; Turán gives $\alpha(G)\ge N/(1+d)$, Ajtai, Komlós
  and Szemerédi give $\alpha(G)\ge N\log d/(100d)$ for triangle-free $G$
  (p. 124), and Shearer gives $\alpha(G)\ge Nf(d)$ with
  $f(x)=(x\log x-x+1)/(x-1)^2\sim(\log x)/x$. The paper's plan is to
  generalize Shearer's inequality so that it depends on an upper bound for
  the average degree of every neighborhood subgraph, and to turn that into
  the improvement of $r(k,n)$ for $k\ge4$. Notation (p. 124): $G_v$ is the
  subgraph of $G$ induced by the neighborhood of $v$, and
  $f_m(x)=\int_0^1\frac{(1-t)^{1/m}}{m+(x-m)t}\,dt$.
- Theorem 1 (p. 124, quoted): "Let $G$ be a graph with $N$ vertices and
  average degree $d$. If for any vertex $v$ of $G$, the average degree of
  $G_v$ is at most $a$, then $\alpha(G)\ge Nf_{a+1}(d)$." The case $a=0$
  (triangle-free $G$, every $G_v$ edgeless) is Shearer's inequality, since
  $f_1(x)=\int_0^1(1-t)/(1+(x-1)t)\,dt$ is Shearer's $f(x)$; this
  identification is made here, not in the paper.
- Theorem 2 (p. 124, quoted): "Let $k$ and $l$ be any two fixed integers.
  Then, as $n\to\infty$,
  $r(K_k+\bar K_l,K_n)\le(l+o(1))\frac{n^k}{(\log n)^{k-1}}$." Here
  $K_k+\bar K_l$ is the join of a $k$-clique with $l$ independent vertices;
  $K_1+\bar K_l=K_{1,l}$ (p. 126) and $K_k+\bar K_1=K_{k+1}$. Paged at
  [[ramsey_theory/li_rousseau_zang_2001_asymptotic_upper_bounds_ramsey_functions/theorem_2|theorem_2]].
- § 2, The Proofs: the Lemma and the Corollary (pp. 124--125, page
  images). Lemma (quoted): "For $m\ge1$ and $x\ge0$, the function
  $f_m(x)=\int_0^1\frac{(1-t)^{1/m}}{m+(x-m)t}\,dt$ satisfies the
  differential equation $x(x-m)f_m'(x)+(x+1)f_m(x)=1$. (1) Moreover,
  $f_m(x)$ is completely monotonic on $(0,\infty)$, that is,
  $(-1)^kf_m^{(k)}(x)\ge0$ for all $k\ge0$ and $x\ge0$. In particular,
  $f_m$ is positive, decreasing, and convex." Proof (pp. 124--125,
  structure only): differentiation under the integral and integration by
  parts give $x(x-m)f_m'(x)=-xf_m(x)+1-f_m(x)$; complete monotonicity "can
  be seen by repeated differentiating under the integral". Corollary
  (p. 125, quoted): "The following two statements hold. $f_m(x)\le1/(x+1)$
  if $x\le m$; $\ge1/(x+1)$ otherwise. For $m\ge1$,
  $f_m(x)\ge\int_0^1\frac{(1-t)}{m+(x-m)t}\,dt
  =\frac{x\log(x/m)-(x-m)}{(x-m)^2}$."
  No proof is printed for the Corollary. The closed form is the value of
  its integral with the natural logarithm, so $\log$ is the natural
  logarithm throughout the paper; the constant $1+o(1)$ of Theorem 2 and of
  the concluding remark is read with that base.
- Proof of Theorem 1 (pp. 125--126, page images, structure only). Induction
  on $N$. If $N\le a+2$ then $d\le a+1$, and Turán's theorem with
  $1/(d+1)\ge f_{a+1}(d)$ gives the bound; so $N>a+2$ and $d>a+1$. If some
  vertex has degree $N-1$, Turán on $G_v$ gives
  $\alpha(G)\ge\alpha(G_v)\ge\frac{N-1}{a+1}\ge\frac N{a+2}=Nf_{a+1}(a+1)\ge
  Nf_{a+1}(d)$ (2); so the maximum degree is at most $N-2$. With
  $P(v)=\deg(v)+1$ and $Q(v)$ the number of edges incident with $v$ or a
  neighbor of $v$, the hypothesis on $G_v$ gives
  $Q(v)\ge\sum_{vw\in E(G)}\deg(w)-\frac a2\deg(v)$ and an average of $Q$
  at least $d^2-ad/2$; the weight
  $R(v)=1+[P(v)d-2Q(v)]f_{a+1}'(d)-P(v)f_{a+1}(d)$ has average at least
  $1-d(d-a-1)f_{a+1}'(d)-(d+1)f_{a+1}(d)=0$ "by (1)", so some $v_0$ has
  $R(v_0)\ge0$ (3). Deleting $v_0$ and its neighbors leaves $H$ with
  $N-\hat P$ vertices and $Nd/2-\hat Q$ edges; the induction hypothesis,
  $\alpha(G)\ge1+\alpha(H)$ and the convexity of $f_{a+1}$ (the tangent
  inequality $f_{a+1}(x)\ge f_{a+1}(d)+f_{a+1}'(d)(x-d)$) give
  $\alpha(G)\ge Nf_{a+1}(d)$ by (3). The printed step applies the induction
  hypothesis to $H$ without checking that, for each vertex $u$ of $H$, the
  graph $H_u$ induced by the neighborhood of $u$ in $H$ has average degree
  at most $a$; $H_u$ is an induced subgraph of $G_u$, and average degree
  does not pass to induced subgraphs (a triangle with three isolated
  vertices has average degree 1, the triangle alone 2). The argument goes
  through when every $G_v$ has maximum degree at most $a$, a hypothesis
  $H$ inherits and the one the proof of Theorem 2 supplies (p. 126), so
  Theorem 2 is unaffected. This observation is made here, not in the paper.
- Proof of Theorem 2 (pp. 126--127, page images, read in full). Induction
  on $k$. The base $k=1$ is the star $K_1+\bar K_l=K_{1,l}$, for which the
  paper cites Chvátal's theorem [5], $r(K_{1,l},K_n)=l(n-1)+1$. For the
  step, with
  $R(k,l;n)=r(K_k+\bar K_l,K_n)$, take $G$ of order $N=R(k+1,l;n)-1$ with
  no $K_{k+1}+\bar K_l$ and $\alpha(G)\le n-1$: each vertex has degree at
  most $R(k,l;n)-1$, and each $G_v$ has maximum, hence average, degree at
  most $R(k-1,l;n)-1$, so Theorem 1 and the Lemma give
  $n>\alpha(G)\ge Nf_m(R(k,l;n)-1)\ge Nf_m(R(k,l;n))$ (4) with
  $m=R(k-1,l;n)$. For $0<\epsilon<1$ the Corollary gives $M$ with
  $f_m(x)>(1-\epsilon)\log(x/m)/x$ whenever $x/m>M$. The large $n$ are
  split into the $n'$ with $R(k,l;n')/R(k-1,l;n')>(n')^{1-\epsilon}$ (a
  parenthesis notes that every large $n$ is an $n'$ when $k=2$) and the
  $n''$ with the reverse inequality. For $n'$, (4) gives
  $n'>(1-\epsilon)^2N\log n'/R(k,l;n')$, hence
  $N\le\frac{n'}{(1-\epsilon)^2}\frac{R(k,l;n')}{\log n'}$, and the bound
  for $R(k+1,l;n')$ follows from the induction hypothesis on $R(k,l;n')$.
  For $n''$, $f_m(x)\ge1/(1+x)$ for $x\ge m$ gives
  $N\le n''[1+R(k,l;n'')]\le n''[1+(n'')^{1-\epsilon}R(k-1,l;n'')]$, and
  the bound follows from the induction hypothesis on $R(k-1,l;n'')$
  because $(n'')^{2-\epsilon}$ is below $(n''/\log n'')^2$ once $n''$ is
  large. The induction hypothesis is assumed for $1,2,\ldots,k$, and the
  $n''$ case uses it at $k-1$.
- § 3, Concluding Remarks (p. 127, page image). The section opens with the
  specialization of the main result, quoted: "for any fixed $k$,
  $r(k,n)\le(1+o(1))n^{k-1}/(\log n)^{k-2}$ as $n\to\infty$", which is
  Theorem 2 at $l=1$ with $k+1$ written as $k$. It then remarks that at
  $k=2$ this upper bound is trivially the asymptotic formula; recalls Kim's
  lower bound $cn^2/\log n$ for $r(3,n)$ [8] and, on the strength of it and
  the known exact values, states the authors' belief, quoted, "that the
  asymptotic formula of $r(3,n)$ is $n^2/(\log n)$"; recalls that Bollobás
  and Erdős [3] asked whether $r(K_1+K_{1,l},K_n)$ really grows linearly in
  $l$, and poses the same question for the paper's upper bound on
  $r(K_k+\bar K_l,K_n)$; and records, quoted, that "Erdős [4] conjectured
  in 1947 that $r(k,n)\ge cn^{k-1}/(\log n)^{c(k)}$" and that Spencer [11]
  proved $r(k,n)\ge c(n/\log n)^{(k+1)/2}$. The closing remark is that if
  Erdős's conjecture holds, the proof of Theorem 2 yields
  $R(k+1,l;n)\le(1+o(1))R(k,l;n)\frac n{\log n}$ as $n\to\infty$. The 1947
  conjecture is the statement of Problem 986, in the paper's letters,
  attributed through Chung's 1997 problem list.
- What the paper does not print. No explicit threshold $n_0$ or rate for
  the $o(1)$ of Theorem 2 is given; the $o(1)$ depends on $k$ and $l$ and
  on the $\epsilon$ of the proof. The Corollary carries no proof. The
  paper gives no lower bound of its own and states no result for $k$ or
  $l$ growing with $n$.
