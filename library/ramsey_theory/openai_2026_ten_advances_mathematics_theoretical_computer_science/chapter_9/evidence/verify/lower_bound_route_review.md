## Lemma 2.1: saturated matrices

### Restatement

For every integer `H>=2`, let `m=ceil(2H log H)` and `s=m(m+1)+1`.
There exists one `[H]`-valued matrix with row set `[s]` and column set
`[H]^m`, such that every set of exactly `m+1` distinct columns has at least
one row displaying all `H` symbols. The witnessing row may vary with the
column set; the matrix cannot.

### Independent derivation of the vulnerable steps

1. For a specified row and column set, each symbol is absent with probability
   `(1-1/H)^(m+1)`. The event that some symbol is absent has probability at
   most `H(1-1/H)^(m+1)<H exp(-m/H)<=1/H`. The first union bound requires no
   independence between the different missing-symbol events.
2. For one fixed column set, row-failure events depend on disjoint independent
   entries, so their joint probability is at most `H^(-s)`. Different column
   sets overlap and are not independent, but no such independence is needed:
   there are at most `H^(m(m+1))` of them, and their bad-event union has
   probability at most `H^(m(m+1)-s)=1/H<1`.
3. The sample space of matrices is finite and nonempty. A bad-event union of
   probability less than one leaves at least one matrix outside it, proving
   simultaneous satisfaction rather than a separately chosen matrix for each
   column set. This is the exact uniform property used in Lemma 2.2.

The strongest attempted attack was overlapping column sets: multiplying their
failure probabilities would be invalid. The proof multiplies only across
independent rows and then uses a union bound across sets, so the attack fails.
The smallest permitted alphabet `H=2` still has the strict margin `1/H<1`;
there is no limiting or large-`H` qualification.

## Lemma 2.2: fixed two-sided coordinate cover

### Restatement

For every integer `H>=2`, with `m,s` as above, there exist two functions
`f,g:[H]^s -> [H]^s` chosen before any input pair, such that every
`x,y in [H]^s` has some coordinate `d in [s]` satisfying
`x_d=f(y)_d` or `y_d=g(x)_d`. Each function sees only its indicated word.

### Independent derivation of the vulnerable steps

1. Fix a saturated matrix. For any `y`, the prefixes whose columns avoid
   `y_r` in every row form a set `E_y`. If it contained `m+1` different
   prefixes, saturation on those columns would put `y_r` in one of them at a
   witnessing row, contradicting avoidance. Thus `|E_y|<=m`, for every `y`.
2. Fix an ordering of all prefixes before defining `f`. Assign each listed
   exceptional prefix `z^(d)` to its own coordinate `d<=m`, and set that
   coordinate of `f(y)` to `z^(d)_d`. These are valid input coordinates
   because `m<=s`; no distinctness of their symbol values is needed. Fill
   unused coordinates with `1`, including all coordinates when `E_y` is empty.
3. Set `g(x)` to the matrix column indexed by the first `m` coordinates of
   `x`. If that prefix is exceptional, its assigned coordinate guarantees
   the `f` equality. Otherwise failure of universal avoidance gives a row
   with the `g` equality. The alternatives are exhaustive and use the same
   globally defined maps for all pairs.

The strongest attack was a hidden dependence of `f` on `x`, or a coordinate
index exceeding the prefix length. The ordering depends only on `y`, and the
exceptional-set cardinality bounds every assigned index by `m`. This rules out
both failures, even when `|E_y|=m` or `E_y` is empty.

## Lemma 2.3: separated palettes

### Restatement

For every integer `H>=3`, define `m,s` as before,
`ell=ceil(log H)`, and `t=s ell`. Thus `ell>=2` and `t>=2s`.
For every integer `1<=j<=H`, some nonempty family of `t`-element subsets
of `[jt]` has pairwise differences of at least `s` elements in each
direction. Its cardinality `B_j` obeys both

$$
B_j\geq\frac{\binom{jt}{t}}
 {\sum_{d=0}^{s-1}\binom{t}{d}\binom{(j-1)t}{d}}
 \quad\text{and}\quad
B_j\geq\frac{j^t}{s(e^2j\ell^2)^s}.
$$

Binomial coefficients with a larger lower index are zero.

### Independent derivation of the vulnerable steps

1. A maximal separated family exists in the nonempty finite ambient set.
   A `t`-set not within difference less than `s` of any chosen set could
   be adjoined, contradicting maximality. Therefore the difference balls
   cover. Around a fixed palette the shell of difference `d` has exactly
   `binom(t,d) binom((j-1)t,d)` elements: choose the deletions and additions.
   Overlap only reduces the union size, so the covering inequality has the
   claimed lower-bound direction. The `d=0` term makes division legitimate.
2. The numerator is at least `j^t`, since each factor
   `(jt-i)/(t-i)` for `0<=i<t` is at least `j`. For `d<s<=t/2`, increasing
   the ambient set and then the lower index gives
   `binom((j-1)t,d)<=binom(jt,s)` and `binom(t,d)<=binom(t,s)`.
   This deliberately does not rely on monotonicity inside row `(j-1)t`,
   which would be unsafe at `j=1`.
3. There are `s` shells. For `N>=s`, the product bound and the integral of
   `log x` give `binom(N,s)<=N^s/s!<=(eN/s)^s`. Hence the ball size is
   at most `s(e^2jt^2/s^2)^s=s(e^2j ell^2)^s`. This yields the second
   inequality. At `j=1`, the sole palette is `[t]`, the exact ball
   denominator is one, and the looser second bound is harmless.

The strongest attack was the boundary `j=1`, where the complement is empty
and a careless binomial monotonicity step would fail. The actual argument
passes to ambient size `jt` first and uses the zero convention, so its
inequalities remain valid. A separate attempted reversal of packing and
covering also fails: maximality supplies coverage by possibly overlapping
balls, exactly what is needed for a lower bound on their number.

## Proposition 3.1: the full recursive invariant

### Restatement

Fix any integer `H>=3`, its parameters above, and for each `1<=r<=H` a
palette family as in Lemma 2.3, with size `B_r`. Put `M_j=jt` and
`n_j=product_{r=1}^j B_r`, the empty product being one. For each integer
`0<=j<=H` there exists a coloring of every edge of `K_(n_j)` into
`[M_j]` with no monochromatic triangle, and each individual color graph
admits a proper vertex coloring into a set of at most `j+1` labels.

### Independent derivation of the vulnerable steps

1. At stage `j`, each palette's complement has exactly `M_(j-1)` colors,
   so copying and relabeling the previous construction is legitimate.
   Its full invariant supplies active-color labels in `[j]`, including
   colors having no internal edge. For a fixed ordered pair of palettes,
   choose distinct `a_d` and distinct `b_d` from the two opposite
   differences. The two lists are disjoint. The resulting label words lie
   in `[j]^s`, hence in `[H]^s`. Lemma 2.2 covers every edge pair, and the
   least-coordinate rule defines one undirected edge color. Outputs of
   `f` or `g` need not themselves lie in `[j]^s`; their equality guarantee
   is enough. Nothing requires all palette-complement colors to occur.
2. For a possible two-block monochromatic triangle, fix the outside vertex
   and the two inside vertices. If the common color is missing inside,
   the internal edge already has a different color. If it is active,
   that color occurs at a unique coordinate of the relevant list for
   this fixed block pair. Both inside labels equal the same function of
   the outside vertex, so properness forbids the internal edge. Reversing
   the block order changes which of `f` and `g` is used, not the
   conclusion. For three blocks, a color would have to change its
   palette-membership bit along each of the three edges, impossible on an
   odd cycle. This checks all vertex distributions of a triangle.
3. To propagate the stronger invariant, label active blocks internally by
   `[j]` and every missing block by the new label `j+1`. Internal edges
   are proper by induction. A cross edge of color `c` has opposite
   membership of `c` in its two palettes, so joins an active block to a
   missing block. Thus its labels differ. There are no `c` cross edges
   between two active blocks, so their independently chosen internal
   labels need not be coordinated. The `B_j` blocks give the stated
   product of vertex counts. The final `H+1` labels are never fed into a
   further stage requiring the alphabet `[H]`.

The strongest attack was a two-block triangle whose active-endpoint labels
depend on changing coordinate choices. Distinct colors within each fixed
pair's lists make the coordinate unique once the shared edge color is fixed;
the common outside endpoint then fixes both labels. Choices for other block
pairs do not enter this triangle. I also attacked the invariant by giving two
active blocks conflicting labels, but those blocks have no cross edge of that
color. Both attacks fail for structural reasons, not because examples were
sampled.

## Theorem 1.1: every color count and the infinite consequence

### Restatement

For each positive integer `k`, let `R_k(3)` be the least finite forcing
order for a monochromatic triangle in every edge coloring into a
`k`-element color set, with unused colors permitted. There is a single
real constant `c>0`, independent of `k`, such that for every integer
`k>=2`,

$$
R_k(3)\geq\left(\frac{c k^{1/3}}{\log k}\right)^k.
$$

Consequently the positive real `k`th roots tend to positive infinity, in
the full sequential sense: for every real threshold they eventually exceed
that threshold for every integer `k`, not just along a subsequence.

### Independent derivation of the three weakest steps

1. **The palette product gives the necessary exponential base.** Write
   `u=log H`, `ell=ceil u`, and `k_H=Hs ell`. Applying the proved
   palette inequalities at each stage gives

$$
N_H=\prod_{j=1}^H B_j
\geq \frac{(H!)^{t-s}}{s^H(e^2\ell^2)^{sH}}.
$$

   Taking logarithms and dividing by `Hs ell` yields

$$
\frac{\log N_H}{k_H}
\geq u-1-\frac{u+1+2\log\ell}{\ell}
       -\frac{\log s}{s\ell}.
$$

   Here the factorial estimate is `log(H!)>=Hu-H`, and its coefficient
   `1-1/ell` is positive. Since `ell>=2`, `u/ell<=1`,
   `2 log ell/ell<=1`, and `log s/(s ell)<=1/ell`, the total loss from
   `u` is at most `3+2/ell<=4`. Thus `N_H>0` and
   `N_H>=(e^(-4)H)^(k_H)` for every `H>=3`. Some factors in the lower
   palette product can be below one; this neither reverses the
   multiplication nor invalidates its logarithm. A base below one at
   small `H` merely gives a weak bound.

2. **Ceiling jumps cause only bounded loss after normalization.** The
   definitions imply

$$
4H^3u^3\leq k_H\leq18H^3u^3.
$$

   The lower bound uses `m>=2Hu`, `s>=m^2`, and `ell>=u`. The upper
   bound uses `m+1<=2Hu+2<=3Hu`, `s<=(m+1)^2`, and
   `ell<=u+1<=2u`. The sequence `k_H` is strictly increasing and
   unbounded. Denote the parameters at `H+1` by primes. Independently
   expanding the ceiling error using `log(1+1/H)<=1/H` gives

$$
m'\leq2Hu+2u+3+2/H\leq m+2u+4,
\qquad m'/m\leq1+3/H.
$$

   Since `s/m^2=1+1/m+1/m^2` decreases with `m`,
   `s'/s<=(m'/m)^2`. Also `ell'<=ell+1`. Therefore

$$
\frac{k_{H+1}}{k_H}
\leq(1+1/H)(1+3/H)^2(1+1/\ell)
\leq\exp(7/H+1/\ell)\leq\exp(8/u).
$$

   This includes a jump of either ceiling, or simultaneous jumps. For
   `k_H<=k<k_(H+1)`, set `alpha=k_H/k`. Then
   `alpha>=exp(-8/u)` and `(1-alpha)u<=8`. Since unused colors are
   allowed, monotonicity and the constructed coloring imply

$$
\frac{\log R_k(3)}k\geq\alpha(u-4)
=u-(1-\alpha)u-4\alpha\geq u-12.
$$

   This calculation never multiplies a lower bound for `alpha` by a
   possibly negative `u-4`. It is therefore valid at small `H` as well
   as eventually, eliminating a possible sign error in interpolation.

3. **Translate the parameter, cover the finite gap, and prove the limit.**
