**Claim.** For $r\ge2$ let $h(r)$ be the largest finite exact order of an
asymptotic basis of order at most $r$. The development states that this
maximum is attained for every $r$ and that

$$
\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{h(r)}{r^2}=\frac13,
$$

which would answer [[problems/additive_bases/E0336/_index|Problem 336]]. The
site's statement glosses a basis of order $r$ as one in which every large
integer is a sum of at most $r$ elements, so its $h(r)$ ranges over bases of
order at most $r$: this is the claimant's formulation, which the claimant
identifies with the function $X(r)$ of Erdős and Graham studied by Grekos, Nash
and Plagne. Plagne defines $X(h)$ as the largest exact order of $\mathcal
A\setminus\{a\}$, over exact bases $\mathcal A$ of integers bounded below of
order at most $h$ and elements $a$ whose removal leaves a basis. Adjoining $0$
gives $h(r)\le X(r)$; the reverse inequality is the claimant's argument. Erdős
and Graham's own notation of 1980 restricts the maximum to bases of order
exactly $r$ (their $g(r)$, with the order the least such $r$); for that function
the claim gives only $\limsup g(r)/r^2\le1/3$, since $g(r)\le h(r)$. This page
records the claimant's formulation. The claimant's bundle argues, in its
statement audit, that the problem's "order $r$" means order at most $r$, and
checks by padding representations with zero that its function equals $X(r)$; the
argument is the claimant's. The author says periodic bases give the lower bound;
that bound is prior art, as the site's remarks record: Grekos [Gr88] proved
$\liminf h(r)/r^2\ge1/3$ and Nash [Na93] $\limsup h(r)/r^2\le1/2$, with Erdős
and Graham [ErGr80b] before them bracketing the ratio between $1/4$ and $5/4$
and Plagne [Pl04] sharpening the lower-order terms. The new content is the
matching upper bound. By the author's account the question is transferred to a
finite cyclic group $G$; a search over dyadic scales locates one at which a
certain set has doubling constant less than $9/4$, which places the extremal
configuration in a lattice generated by two elements, and there an inequality
$3\lvert G\rvert\le(H+2)^2$ between the group's size and the exact order $H$ is
what produces $1/3$; the remaining cases are handled by classifying the
three-element sets with exactly five distinct pairwise sums. The author writes
that the route began from an argument they attribute to Lev (2020) and that
formalizing it exposed two gaps in that printed proof: a count of differences
that confuses ordered with unordered pairs, which the set
$\{0,1,3\}\subset\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ refutes, and an endpoint configuration
$\{0,x,2x\}$ the proof omits; the author supplies new arguments for both.
