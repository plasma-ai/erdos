**Claim.** Let $g(n)=\#\{m\ge1:\phi(m)=n\}$. Theorem 1.1 of the preprint: for
a fixed nonzero integer $a$ and every $\beta>15/(32\sqrt e)=0.2843\ldots$, at
least $x/(\log x)^{C}$ primes $p\in(x,2x]$ have every prime factor of $p-a$ at
most $x^{\beta}$. Corollary 1.3, by the Erdős--Pomerance transfer: the
integers $m$ with at least $m^{0.7156}$ solutions $n$ of $\phi(n)=m$ form an
infinite sequence $m_1<m_2<\cdots$ with $\log m_{i+1}/\log m_i\to1$. So

$$
g(n)\ge n^{0.7156}
$$

for infinitely many $n$, which answers the question of
[[problems/arithmetic_functions/E0821/_index|Problem 821]] for every
$\epsilon\ge0.2844$, since then $n^{1-\epsilon}\le n^{0.7156}$. The technical
input is Theorem 1.4, a mean value theorem for primes in arithmetic
progressions to quadrilinear moduli $qrst$ reaching moduli up to
$x^{17/32-\epsilon}$, beyond Maynard's $x^{11/21}$. The statements are on the
card
[[../library/arithmetic_functions/lichtman_2022_primes_arithmetic_progressions_large_moduli_shifted/_index|Lichtman 2022]];
the proof is not reconstructed in this repository. The fiber exponent
$1-15/(32\sqrt e)=0.7156\ldots$ improves the $0.7039$ of Baker and Harman
([[problems/arithmetic_functions/E0821/claims/1998_01_01_baker_harman|Baker and Harman 1998]]).

**Covers.** The range $\epsilon\ge0.2844$. Corollary 1.3 gives infinitely many
$n$ with $g(n)\ge n^{0.7156}$, a non-strict inequality; the strict one the
problem asks for follows from Theorem 1.1, which holds for every
$\beta>15/(32\sqrt e)=0.28434\ldots$: the same transfer at a $\beta$ in
$(15/(32\sqrt e),0.2844)$ gives infinitely many $n$ with
$g(n)\ge n^{1-\beta}>n^{0.7156}\ge n^{1-\epsilon}$. The question for smaller
$\epsilon$, and so the problem as posed, is not addressed.

**Depends on.** Nothing in this wiki.

**Standing.** Claimed. The arXiv record of arXiv:2211.09641 has one version,
submitted 14 November 2022, which dates this page, and lists no journal
reference; no journal record of the paper is known, so no `refereed` evidence
is listed. The site's commentary (page last edited 1 October 2025) gives
$g(n)>n^{0.71568\cdots}$ infinitely often as the best known bound and credits
it to Lichtman, but labels the problem OPEN, which is not an acceptance, so no
`reviewed` evidence is listed. The formal-conjectures statement file for the
problem states the bound as `erdos_821.variants.lichtman` (category research
solved) with a `sorry` body at the commit the problem page links; it is not a
formalization.
