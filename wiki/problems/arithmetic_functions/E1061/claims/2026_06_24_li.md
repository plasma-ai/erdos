**Claim.** Let $S(x)$ count the ordered pairs $(a,b)$ of positive integers
with $a+b\le x$ and $\sigma(a)+\sigma(b)=\sigma(a+b)$. For every fixed $R>0$,

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{S(x)}{x(\log x)^{R}}=\infty
$$

(Theorem 1.1 of the preprint, the first link, dated June 24, 2026). So $S(x)$
is not asymptotic to $cx$ for any constant $c$, which answers the yes-or-no
question of [[problems/arithmetic_functions/E1061/_index|Problem 1061]] no; in
Guy's formulation (B15), which asks whether the count is $cx+o(x)$ or of
higher order, the count is of higher order. The preprint's Remark 1.2 notes
that there are no solutions with $a=b$, since $\sigma(2m)>2\sigma(m)$, so the
unordered count is exactly $S(x)/2$ and the conclusion does not depend on
whether the problem counts ordered or unordered pairs. The result is a lower
bound: no asymptotic for $S(x)$ is claimed. The construction starts from three
integers of equal abundancy index $\sigma(n)/n$ and reduces the identity to
two equations in six primes, which a linear change of variables places on a
split quadric; a three-parameter rational ruling of the quadric gives many
affine systems of six linear forms, an exact lattice-index computation and an
elementary parameter sieve in codimension two prepare them, and Bienvenu's
higher-dimensional Siegel–Walfisz theorem, the only deep theorem the preprint
uses as a black box, supplies prime points on the planes uniformly. The cores
$(u,v)$ of Theorem 1.3, the two-height core estimate that drives the count,
are not solutions, but $(3600u,3600v)$ are: the fixed multiplier $3600$ is
deliberately not coprime to them. Multiplying such a solution by any $g$
coprime to $3600uv(u+v)$ gives another solution, since $\sigma(g)$ factors out
of all three divisor sums, and counting these multiples over about $\log x$
disjoint shells $c_0Y_j\le u+v\le Y_j$ at geometrically spaced scales
$Y_j=\chi^j$ ($\chi>1/c_0$) gives $S(x)\gg_\kappa x(\log x)^{3\kappa-5}$ for
every $\kappa>0$, hence the stated growth (Section 9). The library's
[[../library/arithmetic_functions/li_2026_resolution_erdos_problem_1061_sum_divisors/_index|source card]]
digests the preprint.

**Submission note.** Posted to erdosproblems.com as a proof claim by Eric Li
(account EricLi) on 26 July 2026, giving "GPT-5.5 Pro" as the AI used:

> We resolve Erdős Problem 1061, the question whether the number\[
> S(x)=\#\{(a,b)\in N^2:a+b\le x, \ \sigma(a)+\sigma(b)=\sigma(a+b)\} \]of
> ordered solutions has a linear asymptotic $S(x)\sim cx$. In fact the opposite
> extreme holds at every fixed logarithmic scale: for every $R>0$,\[
> \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{S(x)}{x(\log x)^R}=+\infty. \]The construction begins
> with three integers having the same abundancy index and reduces the
> divisor-sum identity to two equations in six primes. After a linear change of
> variables, these equations lie on a split quadric. A three-parameter rational
> ruling of the quadric supplies many affine systems of six linear forms. An
> exact lattice-index calculation, an elementary codimension-two parameter
> sieve, and Bienvenu's higher-dimensional Siegel--Walfisz theorem give prime
> points uniformly on these planes. Coprime multiplier amplification then yields
> the stated resolution. Notes: The true provenance, B15 in Guy’s Unsolved
> Problems in Number Theory, states "Erdős asks how many solutions (not
> necessarily primitive) are there with $ q+r<x $; is it $ cx+o(x) $ or is it of
> higher order?". It is therefore not necessary to determine a precise
> asymptotic formula in order to resolve the question as posed. Establishing
> that $S(x)$ exceeds $x$ by every fixed power of $\log x$, which this paper
> does, already settles the dichotomy in the “higher order” direction and
> answers the problem Erdős formulated.
