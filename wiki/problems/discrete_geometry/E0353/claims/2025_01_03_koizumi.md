***

**Claim.** Let $S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2$ be measurable. If $S$ is
unbounded and has positive Lebesgue measure, it contains the three vertices
of an isosceles triangle of area $1$ and the three vertices of a right
triangle of area $1$ (Theorem 1). If $S$ has infinite Lebesgue measure, it
contains the four vertices of an isosceles trapezoid of area $1$ (Theorem 2).
The trapezoid statement does not extend to unbounded sets of positive
measure: a small disk about the origin together with the points $(n,0)$ for
$n=1,2,3,\dots$ is a counterexample, as the paper remarks.

**Covers.** Three of the five questions in the problem's statement, all
answered yes: the isosceles trapezoid of area $1$, which Theorem 2 gives, and
the isosceles triangle and the right triangle, which Theorem 1 gives. The
cyclic quadrilateral (yes) and the convex polygon with congruent sides (no)
are Kovač and Predojević's, recorded on their
[[problems/discrete_geometry/E0353/claims/2024_12_16_kovac_predojevic|claim page]];
the two pages together settle every part of the statement, one of them
negatively.

**Method.** The proof builds an area-preserving map $f$ of the plane that
rotates each circle about the origin by an angle $\varphi(r)=\arcsin(2/r^2)$
depending on the radius, so that the origin, $p$ and $f(p)$ always span an
isosceles triangle of area $1$; Lebesgue's density theorem then forces both
$p$ and $f(p)$ into the set. Right triangles use the midpoint of $p$ and
$f(p)$ in place of $f(p)$, and trapezoids come from cutting the apex off the
isosceles triangle. The paper credits the approach of Kovač and Predojević
as its inspiration. The
[[../library/discrete_geometry/koizumi_2025_isosceles_trapezoids_unit_area_vertices_sets/_index|source card]]
lists the theorems and the sharpness remark.

**Formalization.** The site's label carries a Lean qualification. The
formal-conjectures statements of the problem and its variants are marked

**Acceptance.** The paper is refereed: J. Koizumi, Isosceles trapezoids of
unit area with vertices in sets of infinite planar measure, Proc. Amer. Math.
Soc. 153 (2025), no. 11, 4753–4758, published electronically 2025-08-29, DOI
10.1090/proc/17322. The curator of erdosproblems.com, Thomas Bloom, labels the
problem proved and credits this paper with resolving the question, naming the
trapezoid and the two triangles.
