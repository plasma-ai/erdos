***

**Claim.** P. D. T. A. Elliott, *On the number of circles determined by $n$
points*, Acta Math. Acad. Sci. Hungar. 18 (1967), no. 1-2, 181–188. The
statement here follows the report of Purdy and Smith [PuSm, Section 2.1]:
$n>393$ points of the plane, not all on one circle and not all on one line,
determine at least $\binom{n-1}{2}$ circles, a circle being determined when
it passes through at least three of the points. In the notation of
[[problems/discrete_geometry/E0506/_index|Problem 506]] this is the lower
bound $f(n)\ge\binom{n-1}{2}$ for every $n\ge394$. The paper has no library
card.

**Covers.** The claimed lower bound $f(n)\ge\binom{n-1}{2}$ for $n\ge394$,
which is false; the paper determines no value of $f(n)$.

**Rejection.** The bound is false for every $n\ge5$, following Section 2.1
of Purdy and Smith [PuSm]: $n-1$ points on a circle and one point $p$ off
it, placed so that $p$ lies on $\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor$ lines through pairs
of the circle points, determine the circle itself and one circle through $p$
and each pair of circle points not collinear with $p$, that is
$1+\binom{n-1}{2}-\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor$ circles, fewer than
$\binom{n-1}{2}$ as soon as $\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor\ge2$. Purdy and Smith add
that Elliott had cited Segre's eight-point counterexample to his result, the
projection of a cube, and that Elliott's proof can be modified to give the
corrected bound with the same threshold $394$; that corrected bound is the
accepted partial claim on
[[problems/discrete_geometry/E0506/claims/2009_07_03_purdy_smith|their claim page]],
and Bálintová and Bálint had printed it in 1994 without proof
([[problems/discrete_geometry/E0506/claims/1994_09_01_balintova_balint|their claim page]]).

**Depends on.** No page of this wiki.

**Acceptance.** None. The paper appeared in a refereed journal, and the
site's commentary says the problem was resolved by Elliott before recording
the error, but the theorem as printed is false, so no evidence is listed and
the claim is rejected. The site's curator labels the problem DECIDABLE on
the corrected bound and credits Purdy and Smith with it.
