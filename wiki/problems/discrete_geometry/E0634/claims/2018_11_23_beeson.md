***

**Claim.** Michael Beeson, No triangle can be cut into seven congruent
triangles, arXiv:1811.09723 (version 1 posted 23 November 2018, version 5 of
17 June 2019), proves the theorem of its title and the same for eleven: no
triangle can be cut into $7$ or into $11$ congruent triangles. By its
abstract, earlier work reduces the question to a number of cases, and the
paper settles the cases not already solved; its later revision replaced the
treatment of isosceles triangles by a citation to Beeson's paper on isosceles
tilings (arXiv:1206.1974). The site's remarks on
[[problems/discrete_geometry/E0634/_index|Problem 634]] credit Beeson with
the exclusion of $7$ and $11$ and link Beeson's talk slides; this paper is the
written source. Beeson's 2026 preprint on the prime counts
([[problems/discrete_geometry/E0634/claims/2026_07_26_beeson|claim page]])
describes this paper's method as direct and short but unable to reach $19$.

**Covers.** The values $7$ and $11$ do not occur as the number of congruent
triangles into which some triangle can be cut. Nothing is claimed about
other values of $n$.

**Depends on.** No page of this wiki.

**Standing.** Claimed. The paper has no journal record; the site labels the
problem OPEN, so the curator's credit is commentary, not acceptance. The
primes $7$ and $11$ are also excluded by the three 2026 prime manuscripts
and, $7$ unconditionally and $11$ by certified exhaustive search, by
Bonfioli's manuscript
([[problems/discrete_geometry/E0634/claims/2026_06_27_bonfioli|claim page]]),
which lists $11$ among the values it says the published theorems do not
settle, because the exclusions in Beeson's paper on the case
$3\alpha+2\beta=\pi$ (arXiv:1206.2229) rest on a divisibility it refutes;
this page records that dispute without resolving whether it reaches the
argument of this paper.
