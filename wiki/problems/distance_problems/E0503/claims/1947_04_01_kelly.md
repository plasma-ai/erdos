***

**Claim.** Call a finite set isosceles when every three of its points form an
isosceles triangle. Kelly's solution of Monthly problem E735, which Erdős
had posed in 1946, proves that no isosceles set in the plane has seven
points and that, up to similarity, the only isosceles set of six points in
the plane is the set of vertices of a regular pentagon together with its
center. The largest isosceles subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$ therefore has $6$
points, which answers the instance $d=2$ of
[[problems/distance_problems/E0503/_index|Problem 503]]. The solution also
exhibits an isosceles set of eight points in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the lower bound
that Croft's theorem later matched. Ionin's paper of 2009, Section 1,
describes the solution in these terms, and Kovács's note of 2024 re-proves
the planar classification by computer algebra
([[../library/distance_problems/kovacs_2024_note_erdos_s_mysterious_remark/_index|card]]).

**Covers.** The instance $d=2$ of the problem, answered $6$, with the
six-point set unique up to similarity; and the lower bound $8$ for $d=3$,
whose matching upper bound is on
[[problems/distance_problems/E0503/claims/1962_01_01_croft|Croft's claim page]].
Nothing is claimed about $d\ge3$ beyond that example.

**Acceptance.** The solution is refereed: P. Erdős and L. M. Kelly, E735,
Amer. Math. Monthly 54 (1947), no. 4, 227–229, in the journal's problems
section, Erdős as proposer and Kelly as solver. The site records the value
$6$ for $d=2$ with this solution as its source while labeling the problem
OPEN, so the curator's remark is context for this partial claim and not
acceptance evidence. The page is dated to the issue, April 1947.
