***

**Claim.** Let $Q(X)$ count the highly composite numbers in $[1,X]$. Nicolas,
Théorème 4, proves

$$
Q(X)=O\bigl((\log X)^{1+c}\bigr)
$$

for an absolute constant $c>0$, the constant of his Théorème 3. So
$Q(x)\gg_k(\log x)^k$ fails for every $k>1+c$, and the answer to
[[problems/divisors/E0381/_index|Problem 381]] is no. Théorème 3 counts the
highly composite numbers lying between two consecutive superior highly
composite numbers $N<N'$ and finds $O((\log N)^c)$ of them; the count up to $X$
follows by summing over the superior highly composite numbers below $X$. The
local count rests on Théorème 1, a bound on how far $d(A)/A^{\varepsilon}$
can fall below $d(N)/N^{\varepsilon}$ for a highly composite number $A$, where
$N$ is the superior highly composite number below $A$ at which that ratio is
largest (Nicolas's bénéfice of $A$, at most $Cx^{-\gamma}$ with
$x=2^{1/\varepsilon}$), proved through Diophantine approximation of the numbers
$\log(1+1/k)/\log 2$ with Feldman's refinement of Baker's theorem on linear
forms in logarithms. Théorème 5 of the same paper reproves Erdős's 1944 lower
bound $Q(X)\ge(\log X)^{1+c'}$ with a larger constant, so the count is pinned
between two fixed powers of $\log X$; Nicolas conjectures the exact exponent,
$\log Q(X)/\log\log X\to1+(\log(3/2)+\log(5/4))/(4\log 2)=\log30/\log16
=1.2267\ldots$ (the print gives the value as 1.277 on p. 117, a misprint of
the exact expression), which no result recorded here settles. The source
card is
[[../library/divisors/nicolas_1971_repartition_des_nombres_hautement_composes/_index|Nicolas 1971]];
the lower bound it reproves is the accepted partial claim
[[problems/divisors/E0381/claims/1944_07_01_erdos|Erdős 1944]]
([[../library/divisors/erdos_1944_highly_composite_numbers/_index|card]]),
where Erdős records that he could not decide the question this page answers.
