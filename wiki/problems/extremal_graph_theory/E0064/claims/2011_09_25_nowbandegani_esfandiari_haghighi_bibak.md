***

**Claim.** Every cubic claw-free graph on fewer than $114$ vertices contains a
cycle whose length is a power of $2$ (Theorem 9 of the published paper and of
arXiv v3, numbered Theorem 8 in arXiv v1 and v2, before Conjecture 8 was
inserted); every claw-free graph with
minimum degree at least $3$ contains a cycle of length $2^k$ or $3\cdot2^k$
for some $k\ge1$ (Theorem 1); and in a claw-free graph of minimum degree at
least $4$ without a $4$-cycle every non-cut vertex lies on a cycle whose
length is a power of $2$ (Theorem 5). The results are those of Pouria Salehi
Nowbandegani, Hossein Esfandiari, Mohammad Hassan Shirdareh Haghighi and
Khodakhast Bibak, *On the Erdős--Gyárfás conjecture in claw-free graphs*,
Discuss. Math. Graph Theory **34** (2014), no. 3, 635--640, first posted as
arXiv:1109.5398 on 2011-09-25 (the claim's date; v3 of 2013-02-07 is the
final version). The $114$-vertex theorem is proved by
contracting the triangles of a $C_4$-free cubic claw-free graph of order $3n$
to a cubic graph of order $n$ and counting the vertices a cycle-free
neighborhood expansion forces, giving $n\ge38$. Read depth: the
statements and the proof of the $114$-vertex theorem in arXiv v1, and the
introduction and references of arXiv v3; the journal text was not compared.
The thread comment of 6 December 2025 that the site's remark on
[[problems/extremal_graph_theory/E0064/_index|Problem 64]] points to cites
the paper for the $114$-vertex bound. The introduction of v3 also states the
bipartite bound of two of the authors, recorded on
[[problems/extremal_graph_theory/E0064/claims/2011_09_18_nowbandegani_esfandiari|its own claim page]].

**Covers.** The statement of Problem 64 for cubic claw-free graphs on at most
$113$ vertices, and for claw-free graphs of minimum degree at least $4$
(either a $4$-cycle exists or Theorem 5 applies to a non-cut vertex, which
every finite graph has); the latter class is also covered by Shauger's
theorem at $m=3$
([[problems/extremal_graph_theory/E0064/claims/1998_01_01_shauger|claim page]]).
Theorem 1 settles no instance by itself, since $3\cdot2^k$ is not a power of
two.

**Depends on.** No page of this wiki.

**Acceptance.** Refereed: the paper is a publication in Discussiones
Mathematicae Graph Theory. The site's curator cites the family list that
names the paper while labeling the problem FALSIFIABLE, which is commentary
on an open problem and not reviewed evidence.
