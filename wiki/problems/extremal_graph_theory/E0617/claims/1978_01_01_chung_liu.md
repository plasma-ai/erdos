**Claim.** K. M. Chung and C. L. Liu, *A generalization of Ramsey theory for
graphs*, Discrete Math. **21** (1978), no. 2, 117--127, define the
$2$-chromatic Ramsey number $R^3_2(K_i,K_j,K_l)$ as the least $p$ such that
every coloring of the edges of $K_p$ with colors $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ has an
$\alpha$-$\beta$ $K_i$, an $\alpha$-$\gamma$ $K_j$ or a $\beta$-$\gamma$
$K_l$, a complete subgraph whose edges use only the two named colors. Their
Theorem 3.6 (p. 125, proof p. 126) proves $R^3_2(K_4,K_4,K_4)=10$. A $K_4$
whose edges use at most two of the three colors is exactly a $K_4$ on which a
color is missing, so the upper bound says that every three-coloring of the
edges of $K_{10}$ has four vertices on which some color does not appear: the
case $r=3$ of [[problems/extremal_graph_theory/E0617/_index|Problem 617]]. The
lower bound is their Theorem 2.6 (p. 120), $R^3_2(K_i,K_j,K_l)>(i-1)(j-1)$, by
a product coloring: on the pairs $(a,b)$ with $1\le a\le i-1$ and $1\le b\le
j-1$, an edge is colored $\alpha$ when the first coordinates agree, $\beta$
when the second coordinates agree, and $\gamma$ otherwise. For $i=j=4$ this
colors $K_9$ so that every four vertices see all three colors. The proof of
Theorem 3.6 shows that in a counterexample on $10$ vertices every color has
degree three at every vertex, finds an $\alpha$ triangle from $R(3,4)=9$, and
excludes each possible configuration of the remaining $\alpha$ edges by hand.

**Covers.** The fixed case $r=3$ only, twenty-one years before the proof of
the same case in
[[problems/extremal_graph_theory/E0617/claims/1999_04_01_erdos_gyarfas|Erdős and Gyárfás 1999]].
Munemasa and Shinohara (arXiv:1406.2050, p. 2) cite this theorem as the value
$10$ of the diagonal complementary Ramsey number for three colors and $K_4$.

**Depends on.** Nothing in this wiki; the proof is the paper's own, with
$R(3,4)=9$ as its external input.
