**Claim.** For $n\ge600$, among the graphs with $2n+1$ vertices and at least
$n^2+n$ edges, the complete bipartite graph $K_{n,n+1}$ is the only one in which
no two vertices of equal degree are the ends of a path with three edges. Hence
for $n\ge600$ every graph with $2n+1$ vertices and at least $n^2+n+1$ edges
contains two vertices of the same degree joined by a path of length $3$, which
is the corrected Statement of
[[problems/extremal_graph_theory/E0816/_index|Problem 816]] for those $n$, in
the stronger "at least" form: the property is not monotone in the edge set,
since adding edges changes degrees, so the statement for at least $n^2+n+1$
edges is genuinely stronger than the statement for exactly $n^2+n+1$ edges. The
result is Theorem 2 of K. Chen and J. Ma, *A problem of Erdős and Hajnal on
paths with equal-degree endpoints*, J. Combin. Theory Ser. B 179 (2026), 1--18,
first posted as arXiv:2503.19569 on 2025-03-25; the corpus states it on its
[[../library/extremal_graph_theory/chen_2025_problem_erdos_hajnal_paths_equal_degree/theorem_2|result page]].
In the paper's notation, $p_3(2n+1)=n^2+n$ for $n\ge600$, where $p_3(m)$ is the
largest number of edges of an $m$-vertex graph without such a pair. The proof
bounds the maximum degree $\Delta$ from above by $n+\sqrt{2n}+\frac32$ and from
below by $\frac{17}{16}n$, which is impossible for $n\ge600$; the authors say a
sharper estimate would lower the constant below $150$. Theorem 3 of the paper
gives the even-order analog, $K_{n-1,n+1}$ unique with at least $n^2-1$ edges
for large $n$, which the problem does not ask.

**Covers.** The corrected Statement for every $n\ge600$. For $2\le n\le599$ this
theorem says nothing; the case $n=2$ is checked on the problem page, and the
whole range $n\ge2$ is the subject of the pending full claim of
[[problems/extremal_graph_theory/E0816/claims/2025_05_01_liu_zeng|Liu and Zeng]].
