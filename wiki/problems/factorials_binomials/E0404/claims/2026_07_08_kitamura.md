**Claim.** For the pairs $(a,p)$ in the repository's exact rows the
exponents $k$ with $p^k\mid a_1!+\cdots+a_n!$ for some $a=a_1<\cdots<a_n$ are
bounded, the first question of
[[problems/factorials_binomials/E0404/_index|Problem 404]] at those pairs,
and the largest such exponent is computed: the rows displayed in the thread
post are

$$
f(3,3)=3,\ f(39,3)=25,\ f(700,3)=363,\ f(110,5)=30,\ f(236,5)=61,
$$

$$
f(42,7)=7,\ f(113,11)=11,\ f(163,13)=13,
$$

and the Lean examples add $f(4,7)=0$ and $f(6,7)=0$. Kenta Kitamura, under
the forum name KentaKitamura, announced the repository
`KitaKen1/erdos-404-odd-prime-landscape` in the site's discussion thread on
8 July 2026, the day after the $p=2$ repository recorded on
[[problems/factorials_binomials/E0404/claims/2026_07_07_kitamura|its page]];
its README (linked at the commit of that day) is the write-up. An exact row
means that the search finds a witness at exponent $K$ and rules out exponent
$K+1$ by the same finite argument as at $p=2$, with Legendre's formula
bounding the candidate terms and a windowed dynamic program over residues
modulo $p^{K+1}$. The repository also records witnesses giving
$f(1,3)\ge20000$, $f(1,5)\ge20000$, $f(1,7)\ge20000$, $f(1,11)\ge5000$ and
$f(1,13)\ge5000$, explicit lists checked modulo $p^K$, in the direction of
Tao's thread remark that $f(1,5)$ appears very large and possibly infinite,
and baseline lower bounds $f(a,p)\ge v_p(a!)$ for rows the scan left open;
the post says these are finite witnesses, not claims of infinitude, and they
settle no instance. The three Lean files (the `formalization` links) prove
$f(3,3)=3$, $f(4,7)=0$ and $f(6,7)=0$ from both sides; this corpus has not
built them. The post says the search programs, organization, Lean files and
the post were prepared with assistance from Codex, ChatGPT and Claude Code
(Fable 5); the human submitter is the claimant, with the systems named as the
submitter names them.

**Covers.** The first question at the pairs $(3,3)$, $(39,3)$, $(700,3)$,
$(110,5)$, $(236,5)$, $(42,7)$, $(113,11)$, $(163,13)$, $(4,7)$ and $(6,7)$:
a finite bound exists, with the value of $f$ computed exactly. Not covered:
the lower-bound rows, which decide nothing; $a=1$ at any odd prime; the
behavior of $f$ in general; and the third question.
