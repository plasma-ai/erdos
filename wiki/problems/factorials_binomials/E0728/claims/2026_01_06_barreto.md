***

**Claim.** The answer to
[[problems/factorials_binomials/E0728/_index|Problem 728]] is yes, in the
reading the site's commentary identifies as the intended one. For every
$0<\varepsilon<1/2$ and every $0<C_1<C_2$ there are infinitely many triples
$(a,b,n)$ with $\varepsilon n\le a,b\le(1-\varepsilon)n$,

$$
a!\,b!\mid n!\,(a+b-n)!
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
C_1\log n<a+b-n<C_2\log n.
$$

The examples have $n=2m$, $b=m$ and $a=m+k$ with $k\asymp\log n$, so the
divisibility is $\binom{m+k}{k}\mid\binom{2m}{m}$, and the triples satisfy
the statement's $a,b\ge\varepsilon n$ and $a+b>n+C\log n$ for every $C$. The
statement as the site prints it also has trivial solutions, for instance
$a=b=n$, which is why the commentary reads the question with an upper bound
on $a$ and $b$; the theorem answers that reading, and the literal one with
it.

**The argument.** For a prime $p$, Kummer's theorem makes
$\nu_p\binom{2m}{m}$ the number of carries when $m$ is added to itself in
base $p$, and $\nu_p\binom{m+k}{k}$ is at most the largest $\nu_p(m+i)$ with
$1\le i\le k$ (for $p>2k$ exactly the valuation of $(m+1)\cdots(m+k)$).
Primes $p>2k$ are handled directly: a high power of $p$ dividing some $m+i$
forces as many carries. For $p\le2k$ the proof counts, on each dyadic
interval, the $m$ with too few carries (a Chernoff bound on the base-$p$
digits) and the $m$ with an unusually high prime power among
$m+1,\ldots,m+k$, and a union bound over these primes leaves an $m$ that is
good for every $p$ at once; no sieve input and no Chinese remainder step is
needed. The informal argument was produced by the AI system ChatGPT-5.2
(GPT-5.2 Pro), the formal proof by Harmonic's Aristotle. Kevin Barreto
submitted both to the site's discussion thread, is the claimant here, and
names the two systems; the thread records that a first attempt of 2026-01-04
only reached $a+b-n=c\log n$ for a small constant $c$. Barreto posted the
informal proof for every $C$ on 2026-01-05, saying they did not claim its
accuracy, and the Lean proof on 2026-01-06; both postings are linked above,
and the page is dated by the posting of the formally verified proof because
the earlier post disclaimed accuracy.

**Formalization.** The Lean development linked above at its pinned commit,
in Boris Alexeev's repository of formalized Erdős problems, is Aristotle's
proof rerun by Alexeev to shorten it, and it includes a proof of the
statement in the formal-conjectures project, which adds the upper bound
$a+b<n+C'\log n$ on the gap. The same file, at the repository's current Lean
version, is the second formalization link; that version carries the header
naming ChatGPT-5.2 and Barreto as the informal authors and Aristotle, Barreto
and Alexeev as the formal authors. The informal proof was written up as a
paper by Nat Sothanaphan, *Resolution of Erdős Problem #728: a writeup of
Aristotle's Lean proof*, arXiv:2601.07421 (first version 2026-01-12), whose
Theorem 1 is the statement above with the $(1-\varepsilon)n$ bound and whose
appendices derive the same method's consequences for Problems 729 and 401;
the writeup is carded at
[[../library/factorials_binomials/sothanaphan_2026_resolution_erdos_problem_728_writeup_aristotle/_index|Sothanaphan 2026]].
This corpus has not built or audited the Lean development, so the page lists
no `formalized` evidence.
