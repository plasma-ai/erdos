***

Hančl, Jaroslav, *Expression of real numbers with the help of infinite
series*, Acta Arith. 59 (1991), no. 2, 97–104. Call an increasing sequence
of positive integers $a_n$ an irrationality sequence when $\sum 1/(t_na_n)$ is
irrational for every choice of integers $t_n\ge1$. Hančl's Corollary 2 states
that every sequence of positive reals $c_n$ with

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log_2\log_2 c_n}{n}<1
$$

is a rational sequence: some choice of positive integers $b_n$ makes
$\sum 1/(c_nb_n)$ rational. An irrationality sequence therefore satisfies
$\limsup(\log_2\log_2 a_n)/n\ge1$. His Theorem 2 and Corollary 1 give the
finer exclusion the site records: if $a_n\ll 2^{2^{n-F(n)}}$ with $F(n)<n$
and $\sum 2^{-F(n)}<\infty$, then $a_n$ is not an irrationality sequence. The
proofs run a greedy induction choosing the factors $b_n$ one at a time so that
the remainder of a target rational is driven to zero. Erdős had shown in the
1975 paper the site cites that $a_n=2^{2^n}$ is an irrationality sequence
([[../library/irrationality/erdos_1976_problems_results_irrationality_sum_infinite_series/_index|source card]]),
so the answer to how slowly $a_n$ can grow is: doubly exponentially, with
$\limsup(\log_2\log_2 a_n)/n=1$ attained and nothing below it possible. The
[[../library/irrationality/hancl_1991_expression_real_numbers_help_infinite_series/_index|source card]]
digests the paper. Hančl remarks that he does not know whether $2^{2^n/n}$ is
a rational sequence, so the exact boundary at the threshold is not described.
