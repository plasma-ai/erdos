***

**Claim.** Theorem 2 (p. 2) is printed as "For every $0<\epsilon\le1$, and every
integer $k\ge1$, there exists an integer $N=N(\epsilon,k)$ such that for every
prime $p\ge2$, and every integer $0\le a\le p-1$, there exist integers
$x_1,\ldots,x_N$ such that $1\le x_i\le p^\epsilon$, and
$a\equiv\frac1{x_1^k}+\cdots+\frac1{x_N^k}\pmod p$" (Integers 4 (2004), #A20, p.
2, as in arXiv v2; arXiv v1 and the copy on the author's papers page print
$x_1,\ldots,x_n$). With $k=1$ this is the question of
[[problems/number_theory/E1180/_index|Problem 1180]] for $0<\epsilon\le1$, with
$C_\epsilon=N(\epsilon,1)$ and repetition allowed, read with at most $N$
summands. As printed, with exactly $N$ summands, the statement holds only for
large $p$: for a prime with $p^\epsilon<2$, and for $p=2$ at every
$\epsilon\le1$, the only admissible $x_i$ is $1$, and a sum of exactly $N$ of
them is the single residue $N\bmod p$. The proof covers the finitely many small
primes by enlarging $N$, which works when $N$ bounds the number of summands, and
the one-line completion of Shparlinski's and Glibichuk's pages (a residue $a$ is
the sum of $a$ copies of $1=1^{-1}$) gives them; Glibichuk's introduction
restates the theorem for sufficiently large $p$. For $\epsilon>1$ the set
$\{n^{-1}:n\le p^\epsilon\}$ contains the set for $\epsilon=1$, so
$C_\epsilon=C_1$ serves (the monotonicity remark of the problem page). The
resulting $N$ is not explicit. The theorem is compiled on the result page
[[../library/number_theory/croot_2004_sums_reciprocal_powers_modulo_prime/theorem_2|theorem_2]];
the digest is on the card
[[../library/number_theory/croot_2004_sums_reciprocal_powers_modulo_prime/_index|croot_2004_sums_reciprocal_powers_modulo_prime]].

**Argument, in outline.** The Bourgain--Katz--Tao sum-product estimate is
applied to the sums of inverse $k$th powers of small primes, and an
exponential-sum lemma then writes every residue as
$t_1t_2+t_3t_4+\cdots+t_{15}t_{16}$ with the $t_i$ in a set $T$ of sums of
inverse $k$th powers, which the paper counts as at most $16h^2$ terms
$1/(qq')^k$ (pp. 2--5). The outline records the structure only; no step is
checked. The paper's introduction states the Erdős--Graham question in the
monograph's form and credits its first affirmative answer to Shparlinski,
through Karatsuba's result in the simplified form of Friedlander and Iwaniec;
that answer is on
[[problems/number_theory/E1180/claims/2002_06_01_shparlinski|Shparlinski's page]].
