***

**Claim.** N. Hindman, *Partitions and sums and products of integers*,
Trans. Amer. Math. Soc. 247 (1979), 227--245. Theorem 4.4 (p. 244): every
partition of $\{2,\ldots,990\}$ into two cells has distinct $x,y$ with
$\{x,y,x+y,xy\}$ in one cell, and $990$ cannot be lowered. Theorem 4.3
(p. 243) is credited to Graham's unpublished computation, which Hindman
verified independently; it gives the same for $\{1,\ldots,252\}$, with
$252$ sharp. Either theorem gives every $2$-coloring of $\mathbb N$ a set
$A=\{x,y\}$ whose sums and products of distinct elements share one color:
$x$, $y$, $x+y$ and $xy$, with the singletons included as the problem page
reads them. Both theorems rest on computer searches: Theorem 4.3 lists its
27 cases and checks one in print, and Theorem 4.4 prints only the extremal
partition of $\{2,\ldots,989\}$. Erdős reported both computations in 1977
(p. 58). Bowen's Theorem 1.1 at $n=2$ (Adv. Math. 462 (2025), 110095;
arXiv:2205.12921, 25 May 2022) reproves the case without a computer and
gives arbitrarily large such $x,y$. It settles the same instance and has no
page of its own.

**Covers.** The case of two colors and $|A|=2$. Not covered: three or more
colors, and $|A|\ge3$.

**Depends on.** Nothing in this wiki; the result rests on the cited paper.

**Acceptance.** Refereed: Trans. Amer. Math. Soc. 247 (1979), 227--245.
The computer searches were not rerun here. The site labels the problem
OPEN and cites neither this paper nor Bowen's, so no curator credit is
listed.
