# Problem 966

[[problems/ramsey_theory/_index|..]]

[[problems/ramsey_theory/E0966/claims/_index|claims/]]: The 1 claim page of Problem 966, one per claimant's result; the problem's standing derives from them.

***

**Statement.** Let $k,r\geq 2$. Does there exist a set $A\subseteq \mathbb{N}$
that contains no non-trivial arithmetic progression of length $k+1$, yet in any
$r$-colouring of $A$ there must exist a monochromatic non-trivial arithmetic
progression of length $k$?

**Formulation.** The site's wording (the page shows no last-edited date). A
non-trivial arithmetic progression has nonzero common difference. Erdős's 1975
wording (printed p. 306, quoted below) asks for "a sequence without the
property $A(k+1)$, but is such that if we split it into $r$ subsequences at
least one of them has the property $A(k)$", where "A sequence of integers is
said to have the property $A(k)$ if it contains an arithmetic progression of
$k$ terms" (p. 295); a sequence and its $r$ subsequences are the site's set
and $r$-coloring. The site adds $k,r\ge2$; for $k=1$ or $r=1$ the question is
trivial. Spencer's paper, the status-defining source, calls a set of integers
a $V$-set (for $k$, $c$) if every $c$-coloring of it yields a monochromatic
arithmetic progression of $k$ elements, and constructs such a set with no
progression of length $k+1$; his $c$ is the site's $r$, his progressions have
nonzero difference in both clauses, and his set is a finite subset of
$\{0,\dots,p^n-1\}$ that a translation by $1$ moves into the positive
integers, so the reading of $\mathbb N$ is immaterial.

**Status.** Proved. Spencer's Theorem 1 [Sp75] (J. Combin. Theory Ser. A 19
(1975), no. 3, 278--286; refereed) gives, for all $k$ and $c$, a $V$-set
with no arithmetic progression of length $k+1$, which is the statement with
$c=r$. Erdős announced the result in 1975 as "added in proof: Spencer has
recently shown that such a sequence exists", without a reference; Spencer's
paper is the published proof, from the Hales--Jewett theorem. The site's
Lean suffix is a catalog label explained under Formalization: an external
Lean proof, generated by Aristotle from the statement and posted to the
site's thread by JoshuaB on 25 February 2026, exists in a later repository
copy and was not built here. The claim page
[[problems/ramsey_theory/E0966/claims/1975_11_01_spencer|Spencer 1975]]
(accepted on the refereed publication and the curator's credit) records the
result, its postings, the Aristotle-generated Lean proof behind the site's
suffix and the acceptance evidence, and the frontmatter standing derives
from it.

## Current assessment

**The question (site formulation of 2026-09-18).** The statement
above; PROVED (LEAN), the site's label for an affirmative answer whose proof
is verified in Lean; no last-edited date. The commentary says that Erdős
[Er75b] reported Spencer's result, in the words "Spencer has recently shown
that such a sequence exists" (Erdős's, p. 306, quoted again below), without
giving a reference, and calls the problem the arithmetic analog of the graph
question, Problem 924. The thread, oldest first: a comment of 25 February 2026
by JoshuaB reporting that Aristotle produced a Lean proof of the statement in
one attempt, from the statement alone and an instruction to prove it in the
positive, that the commenter removed unused lemmas and fixed warnings, and that
the proof builds a hypercube with the needed properties by Hales--Jewett and
projects it to a lower dimension, with a link to the proof in a Lean web
playground; a one-word comment of congratulation the same day; and a comment of
26 February 2026 (Terence Tao) observing that this is plausibly Spencer's own
solution: the infinite cube $[k]^\omega$ has no arithmetic progression of length
$k+1$, the Hales--Jewett theorem gives every finite coloring of the cube a
monochromatic line, which is a $k$-term progression, and an embedding in base
$q$ for any $q>2k$ (a standard Freiman-isomorphism device) projects the example
onto the integers; since Mathlib has the Hales--Jewett theorem, the comment
adds, the formal proof is comparatively straightforward. The proof-claim tab is
empty. The community database records "proved (Lean)".

**The origin (Er75b, printed p. 306).**
Chapter IV, item (i): "Is it true that for every $k$ and $r$ there is a
sequence without the property $A(k+1)$, but is such that if we split it into
$r$ subsequences at least one of them has the property $A(k)$? (added in
proof: Spencer has recently shown that such a sequence exists)." The next
paragraph: "The conjecture was motivated by the following older conjecture of
Hajnal and myself", the graph question that is
[[problems/ramsey_theory/E0924/_index|Problem 924]], with Folkman's two-color theorem
and the Nešetřil--Rödl theorem reported there. Property $A(k)$ is defined on
p. 295. The announcement names no paper; the page below identifies it.

**Status-defining source (refereed).** Spencer's
[[../library/ramsey_theory/spencer_1975_restricted_ramsey_configurations/theorem_1|Theorem 1]]
(restricted Van der Waerden configuration; printed p. 279, checked clause
by clause): "For all $k$, $c$ there exists a $V$-set $A$
such that $A$ contains no arithmetic progression of length $k+1$", where a
$V$-set is a set of integers any $c$-coloring of which "yields a m.a.p.
[monochromatic arithmetic progression] of size $k$". Section 2 introduces it
as "a result on Van der Waerden's theorem analogous to the result of Nešetřil
and Rödl", the graph theorem of Problem 924. The proof (p. 279, half a page,
read for its structure): by the Hales--Jewett theorem there is $n$ such that
every $c$-coloring of the cube $k^n$ has a monochromatic line; with a prime
$p>k$ let $A=\{a_0+a_1p+\dots+a_{n-1}p^{n-1}:0\le a_i<k\}$; a monochromatic
line of the cube is, in $A$, a monochromatic arithmetic progression of length
$k$; and a progression $x,x+d,\dots,x+kd$ in $A$ is impossible because the
$p^i$ digit of $x+sd$, for $d_i$ the lowest nonzero digit of $d$, runs through
$k+1$ distinct residues modulo $p$ while $A$'s digits take only $k$ values.
Theorem 6 (p. 285) refines the construction with $p>2k$ so that any two
$k$-term progressions in the set meet in at most one point, which is the
base-$q$ embedding with $q>2k$ of the thread's sketch. Acceptance: the
paper appeared in J. Combinatorial Theory Ser. A 19 (1975), no. 3, 278--286
(the publisher's record), a refereed journal; Erdős's
added-in-proof note attests the result, and Spencer's acknowledgment thanks
Erdős "for his conjectures, theorems, and encouragement". The Semantic Scholar
list of works citing the paper (28 records, 1979 to 2026, among them the
restricted and canonical versions of van der Waerden's and Hales--Jewett's
theorems of the 1980s and Ramsey Theory on the Integers) records no dispute.
Read depth: claims checked for the definitions and Theorem 1; the proof was
read for structure and not checked step by step; nothing is independently
reviewed.
