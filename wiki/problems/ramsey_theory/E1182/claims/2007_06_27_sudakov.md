***

**Claim.** Sudakov, *Ramsey numbers and the size of graphs* (SIAM J.
Discrete Math. 21 (2007), no. 4, 980--986), proves, as its abstract states
the theorem, that for every $s\ge3$ there is a constant $c=c(s)>0$ such
that every graph $G$ with $m$ edges satisfies

$$
R(K_s,G)\ge c\left(\frac{m}{\log m}\right)^{\frac{s+1}{s+3}};
$$

the abstract adds that the bound improves an earlier one of Erdős, Faudree,
Rousseau and Schelp and is tight up to a polylogarithmic factor when $s=3$.
In the letters of [[problems/ramsey_theory/E1182/_index|Problem 1182]], take
$s=3$, so the exponent is $2/3$: a connected graph $G$ on $n$ vertices with
$f(n)$ edges and $R(K_3,G)=2n-1$ satisfies $c(f(n)/\log f(n))^{2/3}\le2n-1$,
so $f(n)\le Cn^{3/2}\log f(n)\le2Cn^{3/2}\log n$, since $f(n)\le n^2$;
that is,

$$
f(n)=O\bigl(n^{3/2}\log n\bigr).
$$

This one-line deduction from the stated theorem is made here; the paper does not
mention the problem. With the 1980 lower bound $f(n)>An^{3/2}(\log n)^{1/2}$ of
Burr, Erdős, Faudree, Rousseau and Schelp (Theorem 2, on
[[problems/ramsey_theory/E1182/claims/1980_01_01_burr_erdos_faudree_rousseau_schelp|their claim page]]),
the exponent of $f(n)$ is $3/2$ and the two bounds differ by a factor $(\log
n)^{1/2}$, replacing the 1980 upper bound $Bn^{5/3}(\log n)^{2/3}$. The same
theorem with $s=m$ gives $f_m(n)=O(n^{1+2/(m+1)}\log n)$ for the $K_m$ version
recorded on the problem page.

**Covers.** The upper bound on $f(n)$: the exponent $3/2$ is determined and the
order of $f(n)$ is known to within a factor $(\log n)^{1/2}$. The exact order of
$f(n)$, the function $F(n)$ and the closing question about $F(n)/n$ are not
addressed; the closing question is answered no by the pending claim
[[problems/ramsey_theory/E1182/claims/1996_12_01_brandt|Brandt 1996]], and the
pending full claim [[problems/ramsey_theory/E1182/claims/2026_09_10_gu|Gu 2026]]
asserts that $f(n)$ has order $n^{3/2}(\log n)^{1/2}$, closing that gap.

**Depends on.** Nothing in this wiki; the deduction uses the paper's
theorem and the definition of $f(n)$.
