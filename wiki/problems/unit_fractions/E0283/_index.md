# Problem 283

[[problems/unit_fractions/_index|..]]

[[problems/unit_fractions/E0283/claims/_index|claims/]]: The 4 claim pages of Problem 283, one per claimant's result; the problem's standing derives from them.

***

**Statement.** Let $p:\mathbb{Z}\to \mathbb{Z}$ be a polynomial whose leading
coefficient is positive and such that there exists no $d\geq 2$ with $d\mid
p(n)$ for all $n\geq 1$. Is it true that, for all sufficiently large $m$, there
exist integers $1\leq n_1<\cdots <n_k$ such that

$$
1=\frac{1}{n_1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n_k}
$$

and

$$
m=p(n_1)+\cdots+p(n_k)?
$$

**Formulation.** The site's wording as of 2026-09-18 (page last edited
10 May 2026). The polynomial takes integer values at the
integers and its leading coefficient is positive; "no $d\ge2$ with
$d\mid p(n)$ for all $n\ge1$" says that the values at positive integers
have no common divisor other than $1$, the condition the 1980 monograph
writes as $\gcd(p(1),p(2),\ldots)=1$ and Graham's 1963 conjecture $2'$
writes prime by prime. The denominators are distinct positive integers whose
reciprocals sum to exactly $1$, and the number $k$ of them may depend on
$m$. The question is whether every sufficiently large integer $m$ is the
sum of $p$ over the denominators of such a representation. The cases
$p(x)=x$ (Graham 1963, refereed) and $p(x)=x^2$ (Alekseyev 2019, a
published book chapter) are recorded below (Graham's accepted, Alekseyev's
pending); the general case is the question.

**Status.** The site's label is PROVED (LEAN). Graham's Theorem 1 of 1963
(J. Austral. Math. Soc., refereed) settles $p(x)=x$ for every $m>77$, with
$77$ excluded; Alekseyev's Theorem 1 of 2019 (a chapter of an edited
Princeton University Press volume) states $p(x)=x^2$ for every $m>8542$ (a
pending claim: a book chapter with no refereeing recorded); van Doorn's

## Current assessment

**The question (site formulation of 2026-09-18).** The statement above;
PROVED (LEAN); last edited 10 May 2026; source key [ErGr80, p. 32]. The
commentary, in summary: Graham [Gr63] settled the case $p(x)=x$ and asked
whether the same holds when the reciprocal sum $1$ is any positive rational
$\alpha$ (the threshold for $m$ then depending on $\alpha$); Cassels [Ca60]
showed that the two hypotheses on $p$ already make every large integer a sum
of values $p(n_i)$ at distinct $n_i$, the reciprocal condition dropped; Burr
handled the powers $p(x)=x^k$ when repeated denominators are permitted;
Alekseyev [Al19] settled $p(x)=x^2$ from $m=8543$ on, illustrated by
$1=\frac12+\frac14+\frac16+\frac1{12}$ with $200=2^2+4^2+6^2+12^2$; van Doorn
[vD25] studied the size of the threshold for $p(x)=x$ and, per the thread,
settled a large number of linear and quadratic polynomials, $p(x)=x+5$ and
$p(x)=x^2+100$ among them; and the general case, strengthened to every
positive rational $\alpha$, is credited to a proof given by GPT 5.5 Pro at
Price's prompting, with the thread holding a summary. The thread (ten
comments): van Doorn's summary of Graham's paper and of his own
bounds (11 August 2025), his reduction of the binomial case $p(x)=ax^d+b$ to a
finite search with the linear and quadratic families above (31 August 2025, a
manuscript in progress on GitHub), his asymptotic for $n_{\alpha,m}$ with a
conditional formalization (28 March 2026), Price's announcement (3 May 2026),
Barreto's note on his edits (3 May), Nat Sothanaphan's summary of the argument
(3 May) and his report of 6 May that he had confirmed it, the formalizer's
comments of 6 May with the Lean file, the later formalization of its one axiom
and an updated PDF, and Thomas Bloom's proof summary (10 May). The thread also
links chat-transcript pages, which are not sources. The community database
lists the problem as proved (Lean), as of its last update of 10 May 2026.

**Origin.** Printed p. 32 of the 1980 monograph, with $\mathscr X$ the set
of finite sets $\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$, $0<x_1<\cdots<x_n$, with
$\sum1/x_k=1$. The authors recall Graham's theorem [Gr (63) b] that every
$m\ge78$ is $\sum x_k$ over some set in $\mathscr X$, and that $m=77$ is
not, and then pose the conjecture: "It seems highly likely that for any
polynomial $p:\mathbf Z\to\mathbf Z$ it is true that for all sufficiently
large $m$, there is a set $\{x_1,\ldots,x_t\}\in\mathscr X$ with
$\sum_{k=1}^tp(x_k)=m$, provided $p$ satisfies the obvious necessary
conditions: (i) the leading coefficient of $p$ is positive; (ii)
$\gcd(p(1),p(2),\ldots)=1$." They add that by Cassels [Cas (60)] the two
conditions suffice for every large integer to be a sum $\sum p(a_i)$ over
distinct $a_i$, and that Burr [Burr $(\infty)$] showed that for every $k$
every large integer is a sum $\sum x_i^k$ over a tuple in $\mathscr X'$,
the version with repetitions allowed. The conjecture is the case
$\alpha=\beta=1$ of Graham's conjecture $2'$ of 1963 (Remarks, p. 441):
condition 2 of his Theorem 3 could be replaced by $n=f(a_1)+\cdots+f(a_k)$
for any polynomial $f$ mapping integers to integers with positive leading
coefficient such that for every prime $p$ some $f(m)$ is not divisible by
$p$; Graham adds that very little was then known about the problem.

**Partial results.** Each addresses instances of the problem's quantifier
over $p$ and has its own partial claim page; Graham's is accepted,
Alekseyev's and van Doorn's are pending. Graham's
[[../library/unit_fractions/graham_1963_theorem_partitions/theorem_1|Theorem 1]]
(p. 435; claims checked; J. Austral. Math. Soc.,
refereed; claim page
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0283/claims/1963_03_17_graham|Graham 1963]]):
every integer $n>77$ is $a_1+\cdots+a_k$ with $1<a_1<\cdots<a_k$ and
$\sum1/a_i=1$, the case $p(x)=x$; the Remarks record Lehmer's unpublished
check that $77$ has no such partition. His
[[../library/unit_fractions/graham_1963_theorem_partitions/theorem_3|Theorem 3]]
(pp. 439--440) is the rational-$\alpha$ form of the same case: for
positive rationals $\alpha,\beta$ every large $n$ is a sum of distinct
integers exceeding $\beta$ with reciprocal sum $\alpha$. Alekseyev's
[[../library/unit_fractions/alekseyev_2019_partitions_into_squares_distinct_integers_whose/theorem_1|Theorem 1]]
(preprint p. 1; claims checked; a chapter of an edited volume,
with no record that it was refereed; claim page
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0283/claims/2018_01_18_alekseyev|Alekseyev 2018]]):
$8542$ is the largest integer that is not a sum of squares of distinct
positive integers whose reciprocals sum to $1$, the case $p(x)=x^2$; the
paper's computation was not rerun. Van Doorn's
[[../library/unit_fractions/doorn_2025_partitions_prescribed_sum_reciprocals_asymptotic_bounds/theorem_1|Theorem 1]]
(preprint p. 2; claims checked) quantifies the case $p(x)=x$:
with $n_\alpha$ the least integer beyond which every integer has a
partition into distinct parts with reciprocal sum $\alpha=p/q$,
$n_\alpha<cq\log^3q/(\min(\alpha,1)\log\log q)+c(e+\epsilon)^{2\alpha}$, and
his Theorem 2 gives $n_\alpha>0.3q\log^2q$ for some $\alpha$ with any large
prime denominator $q$ in any interval; $n_1=78$. That paper is a preprint
and settles no instance of the question. Cassels's theorem (completeness of
the values $p(a_i)$ without the reciprocal condition) is recorded as the
site's and the monograph's attribution, and this page consumes no statement
from his paper; Burr's result is cited by the monograph as unpublished. Van
Doorn's binomial-case manuscript ([vD25b]; claim page
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0283/claims/2025_08_31_van_doorn|van Doorn 2025]];
claims checked, proofs not verified) reduces, for
$f(x)=ax^d+b$ with coprime coefficients and any $\alpha>0$, the existence
of the threshold $n_0(f,\alpha)$ to a finite computation (its Theorems 1
and 2) and carries that computation out for $\alpha=1$ in its Theorems
3--5: $n_0(x+b,1)\le172+10b$ for $1\le b\le5000$, $n_0(5x+b,1)\le20000$
for $1\le b<375$ with $5\nmid b$, and $n_0(x^2+b,1)\le50000$ for
$1\le b\le800$, the source of the site's examples $p(x)=x+5$ and
$p(x)=x^2+100$; a manuscript in progress, not on arXiv.

**The general case: the site-accepted AI-generated argument (provenance
recorded, not judged).** The claim page
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0283/claims/2026_05_03_price|Price 2026]] records
this result. On 3 May 2026 Liam Price wrote in the thread that GPT 5.5 Pro,
prompted by him, had resolved the problem for every positive rational $\alpha$,
linking the Overleaf writeup, and that Kevin Barreto had helped clean up the
proof and had noticed that Problem 351 follows; Barreto wrote that he kept the
changes minimal. Bloom's summary of 10 May 2026, in the thread: fix
$p(x)=cx^d+\cdots$, put $u_j=36j+1$ and $D_j=u_ju_{j+1}$, and define $q(j)$ by
$p(6D_j)+p(3D_j)+p(2D_j)-p(D_j)=q(j)$, a polynomial of degree $2d$ in $j$ with
leading coefficient $c(6^d+3^d+2^d-1)\,36^{2d}$; by Theorem 1 of Graham [Gr64]
there are $g,X$
such that every multiple of $g$ that is at least $X$ is a sum of distinct values
$q(j)$; by telescoping, $\frac1{36}=\sum_{0\le j<J}\frac1{D_j}+\frac1{36u_J}$;
choose finite sets $A_k$ with $\sum_{a\in A_k}1/a=1-\frac1{36}$, avoiding the
classes $\{0,1,2,3,6\}$ modulo $36$, with $\sum_{a\in A_k}p(a)\equiv k\pmod g$
(a finite computation for fixed $p$); then $N_J^k=\sum_{0\le
j<J}p(D_j)+p(36u_J)+\sum_{a\in A_k}p(a)$ is representable,
$N_{J+1}^k-N_J^k=c\,36^{2d}J^{2d}+O(J^{2d-1})$, which is smaller than $q(J)$
for large $J$, and $|N_J^k-N_J^{k'}|\ll1$; switching a denominator $D_j$ to
$2D_j,3D_j,6D_j$ keeps the reciprocal sum $1$ and the distinctness and
changes the $p$-sum by $q(j)$; so for a large $N$ in the class $k$ modulo $g$
there is a $J$ with $X<N-N_J^k<q(J)$, and writing $N-N_J^k=\sum_{j\in S}q(j)$
by Graham's theorem forces $S\subseteq\{0,\ldots,J-1\}$, so that switching
the $D_j$, $j\in S$, reaches $N$. (The thread post prints the leading
coefficient of $q$ as $c(6^d+3^d+2^d-1)$, the base term as $p(u_J)$ and the
step as $cJ^{2d}$, and chooses $J$ with $2cJ^{2d}\ge N-N_J^k>X$; the forms
above carry the factor $36^{2d}$ and the term $p(36u_J)$ that the
definitions give.) Bloom describes the
local switches through $1=\frac12+\frac13+\frac16$ as the natural idea and finds
the writeup more complicated than it needs to be. Sothanaphan's summary of 3 May
describes the same structure (the Roth--Szekeres--Graham result applied to
$A(x):=p(2x)+p(3x)+p(6x)-p(x)$) and says he had not examined the technical
steps; on 6 May he wrote that he had confirmed the proof, through a transcript
link, which is not a source. The manuscript at the thread's Drive
link ("Polynomial Egyptian Sums: a formalization-informed revised presentation",
6 May 2026, 13 pages) states as Theorem 8 (p. 4): for $\alpha\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$,
$L\ge1$ and $p\in\mathbb Q[x]$ integer-valued on $\mathbb Z$ with positive
leading coefficient and no fixed divisor on the positive integers, there is
$m_0$ such that every integer $m\ge m_0$ is $\sum_{i=1}^kp(n_i)$ with distinct
$L<n_1<\cdots<n_k$ and $\sum1/n_i=\alpha$; its Theorem 1, the
Roth--Szekeres--Graham completeness theorem (Graham's 1964 Theorem 1), is "the
single external input"; its Appendix A lists twenty-eight presentation changes
made for the formalization and says the proof strategy is unchanged; the
formalizer's comment says nothing changed from the original proof. The argument
is consumed at the level of these summaries and the theorem statement; no step
