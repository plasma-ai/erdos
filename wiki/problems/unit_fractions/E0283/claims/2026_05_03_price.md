**Claim.** Let $\alpha$ be a positive rational, $L\ge1$ an integer, and
$p\in\mathbb Q[x]$ a polynomial that takes integer values at the integers,
has positive leading coefficient, and has no fixed divisor $d\ge2$ of its
values at the positive integers. Then there is $m_0$ such that every integer
$m\ge m_0$ can be written as $m=p(n_1)+\cdots+p(n_k)$ with integers
$L<n_1<\cdots<n_k$ and $\alpha=1/n_1+\cdots+1/n_k$. The case $\alpha=1$,
$L=1$ is the question of
[[problems/unit_fractions/E0283/_index|Problem 283]] for every admissible
polynomial, so the claim answers it yes; the general $\alpha$ is the
strengthening Graham conjectured in 1963, and the same argument settles
[[problems/additive_bases/E0351/_index|Problem 351]] in its corrected form.

**Depends on.** No page of this wiki; the argument's one external input is
Theorem 1 of Graham's 1964 paper on complete sequences of polynomial values,
whose library card the outline below links; the card has no result page for
it.

**Claimant and provenance.** The argument was generated by the AI system
GPT 5.5 Pro at the prompting of Liam Price, who posted it in the site's
discussion thread on 3 May 2026 with a writeup on Overleaf; Kevin Barreto
edited the writeup, keeping the changes minimal, and noted that the
corrected Problem 351 follows. A revised presentation of 6 May 2026
("Polynomial Egyptian Sums: a formalization-informed revised presentation",
13 pages, linked from the thread as a Google Drive file) states the result
as its Theorem 8 (p. 4), names the Roth--Szekeres--Graham completeness
theorem as its only external input, and lists in its Appendix A the
presentation changes made for the formalization, with the proof strategy
unchanged. Price, who submitted the result, is the claimant; the system
that generated the argument is named above.

**The argument in outline.** With $p(x)=cx^d+\cdots$, put $u_j=36j+1$ and
$D_j=u_ju_{j+1}$, so that $\frac1{36}=\sum_{0\le j<J}\frac1{D_j}+\frac1{36u_J}$
by telescoping. Replacing a denominator $D_j$ by the three denominators
$2D_j,3D_j,6D_j$ keeps the reciprocal sum (since
$1=\frac12+\frac13+\frac16$) and the distinctness, and changes the
$p$-sum by $q(j)=p(6D_j)+p(3D_j)+p(2D_j)-p(D_j)$, a polynomial in $j$ of
degree $2d$ with positive leading coefficient. Graham's completeness
theorem of 1964
([[../library/additive_bases/graham_1964_complete_sequences_polynomial_values/_index|Theorem 1]]
of that paper) gives $g$ and $X$ such that every multiple of $g$ that is at
least $X$ is a sum of distinct values $q(j)$. Finite sets $A_k$ of
denominators with reciprocal sum $1-\frac1{36}$, avoiding the residues
$0,1,2,3,6$ modulo $36$ and with $\sum_{a\in A_k}p(a)\equiv k\pmod g$,
supply the residue classes; the $p$-sum of the base representation built
from $D_0,\ldots,D_{J-1}$, $36u_J$ and $A_k$ rises by about
$c\,36^{2d}J^{2d}$ from $J$ to $J+1$, less than $q(J)$, which is about
$c(6^d+3^d+2^d-1)\,36^{2d}J^{2d}$, so for a large target $m$ in the class
$k$ there is a $J$ whose deficit $m$ minus the base $p$-sum lies between $X$
and $q(J)$; Graham's theorem writes that deficit as a sum of distinct
$q(j)$, which must have $j<J$, and switching those $D_j$ reaches $m$. This
outline follows the curator's summary in the thread (10 May 2026), with its
constants corrected as the problem page records, and the manuscript's
statement; no step is verified.
