## Definitions and hypothesis

For an integer polynomial \(f\) of degree \(d\ge2\), set

$$
F_f(n)=P^+\left(\left|\prod_{m=1}^n f(m)\right|\right),
\qquad P^+(1)=1.
$$

Here \(P^+(N)\) is the largest prime divisor of the nonzero integer \(N\).
The degree and irreducibility assumptions ensure that the factors \(f(m)\)
are nonzero.

The source's Hypothesis 3.1 is the following. For every irreducible
\(g\in\mathbb Z[x]\) with positive leading coefficient and no prime dividing
all values of \(g\), there are constants \(A_g>1\) and \(X_0(g)\) such that,
for every real \(X\ge X_0(g)\), some integer

$$
t\in[X,A_gX]
$$

has \(g(t)\) prime.

## Statement

Assume Hypothesis 3.1. For every irreducible
\(f\in\mathbb Z[x]\) of degree \(d\ge2\), there are constants \(C_f>0\) and
\(N_f\ge1\), depending on \(f\), such that

$$
F_f(n)\ge C_fn^d\qquad(n\in\mathbb Z,\ n\ge N_f).
$$

Equivalently, \(F_f(n)\gg_f n^d\) for all sufficiently large integer \(n\).
No uniformity over the choice of \(f\) is asserted.

## Reconstruction

Replace \(f\) by \(-f\) if necessary. This leaves \(F_f(n)\) unchanged and
allows its leading coefficient to be positive. Apply Lemma 2.1 to obtain
\(D>0\), \(M\ge1\), \(0\le a<M\), and \(h\in\mathbb Z[x]\) with

$$
f(a+Mx)=Dh(x),
$$

where \(h\) is irreducible of degree \(d\), has positive leading coefficient,
and has no fixed prime divisor.

Apply Hypothesis 3.1 to this fixed polynomial \(h\), and write
\(A=A_h>1\) and \(X_0=X_0(h)\). For each integer \(n\), define

$$
X_n=\left\lfloor\frac{n-a}{AM}\right\rfloor.
$$

Choose \(n\) large enough that \(X_n\ge\max\{X_0,1\}\). The hypothesis then
provides an integer \(t\in[X_n,AX_n]\) for which \(h(t)\) is a positive
prime. Put

$$
m=a+Mt.
$$

The lower endpoint is part of the prefix argument: since \(a\ge0\), \(M\ge1\),
and \(t\ge X_n\ge1\),

$$
m\ge a+M\ge1.
$$

For the upper endpoint, the definition of the floor gives
\(AMX_n\le n-a\), so

$$
m=a+Mt\le a+M(AX_n)\le n.
$$

Thus \(m\) is an integer with \(1\le m\le n\). The identity above shows that
the prime \(h(t)\) divides \(f(m)\), and hence divides the running product.
Therefore

$$
F_f(n)\ge h(t).
$$

It remains to compare \(h(t)\) with \(n^d\). If \(L_h>0\) is the leading
coefficient of \(h\), then \(h(u)/u^d\to L_h\) as \(u\to+\infty\). Choose
\(c_1>0\) and \(U\ge1\), depending only on \(h\), such that

$$
h(u)\ge c_1u^d\qquad(u\ge U).
$$

Also,

$$
X_n\ge\frac{n-a}{AM}-1.
$$

For \(n\ge2(a+AM)\), the right side is at least \(n/(2AM)\). Increasing the
fixed threshold \(N_f\) if necessary ensures both \(X_n\ge U\) and this
inequality. Since \(t\ge X_n\), we then have

$$
F_f(n)\ge h(t)
\ge c_1t^d
\ge c_1\left(\frac{n}{2AM}\right)^d
=C_fn^d,
\qquad
C_f=\frac{c_1}{(2AM)^d}>0.
$$

All of \(D,a,M,h,A,X_0,U,c_1,C_f,N_f\) is fixed after \(f\) is fixed.
Consequently the estimate holds for every sufficiently large integer endpoint,
not only along a subsequence. Undoing the initial replacement by \(-f\) does
not change the absolute-value product. This proves Theorem 3.2.

**Boundary.** The only analytic input is Hypothesis 3.1 for the one fixed
polynomial \(h\) produced by Lemma 2.1. The hypothesis remains unproved, and
the next page derives it conditionally from a positive-input Bateman--Horn
asymptotic.
